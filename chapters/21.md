# 21. The Confession

He woke at the usual hour. That, of all things, was the first wrong note of that morning — I had spent half the night expecting him to wake into it guiltily, warily, braced the moment his eyes opened for whatever I had found. That is how most men wake into whatever they've been caught at. Lloyd woke as he always does: all at once, both eyes open, his breathing already even before the rest of him had caught up to being awake. The second thing he saw after the ceiling was the floor plan, laid flat on the table between us, weighted at its corners with a teacup, a candle stub, and my own two hands. They had not stopped shaking exactly. They had only learned, somewhere in the small hours, to shake quietly.

Below us the chandler was already at his shutters. I could hear the particular scrape they made, iron dragged along a groove worn smooth by twenty years of the same scrape, and under that the costermongers starting up two streets off, calling things I was too far away to make into words, and under that nothing from Lloyd at all. He had seen the table. He understood what it meant. Neither of us had said anything yet.

Despite the hours I'd had for it, I'd rehearsed nothing. Sitting up through the dark I had turned over what I might open with — a question, an accusation, his name said in some particular voice I hadn't settled on — and briefly, absurdly, considered making a joke of it, something about the state of his coat, because that is what I reach for first when the true thing is too large to pick up with bare hands. In the end what came out of me was none of it.

"Halvard Crane," I said, and that was all.

I watched it land on him, and it did not land as a blow. It landed as a door he had been holding shut with both hands, finally let go. That frightened me a great deal more than a blow would have.

"Yes," he said.

"You're going to kill him."

"Yes."

He did not say it as a man says something he has only just decided. He said it as he answers every question put to him plainly — literal, unhedged, the exact weight of the word and no more — and that flatness told me more than the word did: how long he had been carrying it alone, and how little it cost him, once I'd finally handed him somewhere to set it down. He looked, for the first time since the coal yard started paying him, like a man who had slept the whole night through.

---

He told it in order, as a man recites something he has gone over alone so many times that the telling no longer needs his presence in it.

He had not gone to Stillwell only to come home strong enough to carry his mother out of Callow Ford. That part was true — every fact he'd given Edie and me on the road was true, he said so twice, as though the distinction mattered to him more than it did to either of us — but it was not the whole of what he'd gone for. A son who carries his mother out of a sick valley has a mother who is no longer in a sick valley, and the water does the same thing to the next house, and the one after that, for as long as the man who built the works keeps deciding the number of people it costs is a number he can live with. Carrying her out solved one woman. He had wanted, since before he'd ever heard the word Stillwell, to become a man capable of reaching the kind of person who could be made to stop deciding that.

"Capable how," I said, though I already knew roughly the shape the answer would take, and wanted to hear him say it anyway.

"Strong enough to get close. Steady enough not to lose my nerve once I was. Something in my hand that isn't only my hand." He didn't elaborate on the last part, and I didn't press him. There are rooms in him I have learned not to knock on. That one has been shut since before I knew him, and it stayed shut that morning too.

The case against Crane he laid out like a ledger entry: facts in the order he'd got them, hardest first, no appeal to how any of it ought to make me feel. Years before the works broke ground, Crane had commissioned a survey of the valley's groundwater, received it, and buried it rather than acting on it. He held his interest in the Larrow Consolidated Works through two men who held it in turn for him, so his own name appeared nowhere a clerk might trip over by accident. The Indemnity — the poster money, paid out in the seventh year, the one three strangers in Ammet Ford had thanked him for without knowing whose name was behind it — had not landed on seven years by custom, and not by convenience either. It sat there because that was exactly where the mortality curve in the buried survey put the worst of the dying, and a payment scheduled to arrive once most of the dying was finished cost considerably less than one scheduled before it began. Two inspectors who had pushed to have the survey reopened had been reassigned to postings on the coast, where there was no water worth surveying and nothing further for either of them to find.

"How do you know the schedule was built on the curve," I said, "and not just an ordinary seven-year plan." It was the one piece I could imagine arguing, and arguing it felt a great deal safer than sitting still with the rest of it.

"Clerk at the Reconstruction office in Ammet Ford drinks more than his wages allow, and talks when he does. Took the better part of two years — on and off, a drink here, a question there he never saw coming — to get the whole of it out of him a sentence at a time. He doesn't know what he gave me. I never told him why I wanted it." He said this without any pride in the method, the same flat accounting he gave everything else, and I sat with it a moment before the rest of what it meant caught up to me: two years out of the nine he'd spent sending money home and never once visiting. I had asked him, more than once, why a man so plainly fond of his mother had stayed away so long. Here, finally, unasked, was the answer, folded into the middle of a worse one.

---

"You could take it to Ferrick's panel," I said. "As we did with Vantry."

"We did take Vantry to it."

He let that sit long enough for me to hear it as he meant it — not a rebuke, he doesn't do rebukes, but one fact laid beside another for me to weigh myself. We had brought the sub-panel a wall of case files sixty years deep, a register with a dead woman's name in it, and a building that had, in point of fact, burned to the ground. The whole of what that bought us was eleven days of newspapers and a transferred, promoted Sister Superior writing to ask after my hands. The Church was popular. It was wealthy and widely loved besides, and it had not survived contact with its own documented crimes for even a fortnight before the story curdled into an argument about forged paper. The Senate was all three of those things and heavier. It had no burnt building for anyone to point a camera at. It would not need a fortnight to bury this. It would not need an afternoon.

"So the answer is to become the thing that did the burning," I said, "instead of the thing that reported it."

"The answer is there isn't one that works. I looked." He said it without heat, same as he says everything, and that was somehow worse than if he'd shouted, because shouting I could have met with my own voice raised back at him, and this left me with nothing to push against. "I'm not clever. I know that. I can't write a letter that changes a man's mind, and I watched the cleverest one either of us ever helped write get called a forgery by people who hadn't opened it. I can put my hand on a man. That's the whole of what I've got that might actually move something."

I got up and put the kettle on, because my hands needed something to do that wasn't holding still against the shaking, and because I wanted, badly, to tell him he was wrong. The part of me that still keeps a private, furious ledger of the ward and the promotion and the eleven days could not get the words to form around a conviction I did not, in that moment, entirely hold. The water took its time over the flame. I stood above it and let it.

The light had moved while we talked, unasked and indifferent to either of us, off the far wall and onto the table, striping the floor plan's creases until they stood up out of the paper like the lines in a palm. Somewhere below, the chandler had finished with his shutters and begun on something with a hammer, small and steady and entirely unconcerned with what was happening above his head. I poured two cups because two cups is what I make, out of habit, whether or not either of us is going to drink them, and carried them to the table, and set his down an arm's length from where his own hand lay, and did not put it any closer than that.

---

"Why not me," I said, once the tea had gone from too hot to drink to not worth drinking, and neither of us had touched it. "You've never kept anything else from me this long. Why this."

He was quiet long enough that I thought he wasn't going to answer it at all. That silence would itself have told me something, if it had gone on. Instead: "Because you're the one who tells me what's real."

"Lloyd—"

"Since the chapel. Before the chapel." He said it plainly, an accounting again, not a declaration. "A man who tells you he's going to kill a senator is a man you stop trusting to tell you what's in a room. I needed you not to stop. I needed that more than I needed you to know the rest of it, and I knew what I was choosing when I chose it, and I'd choose it again, and I'm sorry, and none of that changes which one I'd choose."

He didn't reach for my hand. He has never been a man who reaches first. He sat on the edge of the bed with the plan still weighted flat on the table between us, waiting with the same unhurried stillness he has given every corridor and every chapel and every wrong room since Stillwell, for me to rule on whether the thing in front of him was there.

"Tell me I'm wrong about him," he said. "If I am. You'd know before I would."
