# 10. Two Priests

They closed the distance in under a hundred yards, walking at the pace two men keep when they've got a long day still ahead of them and no reason to hurry the middle of it. Maria's hand was still up when the taller of the two lifted his in return, and by the time we were level with them she already had the older one's name — Brother Aldous, greying, broad through the shoulders, the build of a man who'd done labour before he took to devotion — and was working on the younger one's, a narrow, quiet sort called Tam who kept his eyes on Edie's pack a beat longer than the road required and then didn't do it again.

The country here had gone flatter and better tended than anything above Ammet Ford — stone walls kept up instead of leaning, a field with sheep in it that still had all four legs between them — and I understood without being told that we'd crossed back into the part of the valley Carrowgate remembers exists.

They were bound for the almshouse at Selwick, two days on, they said, with a wagonload of blankets and seed corn already sent ahead of them by cart, and they were walking the road themselves because the cart couldn't carry passengers and somebody had to see the state of the crossing there before winter closed it for good. The crossing was out, Aldous told us — the spring flood had taken the footbridge, and the ford below it wasn't safe to try loaded, not with the water still this high — and he drew the alternate route in the dirt with a stick while Edie watched him do it with the flat attention she gives anything that might save her a day's walking. It checked against what she already had from the marker posts. She thanked him for it in her own economical fashion, which is to say she stopped asking him questions about it, and that was the whole of the thanks he got.

We walked together as far as the crossroads at midday and then further, because the sun was out for the first time in three days and nobody wanted to be the one to say we should split off yet. Aldous had a kind of talk that fills a road without asking much back — parish gossip, a bishop and a goat, the price of wool two villages over — and Maria gave as good as she got. Even Edie answered him plainly when he put a direct question to her, which for Edie counts as sociable. Tam said less. He walked a half-step behind the rest of us with his hands folded into his sleeves against the cold, and when he did speak it was short and useful: the crossing again, a dry barn two miles past the fork where we could shelter if the weather turned before dark. Once, unprompted, he asked Edie whether the pack she carried was army issue, and when she said it had been, he nodded like he'd settled something for himself and didn't ask anything further.

Somewhere past noon he brought out bread from inside his coat — good bread, better than we'd had since Ammet Ford, still faintly warm from being carried against his body — and a hard round of cheese wrapped in cloth that he cut without being asked and passed round without keeping the larger piece for himself. Maria pulled her shawl tighter against a gust off the fields and ate her share laughing at the end of Aldous's goat story, a story I'd lost the front half of and didn't ask to have repeated. The wind had dropped enough by then that you could hear the hedgerow settling, small ticking sounds of things drying out after the last rain, and a lark went up somewhere off to the north that I noticed and never heard come down again. For a stretch of maybe twenty minutes it was as close to an easy afternoon as we'd had on the whole road since Callow Ford — close enough that I let my shoulders down from where they usually sit, and didn't notice I'd done it until later.

I was watching Aldous laugh when it happened, which is probably why I saw it and not before. He'd got to the end of the goat story and tipped his head back to laugh properly, and his collar — grey wool, buttoned high, right up under the jaw, same as I'd seen on every Mercy I'd ever passed on a road — should have moved with that. A throat does. It works when a man laughs, rises and settles with the swallow that follows the laugh back down, and his didn't. The cloth sat exactly where it had sat a second before, no give in it at all, like it was buttoned to a fence post and not a neck. Then the laugh finished, and he wiped his eyes, and reached for the water skin, and the moment closed over itself before I could get a second look at it, quicker than these moments ever give you, leaving me with the first one and nothing to check it against.

I've had worse and said less about it. That's the honest accounting of it, if anyone had asked, which nobody had. I sat there running the frame back — throat, collar, the half-second between one and the other — same as I run all of them back now, cataloguing what moved and what didn't, filing it next to the guard with the wrong shadows and the dog that wasn't and the rest of what I carry and don't mention. Somewhere in the running-back, my hand had already decided, on its own and without consulting me, to set the bread down on the cloth in my lap rather than eat it.

Tam noticed. Asked was something wrong with it, the bread, and I told him no, I'd eaten heavy that morning. A lie he had no way to check and probably believed, because why wouldn't he. My other arm — the one nobody sees, the one that isn't where the rest of me is — wanted very badly to cross that circle and lay itself flat against Aldous's chest, through the wool and whatever was or wasn't underneath it, and know outright, the one way that would actually answer it. I didn't let it happen. Not because I doubted it would tell me something. Because I didn't know what I'd do with my face, sitting there, if it did, in front of Maria and a stranger and a road with no cover on either side of it, and a man who turns out wrong about something in front of company is a man who gets put somewhere with locks on it. I have that particular arithmetic memorised down to the coin. I did the sum, put the bread back inside my coat instead, and said nothing else about it for the rest of the meal.

We parted at the fork past the crossroads, where their road climbed north toward the almshouse and ours kept east along the river. Aldous took Maria's hand in both of his and told her the Mercy was in good company, and meant it in the plain way he'd seemed to mean everything he said all afternoon. Tam nodded to Edie and to me and said even less than that, which suited him better than talk ever had. We watched the two of them go up the rise until the grey of their coats stopped being separate from the grey of the sky behind it, and then we didn't watch any more, and turned east ourselves.

"You didn't eat," Maria said, once they were far enough off that saying it wouldn't carry back to them on the wind.

I told her what I'd seen. Plainly, because there wasn't a way to say it that wasn't plain — the collar, the laugh, the half-second where nothing under the cloth answered for what the cloth was doing on top of it. I gave her the whole of it and none of what I thought it meant, because I don't know what it means. I never know what any of it means. I only know what I see, and that it wasn't the first time, and it's never once turned out to be nothing.

She didn't answer straight off. When she did, it came out fast, the words already ahead of whatever she'd meant to say, like something in her had been caught somewhere soft. "He gave us his bread, Lloyd. He drew a map in the dirt for Edie so we wouldn't drown ourselves at a crossing that isn't even his parish to warn about. Men like that don't —" She stopped herself before the end of it, which told me more than the end of it would have done. "It's cold out. His collar's wool, buttoned to his chin, and he's fifty if he's a day. Maybe it doesn't move like you think it should. Maybe you went looking for something wrong with him and found it because you were looking."

"Maybe," I said, and meant it, because it was true, and I couldn't rule it out any better than she could rule the other thing in.

"You said the same about the guard on the rail line. And the dog."

"I know what I said."

"And you were right about neither of them, in the end. Nobody else saw either one."

"Nobody else has to see it for it to be there."

"That's the whole of your case, Lloyd. It's the whole of your case every time. I can't take a man's bread and his map and his kindness and weigh them against a feeling you had watching him laugh."

"It's not a feeling."

"It's a feeling with a reason attached to it. I'm not saying you're lying. I'm saying a reason isn't proof, and you've never once had proof, not for any of it, and you've asked me to rule on faith every time regardless."

She stopped walking for that, just for a step, and I heard it rather than saw it — the change in her footfall on the stone. "That's not an argument, Lloyd, that's just the same claim said twice."

I didn't have an answer that wasn't the same claim said a third time, so I kept it to myself. She turned to Edie for the opinion she wants a third of when it comes to this, a habit I understand and have never once minded, except this time Edie didn't give one. She had her cord out — the hemp one, bound at the ends with brass wire ferrules, that she uses for counting the days a job runs — and she was working a fresh knot into it with her thumb, not looking up from the work.

"I don't have the eyes for it," Edie said. "Either of your eyes. If it comes to a fight I'll have a position on it. This isn't a fight." That was all she gave us. She coiled the cord back into its running hitch at her wrist, and whatever either of us might have said if we'd pushed her further, neither of us did, and the not-pushing left the question exactly where it had been, sitting in the road between the three of us with two answers to it and nobody willing to pick either one up.

We walked the better part of an hour after that without much said at all, which for Maria is its own kind of weather. She chewed the inside of her cheek and looked at the road without seeing much of it, moving faster than the ground under us required — the shape of somebody arguing a case to herself that she'd already lost once and meant to win the second time through sheer repetition. When she finally spoke again it wasn't to argue further. It was a ruling.

"They were real men," she said. "Tired, and kind, and real, and I won't have you carrying the other thing all the way to Carrowgate on my account." She said it flatly, the same final note she puts on every ruling of hers — no room left in it for a second opinion once it's given — and I did what I do, which is nothing. I nodded. I let it go where I let all of it go, filed and closed and not brought up again, because that's the arrangement, and the arrangement has held since a corridor in the Salts where I had nobody else to check anything against. I wasn't going to be the one who broke it over a collar that might, in fairness, have been only a collar.

I noticed, walking, that Edie's thumb had stopped dead on the cord a half-beat after Aldous first told us Selwick was two days on, cheerful about it, easy, back before we'd even traded names properly — and that the marker post at the crossroads that morning had read Selwick, one day, in the country miles the valley prices every job against, the ones Edie would not get wrong and had never once got wrong in nine days on this road. She hadn't said anything then. She still hadn't now, walking beside me with the cord put away and her face giving back nothing at all, and I understood, without either of us saying it, that she'd caught the same gap I had and had decided, just as she had with the collar, that it wasn't hers to arbitrate either.

I still had the bread. I took it out somewhere past the second milestone, unwrapped the cloth from around it, and let it drop into the long grass at the verge without breaking stride, and didn't look back to see what did or didn't come for it. Maria was well ahead of us by then, already saying something to Edie about the younger one's accent, laughing at her own joke before she'd finished getting it out. I caught them up and didn't say anything else.
