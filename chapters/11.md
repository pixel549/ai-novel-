# 11. Permits

Tannery Reach announced itself a mile out, on the wind before we saw any part of it: lime and raw hide and something sweetish gone wrong underneath both, the smell of a trade that eats a town's air and never once pays it back. Edie named it before we'd crested the rise. Tanyards, she said. You could smell one from the next parish over if the wind sat wrong, and hers never had, not in four years of digging toward worse things than leather. Maria pulled her shawl up over her nose and didn't put it down again until we were past the second bridge and the wind had swung round behind us.

The canal ran along the low side of town, flat and the colour of a bruise coming up, barges moored three deep along one bank and hide frames filling the other — skins pegged out taut as drumheads, whitening in rows under a sky that hadn't decided what it meant to do. Men worked the pits in shirtsleeves, forearms stained the colour of old tea to the elbow, and somewhere behind the yards a winch ran on a dry gear, a flat metal complaint that never quite stopped the whole time we were in the town. Nobody looked twice at three strangers on the towpath. A town built on that trade has better things to stare at than us.

Edie had the cord out as we walked in, the hemp one bound at the ends with brass wire ferrules, working a knot into it for the day. She does it every morning before anyone else is properly awake. Eleven days out from Callow Ford, by her count, though she'd never once said the name of the place since we left it and I'd never once asked her to. I watched her hands do the knot without her watching them and thought, not for the first time, that counting was the one thing in this world Edie trusted without needing to see it proved twice.

The crowd at the second lock was a different matter.

Twenty people, maybe, standing well back from the water. Nobody closer than they had to be. At the centre of it a man lay face-down in the canal, his coat ballooned dark around him, held off the lock gate at an angle by a boathook that a lean fellow in a waterman's cap kept nudging without ever quite drawing it in. Every time the body drifted toward the near bank he leaned out and gave it a shove, unhurried, the same motion he'd use on a log fouling his mooring line, and sent it drifting back toward the centre of the channel and a little further along than it had been.

"He's been doing that since before noon." A woman near us, not taking her eyes off it. "Boy's name is Fenwick. Worked Harker's yard, the pits on the far side." She'd clearly said it before that morning, more than once, and had worn all the heat out of it saying it.

I asked her why nobody had pulled him out, because somebody had to ask it and it wasn't going to be anyone else on that bank.

"Centre of the channel's the line," she said, as if that answered everything, and for her it plainly did. "This side's Saint Aldhelm's parish. That side's Marsh Fenton. Whoever lands him pays for him — inquest, coffin, six weeks' board if the coroner's man has to come out from the city to tell us what everyone here can see with their own eyes, which is that the boy went in the water and didn't come up breathing. Nobody's keen to pay out for what's already happened. Paying won't undo it."

Behind her, a second woman stood at the very edge of the bank with both hands pressed flat over her mouth. Not screaming. Not moving. Just watching the pole nudge her son another few feet toward the middle of the water and further from the only ground she had any claim to put him in. Nobody touched her either. I understood that too, in the same flat arithmetic that explained the rest of it: comfort had a cost, no less than a coffin did, and the woman beside us wasn't offering either because neither was hers to spend.

Maria asked, quietly, what had happened to him. The woman near us said nobody rightly knew — a bad step on the lock stones after dark, most likely, coming off a late shift, the same stones that had taken two other men she could name off the top of her head in as many years — and that the mother had no one else living to go to about it. Husband gone in the Attrition and never found enough of to bury. The boy was all the wage that house had seen since. Maria didn't say anything to that. She only looked a moment longer at the woman on the bank — a sum already done, an answer she didn't care for — before she went to her.

I had a use for my hands in a fight. I had a use for the arm nobody can see, when something needed finding that wasn't quite where it should be. I stood on that towpath with both of those uses hanging dead at my sides and watched a man hold a boy off with a stick to keep him from washing up on the wrong side of a line drawn on some surveyor's map before either of them was born, and there was nothing in me built for that. If it had come to a fight I'd have known exactly where to put my feet. It never once looked like coming to one.

Maria went to the mother. She didn't ask leave and she didn't say anything useful, because there wasn't anything useful to say and she's the only one of us who understands that saying nothing useful, up close, beats saying nothing at all from a yard off. She set a hand flat between the woman's shoulder blades — where she sets it on anyone who hasn't fallen yet but might — and stood there with her while the boathook went on working behind them. I don't know what that hand does, exactly, past resting there. I've had it on my own back enough times to know it does something, quietly, whether the person under it asks for that or not.

Edie watched the man with the pole for the length of four or five breaths — long enough to price a job properly, which is how she looks at anything before she decides what it's worth — and then went to find whoever in that town was in charge of deciding who paid for a dead man.

---

It took her under a minute, which told me something about how often this particular argument got had in Tannery Reach. The relieving officer sat behind a narrow desk in the back room of the lock-house, cuffs grey with old ink, a ledger open in front of him that he closed, unhurried, the second Edie's shadow crossed his doorway — a man closing a book he had no wish to see read upside down by a stranger.

"We don't take men out of the water on Saint Aldhelm's side until the boundary's confirmed," he said, before she'd asked him a single thing. "It isn't unkindness. It's procedure. Confirm the parish, confirm the liability, move the body — in that order, not the reverse, or Marsh Fenton says we shifted him to put the cost on them, and then it's the magistrate's hours wasted as well as ours."

"How long does confirming take."

"However long it takes the surveyor to re-stake the channel marker. Flood took the old post in the spring. There's a new one ordered."

"How long's that been."

He didn't answer, which was its own answer. His first line had failed him, so he reached for a second one. "And even granted the boundary — even granted it's our water he's found in — settlement's a separate question from drowning in it. If the boy wasn't born to this parish, if his indentures were filed somewhere else, the burial cost falls to his parish of settlement and not to wherever he happened to go under. That's been the law since before either of us—"

"He drowned in your water," Edie said. "Settlement's an argument you have with Marsh Fenton, or with Harker's yard, after he's in the ground. Not before. You want to leave a dead boy hung up on a lock gate to argue precedent, you can put that reasoning in writing too, and I'll see it gets read by somebody above you who has to explain it to a newspaper."

Edie set her ledger on his desk — the oilcloth one, stained dark at the corners, that she'd carried the whole way from Stillwell without once letting it out of arm's reach, the one she used for counting a job's days and pricing what it was worth before she'd take it. She opened it to a page already marked. She hadn't hunted for it.

"Channel's thirty-one feet at the second lock. I paced it off the towpath stones walking in. Four years of pacing breaches before I'd trust shoring to a number somebody else gave me, and I'm not about to start now." She tapped the page. "Boy's lying eleven feet off the Saint Aldhelm's bank. That puts him on your side of a channel with no marker in it at all, confirmed or not — unless you're telling me the parish line doesn't run down the middle of the water on this canal, when it runs down the middle of every other one in this Commonwealth I've had cause to cross."

"That's not —"

"And every hour he sits there, he's sitting upstream of a lock that feeds the trough half this town draws its drinking water from. An hour from now that's not a parish boundary matter any longer, it's a public health matter, and a public health matter goes to the Canal Company's superintendent in writing, with a date on it. He won't care whose coffin it is. He'll care that it's his water."

The relieving officer gave her the same look I'd worn myself more than once — like she'd produced a second head, and he wasn't sure yet which one he was meant to address.

"I'll want that in writing," he said, which was as close to surrender as a man like that gets.

"You'll have it before the boy's in the ground," Edie said, and that was the whole of the unpleasant conversation — exactly as unpleasant as it needed to be, and not one word more than that.

---

They brought him in at the bank below the lock, because nobody wanted to carry him past his mother twice, and when it came to the lifting — sodden wool, a full dead weight, the kind of work that wants two sets of hands at once and neither waterman keen to be the one taking the shoulders — I went down into the mud and took the weight nobody else wanted.

It wasn't a fight and it wasn't seeing. It was the kind of labour I'd done for coin in half a dozen towns since Stillwell, and I did it the same way now, without being asked twice, because it was the one thing on that bank I was actually built for. He was heavier than he'd looked in the water. His face, when we turned him, was only a face — slack, young, ordinarily dead. No second shadow lying over him. Nothing moving under the skin that didn't belong there. I reached with the arm nobody sees, once, quietly, the half-thought kind of reach I do without deciding to, and there was nothing on the other end of it to find. Whatever had put Fenwick in that water — the ice on the lock stones, his own boots on a bank gone to mud, bad luck plain and complete — it hadn't left anything behind worth my particular trouble. Just a boy who'd gone in and hadn't come up, exactly as the woman on the bank had told it the first time, with nothing underneath her account at all.

I laid him down on the towpath stones myself and stepped back and let his mother come to him. Maria had a cup of water from somewhere and put it in the woman's hands without asking whether she wanted it, which was the right call, because the woman drank from it without seeming to notice she had, her eyes never once leaving her son. That was the last thing in Tannery Reach anybody needed me for.

A parish arguing over who paid for Fenwick was a small thing against what I already knew got decided in rooms further off and further up — a man's life costed out in writing, in advance, by people who'd never once have to stand on a towpath and watch the bill come due. I didn't say any of that to Edie. There wasn't a way to say it that didn't say more than I meant to, not yet, not to her.

We left before the paperwork was finished. Edie stayed long enough to see the relieving officer's letter drafted and signed and folded into her ledger alongside whatever else she keeps in there, and then she said there was nothing left in the town worth the wait, and we went. The smell of the place held another half mile up the road before the wind finally took it off us for good.

"You could have let him go on floating," I said to her, somewhere past the second milestone. It came out flatter than I meant it, more a question than it sounded. "Wasn't your boundary. Wasn't your pole doing the pushing."

"No." She had the cord out again, working a fresh knot into it without looking down. Her hands had decided before she had. "Wasn't anybody's, by the paper. That's most of a job, done right. Finding the piece nobody's claimed, and claiming it before it rots where it's lying."

I didn't have an answer that wasn't the same thing said back to her, worse, so I kept it, and we walked on toward Sennet with the light going long and nobody saying much else before dark.
