# 16. Delirium

Something had hold of my shoulders before I had hold of anything else, and it set me down with more care than the last hands to touch me had managed. That told me nothing about where I'd ended up. It told me a good deal about whoever had me now not being the one who'd decided the plan. Boards shifted under my back, cart-board by the give of it, and somebody's knee braced the corner of it against a turn I felt and never saw. A cloth went under my jaw and was wrung out somewhere close enough that I heard the wringing and not the water. I had stopped being able to tell the difference between being moved and having already been moved, and both of those things were currently happening, as far as I could tell, to a man who was, on paper, me — I noted that much from a little outside and a little above, the same habit I'd kept since I was nineteen, and it had apparently survived even half a skull not working.

The stair started under my hand before I knew I'd reached for a rail. Salt crusted it, in a rind thick enough to take a fingernail, grouting two stones that had never been cut to sit flush, and I had both feet on it with no memory of the first step — the usual arrangement with that particular stair, the one under Stillwell, the one the three of us had gone down and down and never once agreed how far. I went on anyway, hand over hand on a rail gone slick with something that wasn't water. Three strides in, maybe four, the salt under my palm turned to ash, fine and grey and still faintly warm, a hearth some hours cold rather than a fire still going — I didn't let myself finish that thought, because a cold hearth in an empty house belonged to a different stair entirely and I wasn't ready for that one yet. The rail stayed iron under the same hand, and I was two days further into a night already finished, going down into a sub-basement with scorched brine thick at the back of my throat and Edie's voice somewhere above me, mid-word, the start of it already gone. Every single time I was certain the next step was the bottom. Every single time I was wrong, and never once learned from it, because by the time I understood the mistake I was three strides past it, the rail unchanged under my hand, only the stone under my boot gone soft and old, until there was a half-inch gap of daylight showing through where the roof at my mother's house had let the sky in at the north gable. I knew that gap as well as my own knuckles. I'd described it to Maria once, on a road, trying to say what home looked like without once saying the word.

A cup touched my mouth. "Drink," somebody said, not unkindly, and tipped it, and I drank. Some very small, very steady piece of me, running underneath whichever stair I happened to be standing on, understood that the cup was real in a way the stair hadn't been obliged to be. I had stopped being able to say, by then, which of the two mattered more to the man doing the drinking.

Ivet surfaced in the second tank from the door, in the room I'd described to the other two from outside a barred window three nights running before any of us had the nerve to go in and confirm it. She surfaced the slow way the Salts always brought anyone up, vapour peeling off her shoulders in sheets, her hair plastered flat to a face I'd have known blind. She had the question half-shaped before she'd cleared the brine from her mouth enough to ask it, and when it came it was the one she'd have put to me over a cold supper. Why I'd never come home. Not angry. Mild. A neighbour's interest in your excuse for missing church. I had an answer — nine years of one, every month counted out in money sent and never once a letter that said more than the money did — and standing there with both feet still wet from a stair that hadn't existed in the same breath as this room, I could not get a single week of those nine years past my own teeth. I have never once in my life been short of things to say when nobody's listening. Saying them to the person they're actually owed to is a different skill altogether, one I did not have then, in a tank room that wasn't burning yet, or had burned already, or had never been built at all.

"The window," Maria's voice said, from somewhere with no stair in it, flat and even, the voice she kept for ruling on what was real and nothing else. "There's a window over you. Can you feel the air off it? Tell me if you can feel it."

I couldn't tell her anything. I felt a window. Whether feeling it and it being there were the same question answered twice, or two separate questions I'd been mixing up my whole life without noticing, I did not know then and have not settled since. It is the oldest debt I own, and I was no closer to paying it off that night than I had been at nineteen.

Somewhere in a dark that belonged to none of the three places I kept arriving at, a man I never saw said a name across a room, the offhand way you'd drop a mutual acquaintance into a conversation with someone you'd only just met. Crane, he said, and went straight on to something else that I lost completely. I let it go past me. That particular dark had been saying things to me since I was nineteen, and most of it turned out to be worth nothing, and some of it turned out to be worth everything, and I had given up a long time ago being the one who got to sort which was which before it had already happened to me. A different voice — or the same one wearing different manners, since courtesy doesn't always keep one face — asked, gently, whether somebody else was quite all right. Not me. Somebody unnamed — the courtesy of a host checking on a guest who's gone quiet at the far end of a room.

My right hand, the ordinary one, the one that had done every bit of gripping at that stair and come off it broken in three places for the trouble, found a cord. I knew it before I knew I was holding it: hemp, wire at both ends, a shape my fingers had learned long before anything else in any of those places had become familiar to them. I counted it as Edie had taught me, without her ever once calling it teaching. Ninety-one. I ran my thumb back along it and counted again, slower, sure I'd catch the mistake on the second pass. The second pass came back ninety-one as well. I sat there with both counts agreeing and trusted neither, because the cord I remembered carried nineteen, and nobody anywhere near me that night had ever handed me a number that size, and I had no honest account of where it had come from that didn't run through either my own cracked skull or some lower floor of myself I had never had cause to name and wasn't about to start naming now. A man who can't do arithmetic twice and get two different wrong answers has at least that much consistency to his name, I thought, and even half out of my own head I knew better than to say it to anyone.

Tallow, and under it the particular cold of stone that has never once held a fire. The smell arrived with no room attached to it at all. My other hand — the one that had never needed to grip anything to work, the one nobody but me has ever known was there — closed on something coarse and dry, carrying rope's weight without any of its grain, and let go again before I'd decided whether I'd meant to take hold of it in the first place. A word came stitched to it, spoken in a voice I have never since been able to put in any mouth I know. It said the word once, then again, then enough more times that it wore down to a sound with no shape left inside it — my own name does that, I'd guess, if anyone ever said it back at me fast and often enough, and as near as I can report it honestly, that is what this was. I did not get the rest of whatever had come with it. I have never since gotten the rest of it. Some mornings I am glad of that. Most mornings I am not.

"The blanket," Maria said, outside all of it, flat and spaced exactly as she'd given me things to hold onto underground, back when being told was the whole of the job. "You're under a blanket. It's grey. There's a burn mark near your left foot, from before any of this. You know that mark."

I did know that mark. I held onto the knowing of it as I'd have held a rail, and for the length of one breath the stair under me was only a stair again, salt-crusted, going down into the dark the three of us had first gone into together, before any of it had cost either of us more than we could buy back. I would have given whatever I still had left in me to keep that one plain fact for longer than a breath. I did not tell her so. I have not told her so since, on a theory that has served me badly in every other part of my life: that what isn't said out loud is what nobody can ever take back off you.

"My hand," she said next, and set it where she'd said it was, flat against my chest, warmer than anything else that night had managed. "That one's real. Whatever else isn't, that is." I believed her completely, the same as I had every single time since the chapel, and did not tell her that it was the only reason I was still in the room with her rather than any of the others on offer.

Edie's voice came through once more, from no stair and no tank and no room I could draw a wall around: hold still, flat, a fact rather than comfort, the register she kept for both. I don't know if she was talking to me. I have never been entirely sure, piecing it together after, that she was in any room at all that night, or whether some part of me had gone hunting for her voice as a hand hunts a rail in the dark, because a fact stated plainly, by anyone at all, was the nearest thing to ground that whole stretch had to offer.

The courteous man came back once more before the end of it, unhurried, well-spoken, asking after that same somebody else's safety in the tone of a man checking on a guest who'd wandered off from a party. I noted him and set him aside with everything else down there — one more wrong noise among a great many, meaning nothing I could hang a name on, because I didn't have the name yet and wouldn't for a long while, and a voice with no face on it, however well-mannered, is only a voice.

The ash under my boot turned back to salt somewhere a long way down a stair I had by then gone down more times than there were stairs in the world to go down, and the rail under my hand ran on without a seam in it, straight into a half-inch gap of ordinary grey daylight, showing through a roof that had stood unmended, as far as I knew that night, for every one of the nine years I hadn't been home to climb up and mend it.
