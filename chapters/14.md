# 14. The Fourth Mercy

Vantry's haze didn't go anywhere after dark. I'd thought, the first night, that the soot-coloured lid over the city was a daylight habit, something the sun worked on and the dark let go of. Wrong, confidently and only once, same as most of what I'd decided about this city on no evidence at all. Gone midnight the gaslamps still burned into the same low ceiling of smoke they burned into at noon, and the moon behind it was a worse lamp than any of them, smeared out thin and the colour of a bruise three days old. Edie didn't look up at any of it. She had a requisition slip in her coat she'd written herself that afternoon in a clerk's hand none of us had ever seen her use, and she walked us to the service door like a woman delivering linen.

It was the door Maria and I had watched for four days running, the one that did all the actual work while the grand front steps of Ascension House grew grass between the flags for nobody to walk on. At night it was quiet, rutted deep from cart wheels, and a single lamp over it that nobody had thought worth putting out. Edie knocked twice, said four sentences to a porter half-asleep in his chair, and the porter looked at her paper for less time than it would have taken him to read it and waved us through, because a woman with the right kind of paper and the right flat voice is the most invisible thing in any building the Church has ever built. I thought that was worth being grateful for and said nothing, because gratitude toward Edie came out wrong whenever I tried to hand it to her directly; she took it better folded into something else, so I told her, low, that she'd have made a fine thief, and she said she'd have made a better one than this, and kept walking.

The anteroom just inside held a desk, a lamp, and a register the size of a parish Bible, chained to the desk like money rather than scripture. Edie went through the ledgers on the shelf behind it with both hands, fast, pulling delivery dockets and matching them against dates she already had memorised from four days of counting carts from the street — ton after ton of brine, logged in, nothing logged out that wasn't already brine when it left — while Maria stood at the big register with her palm flat on the cover and didn't open it yet. I didn't open anything. There wasn't a door in that room I needed my arm for, so I stood by the one we'd come through and watched the stair going down. I told myself whatever was below would be exactly as bad as I expected and no worse. That has never once been true in my life. It has never once stopped me telling myself so, going down a stair.

On the top step Maria said, to nobody in particular, that she'd always wondered what the inside of a miracle looked like, and now she supposed she'd find out. The joke didn't land. None of us had the spare breath to let it land. She said it anyway, because saying it was the point, not the landing. Edie went first with the lamp turned low. I went last. That was usual. Whatever came up out of the dark behind a person was mine to be the one standing in front of.

The first room past the stair was tanks.

Twelve of them, cast iron and glass, the glass gone milky with salt, the same film that had crusted every wall at Stillwell. Each one held a person. Not drowned — breathing, slow, the chest rising under brine the colour of strong tea, hair fanned out and gone the texture of rope. A woman in a soot-stained apron moved between them with a long brass thermometer and a clipboard, checking each one in turn, unhurried, with the flat professional attention of someone who has done the same eleven steps every night for years and stopped finding them remarkable around year two. She wrote a number, moved to the next tank, wrote a number. None of the twelve had moved to notice us. Maria asked, very quietly, how long, and the nurse — because that was what she was, in the apron, with the clipboard, in every way but the one that mattered — said the newest had gone under in the spring, the oldest since before she'd started the post, four years back, and that none of them had ever once come up on their own. I had seen Maria's own hands shake over a man with a crushed finger. They didn't shake now. She put her palm flat against the glass of the nearest tank for exactly as long as it took to breathe once, took it away, and didn't ask the nurse anything else.

I watched the fourth tank longer than I meant to, because something in it was watching me back that wasn't the man inside it, and I've had enough practice keeping that fact off my face that I don't think either of the women noticed. For one bad stretch of a few seconds the thought ran ahead of me on its own — that there could be eleven more of these for every tank in the room and I'd have no way of telling, that the water itself might be thick with them the way a jar goes thick with flies, that I'd been breathing the air off the top of it since the stair — and I let it run that far and no further, walked it back down the same four steps it had gone up, counted none of it, and arrived back where I'd started: one thing, in one tank, looking. Whatever shared the water with him didn't reach for me. It only looked — unhurried, nowhere else to be, no clock running against it — and I looked back exactly as long as it took to be sure it wasn't going to do anything else, then moved on when Edie moved on, because looking back is the whole of what I know how to do with something that only looks.

The second room was smaller and worse in a quieter way. Shelves, floor to ceiling, and on the shelves, in rows, things that had been grown rather than born — the word the nurse's clipboard would have used, if we'd stopped to ask her, was specimens, and I'm grateful we didn't stop to ask her, because I'd rather carry the sight itself than the word for it. Most didn't move. A few did. A held breath is worse than a drawn one, and that was the whole of the difference between them. Each shelf had a card. Each card had a date and nothing else useful on it. That omission was its own kind of mercy, though I didn't let myself think the word, not even once, not in that particular room.

The third room was narrow cots in two facing rows, made up tight and empty, the kind of order that comes from nobody staying in a bed long enough to disturb it. Edie found the stud book on a lectern at the far end, built for it, bound like a farmer's record of a good bloodline, lines and dates and outcomes entered in three different clerks' hands across what had to be decades. She read two pages standing, closed it, and said one word — *livestock* — flat, the same trade register she'd use for spoil, or shoring, or face, vocabulary for a trade I hadn't known the Church ran until that night. I didn't ask to see it. Maria didn't either. Edie carried that one for both of us without being asked to. I didn't thank her for it. Thanks was the one currency she never knew what to do with, and I'd learned better, by then, than to hand it to her straight.

The extraction theatre was the fourth room, and it was the one built to look like care: tiled, drained, a chair with restraints worn soft at the leather, and against the far wall a wall of files that ran the length of it and most of the height, sixty years of them by the first dates we checked and the last, each folder a name, a date admitted, a date of procedure, and a single closing line in the same clerk's economical hand: *result negative, subject retained*, or, further along the wall, in a thinner, older ink: *result negative, subject not recoverable*. Edie pulled one folder at random from somewhere in the middle of the run and read it through twice without her face changing once, which told me more about what was in it than reading it myself would have, and put it back exactly where it had been. Nothing in that room had worked. Not once, not for sixty years, and the only thing that had changed across sixty years of trying was the ink.

Pinned beside the door going out, where a hundred years of novices must have filed past it on the way to somewhere else, was a card gone brown at the corners with a child's catechism on it in large clear print — *bread, shelter, absolution*, the three public Mercies, the same words carved soft into every lintel I'd seen since Carrowgate — and under it, in a different and much more recent hand, in the flat numbered style of a ledger entry rather than a prayer, a fourth line had been added to the list. I won't set down the exact wording, because the wording wasn't the point and never had been; what mattered was that nobody had needed to hide it, that it hung at novice height where anybody could have read it any day for years, filed as plainly as a shift roster. Bread and shelter find who nobody will miss. Absolution gathers them in. The fourth line was simply what the gathering was for, printed in the same hand that would have printed a laundry schedule, because to whoever wrote it, it was the same kind of fact.

I thought of a groundwater survey I'd never seen and a man I'd never met, and a number I was fairly sure he'd been handed on a sheet not unlike this one, and I didn't say any of that to either of the women, because I hadn't told them yet what I was in Carrowgate to do, and a card on a wall at novice height was not where I meant that conversation to start.

The last room was the smallest and had no shelves and no tanks, only a floor marked out in a wide ring of brass fittings gone green, and inside the ring, something that had been a person before it was bound and fed and kept, in the specific sense that it had the general shape a person leaves behind and nothing else a person has. It didn't move when we came in. My right arm — the one that goes through walls, the one that touches what the other two can't see — went cold at the shoulder the moment I was through the door, the particular cold it only gets near something out past the ordinary, and I kept it close to my side and didn't let either woman see my hand close on nothing. The thing in the ring turned toward that hand, not toward me, which told me something I didn't want told, and I was glad when Edie said, low, that we weren't going to be the ones who tested whatever that ring was for, and turned us back toward the stair.

We went back up through the extraction theatre, the breeding room, the shelves, the tanks, faster than we'd come down, and stopped in the anteroom because Maria had finally opened the big chained register and was running her thumb down a column of names with the lamp pulled close. I watched her get three pages in before her thumb stopped moving. She didn't say anything. She read the line twice, and I knew, because I'd heard the whole of it from her own mouth two nights running in a rented room, exactly which name was under her thumb and exactly what it had cost her six years ago to spend forty-one crowns on the woman it belonged to, and exactly how long ago the date beside it said the Church had taken her anyway, two years after Maria had already fled for taking that same woman's side once. Her finger didn't move off the name. She didn't call either of us over to see it, and I didn't make her, and Edie, three feet away, cataloguing dockets by lamplight, never once asked what had stopped her hand.

Nobody said anything for what felt like a long time and was probably, if I'd had the stomach to measure it, a page of the register, no more. Maria closed the cover herself, gently, as if someone were sleeping under it, and stepped back from the desk.

"We're burning it," Edie said. She closed the ledger of dockets in her own hands, flat, the decision already filed and dated as far as she was concerned, and looked at the stair instead of either of us, working out, I could tell from the set of her, exactly how many trips it would take to get the right papers up it before anyone lit the first match.
