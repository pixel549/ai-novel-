# 23. Ferrick

I told Lloyd I was going to see a friend. It was true in every particular except the one that mattered, and he nodded over his coal-yard wages and didn't ask which friend, because he already knew, and didn't ask why, because he had already told me what the why was worth. We had stopped arguing about Ferrick's panel three weeks before. I was going anyway. Some decisions survive being lost. This one had simply changed shape, into a question I still needed answered with my own eyes, whatever losing the argument was supposed to have settled.

The street was two turnings off the Senate and nothing like it: narrow fronts of good grey stone, window boxes gone leggy with the season, a terrace built for men who wanted it known they hadn't spent the valley's money on themselves. Ferrick's house sat in the middle of the row with no plate on the door and no man at the step pretending not to watch it, and I had walked past it twice before I understood that the absence was the guard, not the lack of one.

The girl who let me in could not have been twenty. She had a ledger of her own, thinner than Edie's old oilcloth one and newly bought, and she matched my face against something written in it before she said a word to me, then smiled like she'd been told it was allowed. Behind her the hall was lined floor to picture-rail with letters, each one framed rather than simply pinned, each one from a name that meant nothing to me — a parish clerk in some coastal town, a widow thanking the senator for a culvert, a schoolmaster whose roof no longer leaked onto the register. I counted past a dozen before I stopped counting on purpose. Somebody had chosen to frame them rather than hang them loose. It told me more about this house than anything I'd yet been shown.

"Edie's in the back," the girl said, and didn't offer to take me there herself, because a man at the foot of the stair — heavyset, upright with the stiff carry of a soldier who'd spent twenty years being told to stand easy and never quite managed it — was already turning to do it instead. He didn't ask my name. He'd had it from the girl's ledger before I was through the door, passed along without either of them saying a word I could hear. Somewhere past him, in a room I didn't see, I heard two men talking low and even about a delivery that hadn't come — former constables, I learned later, kept on for the same reason a tunnel gets shored twice where once would probably do. I understood I had just watched the whole of Edie's system work once, quietly, on me. None of the four people running it would ever be worth a second glance on the street. That was presumably the point of hiring them.

---

She was in a room at the back that still had a strip of yellow paper peeling at one corner, the kind a person chooses for a nursery, and nothing in it now but a long table, two upright chairs, and an iron cabinet bolted through the floorboards into whatever joist would hold it. She didn't get up. She looked me over once, the whole of me, cataloguing rather than greeting as she always did, and said my coat was good for the weather and my hands weren't, and went back to whatever she'd been writing before I'd been let in.

"I heard you'd be coming," she said. "Thought it'd be sooner."

"There was an order to finish first." This was true. It was also not the reason, and I let the silence after it do the work of admitting that without my having to say so.

She showed me the cabinet because I was looking at it, and she has never once in all the years I've known her pretended not to notice where my eyes went. The lock, she said, wanted oiling. She'd been meaning to see to it for a fortnight and kept not getting to it. From Edie, that omission was as close to an apology as a piece of iron was ever going to get. Inside, banded in her own hand exactly as it had travelled out of Vantry, were the registers and the requisition orders, the real ones, the ones that had cost Lloyd the use of his arm twice over to bring into daylight. Nobody had opened it, she said, in two months.

"Two months." I heard myself say it back to her flatly, in her own register, and didn't manage the trick of it — my voice came out asking a question hers hadn't been.

"Two months Tuesday." She didn't elaborate. The not-elaborating was its own answer, delivered without a flicker of anything I could point to and call a feeling. If I wanted a feeling to go with it, I'd have to supply my own.

On the wall by the cabinet, where any visitor coming to check on the valley's evidence would see it before they saw the cabinet itself, hung a calendar with a date in the coming spring ringed twice in red ink. I had watched her mark load lines in that same ink on the road out of Stillwell, back when the ledger was still oilcloth and the only thing in danger of collapsing was a cart axle.

"The hearing," she said, before I'd asked.

---

Ferrick found us there. He had come looking — a girl with a ledger had told him who'd arrived, and he hadn't been able to leave it at that. He was taller than I'd pictured him from Edie's flat descriptions and older too, grey gone all the way through rather than coming in at the temples, dressed like a man who owned exactly as many good coats as he needed and not one more. He had my name before I had a chance to give it to him.

"Miss Vesk." He said it like a word he'd rehearsed being glad to use. "Edie's told me — not much, she tells nobody much — but enough. Your hands. Are they recovered?"

It was the first thing out of him, ahead of any courtesy about the weather or the walk, and I understood, from how squarely and immediately the question landed, that Edie must have told him something of the burns, or that he'd simply noticed the old scarring himself and decided it mattered more than small talk. I said they were old wounds and didn't trouble me. That was mostly true. He accepted it as a man accepts an answer he's already decided to be satisfied with.

He wanted to show me the report. He said it as another man might offer a drink — the hospitality of the house itself — and led us both, Edie a half-step behind, watching doors rather than conversation out of a habit that never once let up, into a study with more books than any room I'd been in since Vantry's library. He took a bound volume down from a shelf behind his desk, spine out where any visitor would see its title before they saw anything else in the room, and opened it to a page he had clearly opened before.

Forty pages. Indexed. Mill runoff from six towns tested against the Larrow's own wells, in numbers that didn't flatter anyone, in his own prose, careful and plain and entirely without the warmth he spoke in — the report of a man who knew exactly how to be believed on paper even when nobody downstream of him would act on what he'd written. It said the water would not be fit to drink inside ten years at the current rate. It said so without hedging. It was, in every way a report can be, excellent.

"We finished it in the spring," he said, and there was pride in it, plain and undisguised, the pride of a man showing a guest something he'd built with his own hands. "I've convened the full sub-panel for it. Spring session — I'd have liked sooner, but there's a queue, and I won't jump it just because I think my matter's the most urgent one in it. Everyone thinks that about their own."

I asked, because I had to, whether anything had changed yet on the strength of it. He said the panel had met four times since the report went in. I already knew that from the calendar. He said the fourth session had been the best discussion he'd chaired in years — rigorous, genuinely rigorous — and that a vote would likely follow once two absent members could be got into the same room, and he said all of this with his hand still resting on the bound copy, as if the weight of his palm on good paper were itself a kind of progress.

The other copy, the one he actually worked from, was pencilled in his own hand and lived in a drawer he had to stand and unlock to show me, because nobody asks to see a working draft when there's a finished one on the shelf to admire instead. I looked at both of them for longer than was polite and said nothing about the two months the finished one had spent exactly where it was now, spine out, unopened by anyone who might have voted on it.

---

Edie did not say any of what she was thinking. She never speaks before a sum is finished, and by now I can usually tell when one is still running behind one of her silences. Four sessions. No vote. One report that everyone in the room agreed was excellent and nobody in the room had moved an inch for. She would have that column totalled before I left the house. She would also keep the total to herself, as she kept most of what she'd decided, until deciding what to do with it became a separate question — one she hadn't let herself ask yet.

Somewhere else in the house a tray went down, china by the sound of it, more than one piece, and I watched Edie's shoulders drop a half-second before the crash reached us: the oldest tell I know in her, the one she's never once been able to argue her own body out of. She didn't move to help. There was nothing in it worth moving for, a dropped tray and nobody hurt, and the half-second had bought her exactly what it always buys her. A fraction more warning. Nothing whatsoever to do with it.

"Girl's new," she said, by way of explanation, and went back to watching the door.

Ferrick excused himself to see to it, glad of something with a clean answer in it, and it was just the two of us again, in a house full of framed gratitude and one locked cabinet nobody had needed to open in two months.

"How's the arm," Edie said, not looking up from the notes she'd started making the moment his chair emptied. "Coal yard still giving him grief over it?"

"Mending." I kept it as flat as I'd have said it to anyone, flat enough to pass. It was true as far as it went. That was the whole of what I intended to give her.

She didn't ask a second question. In all the years I've known her she has never once asked either of us for anything, and by now I've watched her not ask often enough to know it isn't the same thing as not noticing. I did not tell her about Crane. She wrote something further down the margin of her page, unhurried, and when I left she walked me as far as the hall herself, past the letters, past the girl with the ledger, and locked the front door behind me before I'd finished buttoning my coat against the weather she'd already told me it was wrong for.
