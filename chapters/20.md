# 20. Drifting

The room Edie's address led us to was two flights up over a chandler's, in a street where the gutters ran straight instead of the crooked Vantry kind, and for the first four or five days I did not do anything with the quiet except have it. Carrowgate took some learning. The soot here settled differently, finer, less of it, so that a white collar lasted two days instead of one, and the gaslamps along the better avenues burned a clean greenish white I had never seen outside a church. The Senate sat at the top of the hill above everything else in the city. I understood it, that first week, only as a fact of the skyline: a long grey roofline with a dome at its centre, the kind of building that has decided in advance it will outlast whoever is currently inside it.

I found work taking in mending from two houses on the next street, which paid badly and asked nothing, and once, for three nights running, sat up with a woman two doors down whose lying-in had gone past its time and whose own sister had gone for a midwife and not yet come back. I did not call what I did in that room Arcana. I called it sitting with her, and held her hand, and did not steady anything I did not have to, because the ordinary kind of comfort cost nothing and the other kind cost a day I did not want to spend explaining myself to strangers. The child came easily in the end, a girl, and the mother cried from relief rather than pain and thanked me by name twice, a name I had not given her, so I must have said it at some point without noticing, and did not correct her, because a woman twelve hours into motherhood has enough to be getting wrong without me adding to the list. Lloyd took day work at a coal yard off the Carrow road, heaving sacks with men half his age who called him "old soldier," which he had never been, not in any uniform that would have entitled him to the name, and let them have it anyway because correcting a kindness costs more breath than it is worth. He came home each evening smelling of coal dust and admitted, when I asked, that his shoulders hurt in the good way rather than the bad.

For those first days I let myself think this was simply what came after. Six weeks of nursing him, eleven days of newsprint, Vail's letter folded under the floorboard where the register used to live — and now this: a street, a trade, a bed that did not need turning anyone in it through the night. I slept without waking at intervals to check his breathing. I ate a meal I had not cooked myself and did not feel guilty about the coin. For six or seven days running I believed, with the particular, undefended relief of someone who has decided not to examine a good thing too closely, that we were simply resting, as two people rest after a bad winter, and that resting was allowed to be exactly what it looked like and nothing underneath it.

It stopped being only that on an evening I can date exactly, because of the boots. He came in past full dark, later than the coal yard had ever kept him, and there was a pale dust ground into the toe of one boot that had nothing to do with coal — too fine, too grey-white, more like the dust off dressed stone than anything a yard full of sacks would leave on a man. I did not ask him about it that night. I set it aside, as if it were a splinter I meant to deal with and then mostly didn't, until an evening a few days later when I found myself doing the sum of our three weeks without having decided to do it, and the sum came out wrong.

---

The pattern, once I saw it, was not subtle. It had simply never been pointed at anything I was looking for.

Lloyd walked every evening, same as he had in Vantry, and I had taken that for a man rebuilding a body that still caught at the wrist when he closed his fist too fast. But a walk that is really about tiring a bad arm goes wherever is nearest and comes home the short way. His walks went nowhere near. He left east, toward the chandler's and the coal yard, and came back from the northwest every single time, along streets that had no reason to connect those two points except that they both passed within sight of the Senate steps. I started noticing it as you notice a word repeated too often in a letter: not on the first pass, not even the second, but by the fourth you cannot read past it to anything else on the page.

I asked him once, lightly, whether he liked the view from up there, meaning the hill, meaning nothing. He said the building put the weather to better use than most — the dome caught the last light and held it longer than the streets below ever got to keep it — and I believed him for an entire day before I noticed that I had not actually asked what he meant by *better use*, and that he had not actually told me.

I heard the shape of it once, by accident, two tables off in the chop-house while he worked through a second round with the man who kept the appointment book for one of the under-secretaries. "Chief clerk still leave his door on the latch through the morning session?" Lloyd asked it, flat and unhurried, as if the answer mattered to nobody at all. "Latch till noon," the clerk said, "bolted after, on account of the messengers." "And the west stair?" "Shut Tuesdays. Carpet men in." Lloyd nodded as a man nods at nothing in particular, and ordered another round the clerk hadn't asked for, and I sat over my own drink, gone flat, and understood, before I understood anything else about it, that I had just watched him pay for something with his coin and his silence and receive, in exchange, exactly nothing a bored man would want.

The men he fell into talking to were the rest of it. Lloyd does not strike up conversations. He answers them, carefully, when he is spoken to first, and he is good at making the answering look like friendliness, so that most people never notice he started nothing. That habit of his was one I had lived with long enough to trust absolutely, and that trust is exactly why it took me two weeks to notice that every single man he had fallen into talking with in this city — the clerk, a doorman at the chop-house, a porter who complained at length about the Tuesday carpet men — wore, somewhere on his person, the small brass pin with the crossed quills that meant the Indemnity Office.

He was not making friends. He was taking a census, polite and patient and entirely without hurry, of who worked where, who came in which door, who kept which hours and which habits, and he was doing it as he does everything that frightens him — quietly, from the edges, never once asking the question that would tell anyone what he actually wanted to know. Back in the valley, the year he worked out which overseer could be reasoned with and which couldn't before he ever said a word to either one, I had seen him learn a man exactly like that. Three weeks spent learning a building instead of a man was something new.

That was the part that frightened me before I even had a reason for it to. A man's habits tell you how to get what you need from him, and then you are finished with him. A building's habits tell you how to get *into* it.

---

I told myself three separate, respectable reasons he might be doing this, and turned each one over long enough to wear it thin.

He might simply be bored, and a man who has spent his whole working life reading a room for danger does not stop reading rooms because the danger is gone; he reads the nearest one out of habit, much as another man might whistle without meaning anything by the tune. He might be angling, in his slow and sideways way, for steadier work — porter, clerk, anything better than a coal yard — and a man looking for work watches the building he hopes will hire him. Or he might only be doing what I privately suspected I was also doing, circling the largest, most permanent-looking thing in a city that had not asked either of us to stay, because permanence is a comfort even to people with no plans against it.

None of the three survived a full evening against the facts I actually had. A bored man does not memorise which door a clerk uses on a Tuesday and which on a Thursday. A man hoping to be hired does not ask after session days rather than hiring days. And I did not, turning it over by the window with the mending gone cold in my lap, believe for one line of it that Lloyd Hale circled buildings for comfort. He had never once, in all the months I had known him, found comfort in anything large.

He slept early that night, worn through from the coal, one arm — the newer scar, the wrist that still caught — thrown up over his face, breathing the heavy, untroubled breath of a day that had simply been hard, not the kind that cost him sleep. His coat hung on the single nail by the door, the good one, the one he wore into the city and not to the yard, heavier in the hand than its cloth accounted for.

I want to set down honestly that I stood over that coat for a long time before I touched it, and that the long time was not caution about waking him. It was the plainer fact that I did not want to be a woman who went through a sleeping man's coat, whatever she told herself about why, and that wanting not to be that woman and being her anyway turned out not to be mutually exclusive after all. Once, for a reason I believed in, I went through a locked register that was not mine to open. I told myself this was the same kind of theft, in service of the same kind of knowing, and did not examine too hard whether that comparison flattered me or simply let me do what I had already decided to do.

The inner pocket held a folded sheet, soft at the creases from being opened and closed more times than three weeks should account for. A calendar of some kind, days of the month ruled in columns, certain dates ringed in a tight, careful hand that was not his best writing but was unmistakably his — he writes as he talks, short strokes, no flourish, nothing spent that doesn't have to be. Session days, I understood after a moment, set against another column of dates in smaller script beneath them: deliveries, shift changes, a watch roster copied from somewhere he had no business reading.

Beneath the calendar, folded separately and pressed flat as if it had lived under something heavy for most of its life, was a plan of a building. Not drawn freehand — traced, or copied with care from something official, corridors and stairwells and a chamber at the centre marked only with a number. Three doors on the plan were marked with small, neat crosses, and against one of them, in the margin, in his own hand, in ink gone slightly brown as ink does when it has sat folded in a coat through weeks of weather, was a name I had never once heard him say in all our months together.

*Halvard Crane.*

I knew the name, and not from the plan in my hands. From him. From the handful of times he had spoken about home at all — the mine, the river, the works, the twelve villages that fed it, all of it Crane's, near enough, signed at the bottom of everything by the same name for longer than either of us had been alive. He had never once told me that in more words than it took to say it, and had never, in all our months, said anything more about the man than that the works belonged to him and so, in the sense that mattered, did the valley. I had taken it for the kind of fact a person mentions once and sets down, as you'd name a river without needing to say anything further about water.

I stood there with the plan open in both hands and Lloyd's breathing even and unhurried six feet away, and did not fold it back along its own creases, because my hands would not do it steadily enough yet to put it back exactly as it had been.
