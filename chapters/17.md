# 17. After

Six weeks, and I came to know the room as I had never let myself know a sickroom at Sennet: every board that gave underfoot, the damp patch over the window shaped like nothing, the particular grey the light turned at four in the afternoon when the mill two streets over banked its fires. Rented by the week, paid in advance, in a district where nobody asked why a woman came in carrying a man who should by every sane account have been dead, because in that district somebody was always carrying somebody, and the landlady's only question was whether we'd be wanting the second blanket.

The nursing was not what the stories make of it. Nobody writes the bucket. Nobody writes that a man who cannot turn himself needs turning every few hours whether or not he wakes for it, or that skin breaks down from simply lying in a bed as stone breaks down from simply standing in weather, a little at a time, in the places that take the weight. I learned his body in that unglamorous way before I learned anything else about those six weeks: the small of his back, the backs of his heels, the places I had to watch. I was good at the watching. I had been a sister. Nobody tells you that the training for tending the dying is exactly the training for tending the nearly dead, and that the two kinds of patience feel identical from inside the work, whatever they feel like from the pulpit.

I talked to him through all of it. In the Salts I had done this whenever he needed telling what was real, and six weeks is long enough to wear a habit into a groove whether or not anyone's listening on the other end of it: cup, window, blanket, my hand, here, on your chest. I also told him, more than once, that if he died on me after everything it had cost to drag him out of that building I would never forgive him, and that he could spend the rest of eternity explaining himself to nobody but himself about it. He never answered any of that part either. I kept saying it anyway. It counted as a joke even on the days when only one of us was in a position to laugh.

I did him in order, because I had to choose an order and there is no version of that choice that isn't a kind of arithmetic I never wanted to be good at. The skull first — I would not have him stupid for want of nerve on my part, whatever it cost the rest of him to wait. Then the ribs, because a man can die of ribs quietly, from the inside, days after the bruise stops being interesting to look at. The arm came last, because the arm was not going to kill him. It took me most of five weeks to get to it properly, an hour or two at a sitting, and by then the break had set itself the wrong way twice over without me, stubborn and ignorant as bone gets when nobody competent is paying attention to it, and what I could do at that point was argue with the mistake rather than prevent it. I straightened what I could. I could not re-break it and start again — that is not a kindness I have, whatever the fire-and-brimstone version of a miracle worker might manage — and so there is a place just above his wrist now where the bone sits very slightly wrong under the skin, a ridge you'd take for a scar if you didn't know to feel for it, and when he makes a fist all the way there's a half-second where it catches before it closes. I did that. I want to be exact about that, even now: a woman with my gift, doing my best, left a mark on him that is going to be there for the rest of his life, and calling it the best I could do doesn't make it not a mark.

Every session cost me the rest of the day. That is not metaphor and I will not dress it as one — ask Edie, who found me three separate times face-down at the table where I'd been sitting upright a minute before, too hollowed to so much as call out. I slept like something switched off rather than something resting. I woke each time with my mouth tasting of pennies and no memory of having dreamed, and I was glad of that, because the one time I did dream in those weeks it was the chapel with the twelve tanks in it, and I woke from that one already reaching for the register before I remembered I was awake and it was under the floorboard where I'd left it.

Edie came twice. The first time with money and bread and a look at Lloyd that was entirely clinical — pulse, colour, the give of the skin over the ribs — delivered without a single word of comfort attached to it. I had stopped finding that strange months before. I had come, if anything, to be grateful for it; there is a mercy in being assessed rather than pitied. The second time she stood in the doorway long enough to say she was taking the stud book and the requisition orders to see about getting them in front of people who'd have to answer for them, and that she'd send word, and that I should not expect the word soon. I asked her if she was alright. She said the building was still standing and the papers were still readable. It was not an answer to what I'd asked. It was, I understood, as much of one as I was going to get. Then she was gone, and it was the two of us again, and the bucket, and the room.

I prayed. I want that written down honestly rather than skipped past, as I skip past it in my own head most days: on my knees by that iron bed, hands moving through shapes they'd been taught before I could write my own name, words coming out of me I had not decided to say, to an authority I had personally watched preside over sixty years of case files each one a name and a date. I despise that I still do it. I have tried stopping and found I cannot, quite. That tells me something about the difference between what a person believes and what a person's body has simply been trained to do since the age of seven, and I have decided I am not equipped to say which of those two things is the truer one. The Church lied. I do not think God did, and I recognise that this is a distinction I have no right to make so cleanly, and I make it anyway, every night, kneeling on a floor that wasn't swept for me and never will be.

It would be tidier if the wound were about doctrine. It is not. I have given up believing the Sanctum answers to anything but its own ledgers, and that cost me less than I expected — I had been circling toward it since the imbuing room, if I am honest, and something you've already half-decided doesn't break you twice. What breaks is smaller and has a face. Ondine Vail taught me my letters on a bench in the Sennet kitchen with a stub of chalk and more patience than my own mother would have had reason to spend on me, whoever she was. Ondine Vail told me, the first time I ever made a candle gutter without touching it, that what I had was a gift and not a sickness, when every other adult in that building would have had me examined by someone with a licence to be frightened of me. Both of those things are still true. I have checked. I keep checking, as you'd check a sum you already know is right, hoping this time it won't be. She is not a liar standing in a good woman's shape. She is the good woman, the whole time, doing the kitchen-and-chalk-stub work in the morning and signing the intake register in the afternoon, and I was raised by both halves of her and I cannot get a knife in between them to tell you where the one stops and the other starts. That is the part I have no ritual for. The Church I can simply stop believing. Her I am going to go on loving for exactly the reasons that make it unbearable, and six weeks in that room taught me there is no stage past which that gets easier to carry, only quieter.

The register itself I kept under the boards, wrapped in a shirt of Lloyd's too torn to mend, and most nights I didn't take it out, and some nights I did. Anse Duley's line sat two-thirds down a page of names I did not otherwise know, in handwriting that was tidy and unhurried and entirely without malice, as a tax roll is without malice. Taken, the entry said, with a date two years after I ran a poor box empty for her and fled the consequence of it before anyone could so much as ask me why. I have turned the arithmetic of that every way it will go. I stole to keep a widow and her three children off that register, as I understood the register then — a list of the failed and the unwanted. That was true, as far as it went. I did not know there was a lower list under that one, a list of the collected, and that what qualified me to the woman who ran both lists was precisely the stealing: a rule set aside for a person in front of her, the rarest thing, Ondine had told me herself, that she ever looked for in anyone. I bought Anse two years. The Church I served without knowing what it was took the rest, and gave me, for my trouble, a promotion into the one building in the Commonwealth where I might have been able to see it coming in time to stop it, if I had asked one single question more than I did. I do not get to call that someone else's failure. I have tried the sentence several ways and none of them let me off.

He surfaced properly on the fourth day. Not the half-waking I'd had before that — eyes open and nobody behind them, hands that gripped mine and let go for no reason I could find — but actually there, looking at the ceiling with the particular stillness of a man taking an inventory before he trusts himself to move. I'd been changing the dressing on his ribs and I stopped with my hand flat on his chest, feeling it go up and down, counting without meaning to, the particular count of someone who has been afraid for six weeks that it might stop.

"Maria." His voice came out wrecked, unused, barely a shape at all.

"Here."

He turned his head — slow, careful, as though testing whether the joint would still answer for it — and looked at the window, the damp patch shaped like nothing, the grey light doing its four o'clock thing on the wall. I watched him take it all in and distrust every piece of it, the same look I'd seen on him a hundred times in the Salts, in corridors that were a different room each time someone counted them.

"Is this real," he said. Not a question, by the time it came out of him. A door he was holding open and waiting to see which way it swung.

I had an answer ready. I had had six weeks to get one ready, and every rehearsed version of it came apart in my mouth the second he actually asked, because I understood, in the half-second before I spoke, that I did not in fact know — not about the room, which was real enough, but about what I was agreeing to be for him again by answering at all, and whether I had the right to want to be that for him as much as I did. The silence ran longer than I meant it to. I watched it frighten him before it frightened me, and then it frightened me too, both of us caught in the same pause, each waiting on the other to decide what the world was.

"Yes," I said. "The window's real. I'm real. You're safe, more or less, for whatever that's worth right now."

He let his breath out slow, the whole six weeks of it, and closed his eyes, and I sat there with my hand still on his chest, feeling him believe me.
