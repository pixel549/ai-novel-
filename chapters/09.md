# 9. Callow Ford, Told Not Shown

Two days out of Ammet Ford the road stopped pretending to be a road. The crushed stone and the poured culverts gave out at the parish boundary the way money always does in the valley — a marker post, then nothing — and what continued north was cart track, rutted and grown up the middle with plantain, banked on both sides with drystone that hadn't been repointed since before the war. Nobody had put glass in a window out here in living memory. The houses sat low and grey against the hillside, thatch gone black at the eaves, and the smoke came up thin and straight in the cold because there wasn't enough wind this high to bend it. I knew this country. I'd spent the first twenty-two years of my life in it and nine more sending money back to it, and somewhere in the second afternoon I noticed I'd stopped watching it the way I'd been watching everything since Stillwell — as a fact to check against what's shared — and started just feeling it, which is worse, because a stranger's country I can keep at arm's length. My own I can't.

We camped that evening in the lee of a fold wall, out of the wind, close enough to the road that Edie could hear anyone on it and far enough off that nobody on it could see our fire. She built it small the way she builds everything, no more flame than the pot needed, and had the tarp strung between two fence posts before the light was properly gone, because Edie does the next thing before the current one's finished and calls it not wasting daylight rather than what it actually is, which is never quite being able to stop.

Maria split the last of the Ammet Ford bread and cheese three ways without being asked, the way she'd split everything three ways since Stillwell, like fairness was a reflex rather than a decision she kept having to make. She sat close to the fire with her knees up under her skirt, and for a while nobody said much, which was its own kind of rest — three people who'd stopped needing to fill a silence for each other.

"Tell me about her," she said eventually, not looking at me, looking at the fire instead, which was a kindness I noticed and didn't say I'd noticed. "Your mother. You've said her name twice in four days and nothing else about her."

"Feels like more than twice," I said. "She'd be put out, hearing I'd been rationing her."

Maria didn't laugh, exactly, but something in her face eased at the joke doing its job, and I got the half-second it bought me and used it.

I had a version ready. I'd had it ready since Stillwell, built the way I build most things, plank by plank, tested before it takes weight. "Ivet Hale," I said. "Sick four years. Some kind of wasting thing, the sort the valley gets — starts in the joints, moves to the chest eventually. Can't afford to move her to Carrowgate, and there's nothing in Carrowgate for it anyway, not for people from here. I'm going home strong enough to carry her out if it comes to that. Literally, if it comes to that."

Every word of it was true. I want that on record, even if there's nobody to put it on record for but myself. Four years — true. Can't afford to move — true. Going home to carry her out — true, and it was the whole reason I'd walked into Stillwell Hydro in the first place, the whole reason I'd put a chalice to my mouth and come up with an arm that hadn't been there before. The omission was the plan, and the plan was simple: tell her everything except the one thing, and let the sheer weight of truth stand in for honesty, because weight is what people check for. Nobody audits a true story for the shape of what's left out of it.

"You never went back," Maria said. Not an accusation — she doesn't do accusations, she does an observation shaped exactly like one and leaves you the room to fill it. "Nine years, and you sent money every month, and you never once —"

"Work," I said. "Money doesn't walk south by itself."

That was true too, in the way most lies of omission are: a smaller truth stood up in front of a bigger one and given its coat and hat. The real answer had a room in it with no window, five weeks of it, in Carrowgate, when I was twenty-two and said out loud to the wrong person what I'd started seeing at nineteen — a woman on a tram platform with a hand that had one joint too many in it, a dog that threw four shadows under a single lamp, ordinary things wearing wrongness the way another man wears a coat. I found out inside a week what that costs, when you've got no name behind you to vouch you're only strange and not dangerous. I learned two things in that room. One, that what I saw was mine to carry and nobody else's to hear, unless I wanted it carried for me instead, behind a locked door, by people paid to decide I was carrying it wrong. Two, that I could build a version of myself that never mentioned it again, and that version could pass for a labourer, a fighter, a man who does jobs, well enough that even people who loved me stopped asking the question twice. I have never told anyone about that room. I wasn't going to tell Maria about it now, on a hillside two days from my mother's door, with the fire doing what fire does to a face and making it easy to say things you'd regret come morning. Not saying it is a kind of honesty too. It just doesn't look like one from outside, and I've made my peace with that being my problem and nobody else's.

Edie had the oilcloth ledger open on her knee, not writing in it, just looking, the way she does when she's totting up something that isn't figures. Maria turned on her the way she turns on a subject that's gone as far as it can go without breaking — sideways, lightly, as if she's only changing it and isn't.

"And you," she said. "What happens to you, after. When we're done in the valley."

"Carrowgate." Three syllables, no more offered.

"That's a place, not a plan."

Edie looked at the fire for a second, which for her is a long pause. "I've a charge there," she said. "Good man. Overworked, underguarded. I go back to him when this is finished."

"A charge," Maria said, delighted despite herself, the way she gets delighted by any fact that makes somebody more of a person than she'd had them down as. "Like a — who is he?"

"Doesn't matter who." Edie closed the ledger, and that was the end of it. She didn't say a name and she wasn't going to, not tonight and I'd guess not ever, and I understood that better than Maria did, I think, because I keep a room with no window too and I know the shape a person goes when you get near its door. But she'd given more than she usually does — Edie doesn't volunteer, she rations, same as me, and she'd just handed over a fact for nothing because Maria had earned it by asking after mine first and taking no for an answer without pushing. That's a kind of trade. Nobody in this camp says the word trust out loud. We just do this instead of saying it.

I still had the Larrow Indemnity handbill folded in my coat, one of the ones they'd stacked by the notice board at Ammet Ford for anyone who wanted their own copy to keep, the print already gone soft at the creases from three days against my ribs. I don't know why I'd taken it. I take things sometimes the way I count things — not because I've decided to, because some part of me that isn't consulting the rest has already reached out and done it, and I find out after. I unfolded it by the fire while Maria and Edie talked low about the road ahead, and I read it again the way you read a thing you've already read when you're hunting for the part that's wrong instead of the part that's true.

*Claims payable in the seventh year.* That was the number that had been sitting under my tongue since the standpipe, sweeter than it had any right to be. My mother had been sick four years. Four, going on five. The Sweetwater — that's what they call it in the valley, not Ammet Ford's kind, ours, the kind that starts in the joints and takes its time about the rest — doesn't kill fast and doesn't kill slow, it kills on a schedule, and I'd watched enough of the valley go under it by now, cousins and neighbours and men I'd worked stone beside as a boy, to know roughly what that schedule was without ever once sitting down and counting it out loud. Five years, most of them. Six, if you'd gone in strong. Nobody drew a payment before year seven because nobody who'd drunk that water long enough to need paying was left standing to collect it before year seven. That wasn't a coincidence of arithmetic. Somebody had run the numbers before they built the scheme, not after, and had set the payout exactly past the point where paying it would cost the company anything at all.

I didn't say any of that out loud. I folded the handbill back along its own soft creases and put it away, and I want to be honest about why, since I'm being honest about everything else tonight except the one thing that matters: it wasn't kindness, keeping it to myself. Maria would have wanted to do something with a fact like that — write to someone, petition someone, believe there was a someone worth petitioning. Edie would have wanted a name and a figure and a plan with a beginning and an end already costed. I didn't want either of those things done with it yet. I wanted to hold it a while longer, the way you hold a coal before you've decided what you're going to burn.

Edie took first watch and walked the wall line with the ledger under her arm, and Maria stayed by the fire building the last of the wood into something that would carry till she woke Edie's relief. I watched the flame do the thing flame does, breathing in and out of itself, throwing shapes up the drystone that moved wrong for half a second and then moved right again, the way everything does when I'm tired enough that I can't trust my own eyes to hold still. I sat with that quietly, the way I sit with all of it, until I couldn't anymore.

"Maria." My hands were doing something without me — turning a stripped willow twig over and over, end for end, and I made them stop, because watching your own hands lie to you about being calm is its own kind of tiring. "The fire. Is it —" I didn't have the rest of the sentence built. I don't usually start one without the whole thing framed and squared first, but this one I'd been carrying since Ammet Ford, since the shadows on the school wall, and it came out before I'd finished making it safe to say. "Is it doing what I think it's doing? Just an ordinary fire. Nothing in it."

She didn't laugh, and she didn't make it smaller than it was by being gentle about it, which I've come to understand is the rarest thing anyone does for me. She looked at the fire properly, the way you'd look at a thing somebody had actually asked you to check rather than be soothed about, and she said, "It's a fire, Lloyd. Ordinary as they come. Bit smoky on account of the green wood in it, which is Edie's fault for building it before the wood had dried."

"Right," I said.

"You needed to ask that."

"Yes."

"Okay," she said, and didn't ask why, and didn't make it bigger than I'd made it, and went back to feeding the one fire in the valley that was, provably, only a fire. I sat there and let the relief move through me slow, the way relief does when you've been braced too long to take it all at once. I hadn't doubted the fire, not really. I'd doubted whatever in me does the doubting. There's a difference, and she'd answered the actual question instead of the one I'd asked, which is a skill I don't have and have watched her use on other people a dozen times without understanding it, until tonight, how much it costs to do that for someone who might, for all she knew, have been asking about the whole world and not just the fire.

What happened at Callow Ford, I'm not going to put down here.

Not because it isn't mine to tell — it's the one thing in this account that's entirely mine, more than the arm, more than the room with no window — but because I've spent the whole of this getting you to understand how I decide what gets shown, and the honest end of all that explaining is to actually do it once, in front of you, instead of just describing the practice. I said the version I'd built. I said it to the one person it was built for. Some of what came back wasn't in any version I'd built room for, and none of it is going in a handbill folded soft in my coat, or a story told to a woman on a hillside, or a page in whatever this is. It cost what it cost. I paid it. We were three days getting to that door, and I've decided that's as much of the getting-there you'll have from me — and I find, setting down even that much, that I can finally do the thing I couldn't do at the fire, which is stop counting.

We were back on the north road inside the week, the fold-wall hills behind us and lower country ahead, and I noticed, the first morning out, that I'd stopped reaching for the coat pocket where the handbill sat, which I took to mean I'd finally decided what I was going to do with it, even if I hadn't decided when. Edie had the map out before either of us had finished the bread, tracing the line toward the next crossing with one blunt finger. Maria walked a half-step ahead of both of us the way she does when she's decided a day's going to be a good one before it's given her any reason to think so, and for a while it was. There were two men on the road ahead of us by midmorning, walking slow, wearing the grey and white of the Mercies, and Maria lifted a hand to them before I'd even finished deciding what it was I was looking at.
