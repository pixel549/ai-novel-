# 19. What It Was Worth

By the twelfth morning the paper boy had stopped crying the story outside our window, and I knew before I was properly awake that something in the world had moved on without asking either of us whether we were ready. I lay there a while working out what the quiet actually was. Not peace. Just the absence of a particular noise. The mill two streets over had started up again at its ordinary hour, which meant somebody had decided the world could go back to needing cloth, and I lay listening to it and thinking that eleven days was apparently the length of time a country could spare for a basement full of the drowned before it needed its cloth again too.

Lloyd was already up, which itself had stopped being news. He'd taken to testing the arm on small, undramatic things — the stuck second drawer of the washstand, which he now opened by reaching straight through the face of it rather than fighting the wood, calm as a man checking a knife was still sharp before he put it away. He did it without an audience in mind, which was how I came to see most of it: through a half-open door, over the top of a book I wasn't reading. Watching him straight on would only have made him stop, so mostly I didn't. That was the part I kept noticing and not saying anything about. For six years of knowing him, every use of that arm had come wrapped in an apology that never got spoken, a kind of flinch before and after, as though the wrongness of it might rub off on whoever saw it done. This had none of that in it. He reached through the drawer, took out his shirt, and let the drawer be a drawer again, and his face while he did it had the specific stillness of a man doing an ordinary chore. He'd taken to shadowing the motions of the fight, too, in the mornings when he thought I was asleep — not the fight itself, just the shape of standing ready for one, weight settled low, the bad arm held as a man holds a tool he trusts. Six weeks, I had nursed him through not being able to stand unaided, and still I did not know what to do with a man who stood like that now, six days clear of the last of it, as though his body had simply decided the matter was closed.

"Stopped shouting it," he said, meaning the boy, meaning the street, meaning the whole eleven days collapsing finally into something a child could be paid to stop repeating. He might have been telling me the kettle had boiled.

"You sound almost disappointed."

"No." He thought about it anyway, honestly — he thought about everything I asked him straight. "Didn't need it to keep shouting. Already happened."

I understood, by then, roughly what he meant, though I would have put it less calmly. He had gone into Ascension House carrying nine years of being the only one who saw what he saw and not one person who'd believe him, and he had come out of it having been right, publicly, documented, in front of a bishop who'd had to weep about it in the Senate. I don't think he'd slept so well in years as he had those eleven nights. I watched him fold his own shirt, one-handed and unhurried, and thought: he has never once, in all the time I've known him, looked less like a man carrying something. It should have comforted me more than it did. I loved him for it and I was frightened of it in the same breath, and I didn't examine the second part too closely, because I had learned by then that some of my own fears about him were better left where they couldn't get at anything.

He caught me watching, once, and didn't flinch from that either — just held my eye a second, unbothered, and went back to the shirt. A year before he'd have found something to do with his hands that put his back to me. I told myself that was healing. Some of it was.

---

Edie came at noon, which was unusual only in that she knocked rather than simply being there when we turned around. She had the oilcloth ledger with her, the same one that had held the stud book until she'd handed that on to whoever was going to make somebody answer for it, and she set it on the table now and opened it as other people say grace. She'd had a bath somewhere between the last time I'd seen her and now, and her hair was combed flat instead of simply out of the way, and I understood both of those as news before she said a word.

"Figured the account," she said.

She had, too — three columns in a hand so plain it looked printed, rent and bread and the surgeon's fee we'd never paid because I'd done the surgeon's work myself, a line for the broadsheet's printing costs that someone at the paper had apparently insisted on itemising to her as though she'd ask for a discount. There was a line for the room's second blanket, and one for the coal the landlady had sworn blind she wasn't charging separately for and was. At the bottom, underlined once: a figure, and beside it, in the same flat hand, two words. *Paid out.*

"You didn't need to bring us the coal," I said.

"Needed to know what it cost." She said it without looking up, as if the distinction were obvious, which to her it plainly was. Edie had never once, in my hearing, cared what a thing was worth. She had only ever cared what it cost. Those were not the same question. Slowly, I'd come to understand that she was the only one of the three of us who never once confused them.

"Ascension House doesn't reopen," she said, not looking up from the page, as if it were simply the next line of the sum. "The Sanctum's keeping the two under-priors it threw to the courts. Nobody's rebuilding a basement that's already in three newspapers. That's the only number in this whole business that was ever actually going to move, and it moved."

Not since she'd first told us, flat and unembellished, what four years in the tunnels had been like, had I seen her so nearly pleased with anything. It sat oddly on her, not wrong, just unfamiliar, and I kept glancing back at her to be sure I'd read it right the second time. She closed the ledger with the particular satisfaction of a woman who has made two numbers meet. Her shoulders, which I had never once seen her set down even sitting still, were down. I don't think she knew it. I didn't tell her.

"Good," Lloyd said, from across the room, not looking up either. One word, and he meant the whole of it — the fire, the register, the eleven days, the bishop's wet eyes, closed and squared like Edie's ledger, an account that had come out even. I believed that he believed it. I think I even believed it myself, for the length of that one syllable; some things are true for exactly as long as it takes someone you trust to say them plainly, in a room with good light, and not a second longer.

Then the light shifted off the wall, as afternoon light does whether or not anyone's finished with it, and I was myself again.

---

I did not tell them the other sum.

I did not tell them that the ward Edie called burned was indeed burned, and that the programme that had filled it was not — that a programme is not a building, it is sixty years of doctrine and a belief sincerely held by people who were, every one of them, still breathing and still salaried; that two under-priors were not the Sanctum, any more than the drawer Lloyd had just put his shirt back in was the house it stood in. I did not tell them that it was, by my own reckoning of the hour, somewhere near dinnertime in Carrowgate, and that I had stood at this same window the evening before and watched the gaslamps come up early over the better streets, soot-haze or no soot-haze, punctual regardless, and thought with a plainness that frightened me how many of the people who had ordered the ward built were at this moment sitting down to a meal they had not had to think twice about ordering.

I did not tell them that my mother had been promoted for it. I still thought the word *mother* before I could stop myself, every time, and hated myself a fresh amount each time for not yet having found a better one; six years of running from her and I had apparently kept the word in a drawer of my own, ready to reach for.

Three days before, in a copy of the broadsheet Lloyd no longer bothered buying, I'd read that the full intake record — the one with Anse Duley's name in it, the one that had come out from under our own floorboard in my own hands — had been consigned, alongside the bound volume of sixty years of dated failures that the under-priors' advocate had produced as a show of the Church's own thoroughness in record-keeping, to the Vantry assembly's standing archive, where a bound volume of that kind, however damning, draws almost no one once the novelty of its existence has worn off a shelf. Nobody reads a ledger once the story about the ledger has stopped being new. Reading that, I thought I finally understood what the Church meant when it called a thing *settled.* Not resolved. Filed.

I thought of the letter under our own floorboard, in the exact space the register had left, and did not go and look at it, because I knew precisely what it said without looking, having read it four times the evening it came and not once since. Asking after my hands. Nothing else. Whether that was tenderness or habit, I still couldn't say — she had always tended to the body first, in the almshouse, on the theory that a fed, warm person answers the soul's questions better than a starved one — and for now I'd given up trying to decide which.

What we had bought, for everything it had cost — Lloyd's arm set crooked under my own hands because I hadn't the strength left to try again, six weeks of a bucket and a prayer I despised and kept saying anyway, a register I'd carried under a floorboard for longer than I'd carried any grief honestly — was eleven days in which it was the only story in the country. Eleven days, against sixty years. I did the arithmetic as Edie would have done it, flat and without comfort, and it did not come out even. I let the two of them have their sums. Mine I kept to myself. Somebody who loved me would only have insisted on carrying half of it, and I wasn't yet brave enough to hand it over.

---

Edie left for Carrowgate two days later, in the grey hour before the mill whistle, with the same canvas bag she'd arrived with and nothing added to it that I could see, except that the bag had been patched since, at the corner, in thread that didn't match. She'd settled the landlady herself, a week paid forward that neither of us had asked her to pay, and she told us this as she told us everything: a fact already closed, not a kindness still open for comment. The ledger she left on the table, closed for good. "For the next one," she said, meaning the next account that would need keeping, not meaning she expected to be paid back, and I understood I was not to argue with that either.

At the door she held out a slip of paper with a street and a number on it in the same plain hand, no name attached to either, folded once, the fold already gone soft as though she'd carried it in a pocket for longer than this morning.

"If either of you comes to Carrowgate," she said.

"Why would we—" Lloyd started.

"Didn't say you would." She was already looking past us, down the stair, at whatever came next on her own private ledger. "Said if."

"What's in Carrowgate," he tried again, mild, same as he asked anything he already half expected would go unanswered.

"Work." A fact set down and not picked back up. She shouldered the bag, and that was the whole of her explanation and the last either of us got.

She did not wait for thanks, because she never had, in all the months I'd known her, waited for anything of the kind, or asked for anything to set against it — not money, though she'd had less of it than either of us; not comfort, though she'd sat up twice watching Lloyd's colour change in the night and said nothing about what that must have cost her to do without anyone asking how she was managing it. I stood holding the paper and worked out, a full ten seconds too late to say anything useful with the knowledge, that in all that time she had never once asked either of us for a single thing. Not her fare out of Vantry. Not a hand with the bag. Not so much as a kind word. She'd have had no use for one. I nearly offered it anyway, out of habit, before I caught myself.

By the time I'd worked out what I might have said to that, I could hear her on the stair, and then on the street below, and then I couldn't hear her at all, and the paper in my hand was the only part of her still in the room.
