# 25. Year Seven

Eleven days after they buried Crane, Ferrick took the Indemnity chair, and I read about it secondhand, three days late, off a broadsheet somebody else had already finished with and left folded on a chop-house counter. The funeral had been enormous. State colours on the coffin, the whole Senate walking behind it bareheaded in a rain the papers called fitting and I called ordinary rain, and Ferrick himself, by every account, white-faced and nearly wordless at the graveside — a man discovering in public exactly how much he had admired someone he'd spent two years quietly failing to move.

He did not stay wordless. Within the week he had the sub-panel's old figures back on the Senate floor, the ones that had sat ringed in red on his own wall for a season doing nothing, and this time nobody voted them down, because voting down a dead man's last cause in front of his widow is something almost no senator will do twice, and Ferrick, grieving and sincere and finally useful, only had to ask once. The schedule came forward eleven years. The works water got tested the same month, by men with proper glass and proper paper rather than a girl with a tin cup and a story nobody printed. Three wells in the lower valley were capped before the first frost. I know the number because I made myself find it, as I make myself find all the numbers that matter now, and most of the ones that don't: three wells, forty-one households, a figure a clerk read aloud at a hearing I did not attend and Lloyd did not get to hear named, though I told it to him anyway, later, across our own table, in the one room in Carrowgate he was still allowed to sit in without a description of his face walking in ahead of him.

Nobody has ever given me a number for what the old schedule would have cost the valley before it paid out, honestly, in the seventh year the scheme had been built around from the start — the year by which enough of the claimants would already be dead to make the payment smaller. Ferrick beat that arithmetic by six years and some months without once noticing he was racing it. I did the sum anyway, asked or not, because I have stopped being able to stop myself: six years is a boy of nine getting to be a boy of fifteen with a mother living in it, instead of a photograph of one on the mantel and a settlement letter underneath. I did not tell Ferrick that sum. I am not certain what a man that decent would have done with it, and some cowardice in me did not especially want to find out.

The Church lost its own spring hearing the same fortnight, and not because the registers had finally persuaded anyone — the registers had been doing the same patient, honest work since Vantry and persuading nobody the whole time. It lost the hearing because a Church that had just put a crude, blinding burst of somebody's Arcana into a Senate corridor, in front of a dozen clerks, to help kill a sitting member, could not also be trusted that same season with a hearing about how it ran its hospitals. I waited that whole autumn for someone clever to pull on the one loose thread I knew about — a scrap of altar-cloth, caught on a window latch an arm's length from where Crane fell, planted by a woman who has never once been wrong about where a building's weak doors are. Nobody pulled on it. Nobody even looked especially hard. It had done exactly the job it was left to do, and something that convenient rarely gets questioned afterward. It only gets used, and filed, and believed.

Lloyd listened to most of this without comment, as he listens to most things that have already happened and can't be changed by his opinion of them, turning a coin over and over on the table without seeming to notice his own hand doing it. When I read him the figure about the wells he said only, "Good," flat as a closed ledger, and went back to stacking what was left of the week's wages into its three small towers. Months ago, I'd stopped asking why he did that. It turned out, in the end, that I was wrong to.

***

Edie sent word she wanted to see me. That alone was unusual enough that I went the same afternoon rather than finding a reason not to. I found her in Ferrick's front hall, under the letters of thanks, with the girl who checked deliveries against a list standing a polite distance off, and Ferrick himself coming down the stairs with both hands out to take mine before I had my coat half off.

He thanked me for my part in Crane's last review. It was generous of him and almost entirely untrue. Then he turned and thanked Edie, properly, in front of me and the girl and anyone else in the house who cared to hear it, for keeping his hands clean through the worst month of his life. He said it exactly like that. Clean. He believed it as he believes everything: completely, without any need to check. I watched Edie's face do nothing at all while he said it, no flinch, no correction, nothing a stranger could have read as anything but a competent woman accepting a fair compliment.

She let him believe it. I understood, watching her, that this was no longer the woman who had spent eleven years holding a line so that one bad night would keep its name. She had stopped asking whether she stood on the right side of anything and started only asking what an action produced, and she had clearly already run that sum on Ferrick's gratitude and found it would cost nothing to let stand. It was not a fall. I want to be honest about that, since nobody else is likely to say it on her behalf: it looked, on her, like the first entirely honest position she had held since whatever happened in that tunnel, because for once she wasn't performing a morality she'd priced too high to actually afford.

On the wall behind Ferrick's shoulder the calendar still hung where I remembered it, the spring hearing's date ringed twice in red, except the month had turned past it now and somebody had simply left the ring there rather than crossing it out, a small unfinished arithmetic of its own. Down the passage, I knew without checking, the iron cabinet was still bolted to the nursery floor, still holding the real Vantry registers exactly as banded, because nobody had needed to open it in the end. The true evidence had lost. The lie about a dead man's murderer had won, cleanly, in a single afternoon, and I left that house with no idea what to do with the comparison except carry it, same as I have carried most of what this year has taught me.

At the door Edie touched the cord at her own wrist — the old hemp one, brass-ferruled, that I had watched her coil and recoil a hundred times without once asking what it had been for before the war took the rest of the meaning out of it — and left it where it was rather than tucking it under her cuff as she usually did. She said nothing about why. I did not ask. Some things between us have never needed the asking.

***

Lloyd could not go north with me. His likeness was on a board in every market town between Carrowgate and the valley by then, close enough to be dangerous and wrong enough in the particulars — a different name under it, a jaw drawn too narrow — that I understood, slowly, the two of them had given a false name to whoever took the job on, tradesman's habit, the same instinct that had Edie pricing a journey to the penny before she'd spend it. It kept him alive. It also meant that a man from Callow Ford walking into Callow Ford, however careful, was a risk neither of us was willing to run the arithmetic on twice.

The night before I left, I finally asked him, after four months of watching him count it out every evening and never once asking, what the habit had always been for, back before any of this, back when he was sending money home every month for nine years and never visiting. He said he didn't know how else to tell a person something was still owed without saying it out loud, and that counting it was the part he could do. Then he pushed one of his three small towers of coin across the table toward me without being asked. From Lloyd, that is nearly a speech. "For her," he said. "Not for anything. Just for her."

Callow Ford had a new standpipe where the old well had been, cast iron, unremarkable, dripping slightly at a joint some mason hadn't quite finished. The house had not changed. The same half-inch gap still showed daylight through the north gable of the roof, exactly where he'd told me it was a year ago in a different county entirely, and I stood under it a moment before I knocked, absurdly moved by the fact that a fortune's worth of reform had reached the well and stopped twelve feet short of the roof, because nobody had thought to put the gap on a schedule.

Ivet Hale opened the door herself. That told me everything the broadsheets hadn't about how much better she was. Thinner than I'd pictured her, grey at the temple in the same place Edie is, with a steadiness to her that four years of the old water would have burned out of most people twice over. She took me for a friend of Lloyd's from his working days. It was true in every particular except the ones that mattered, and she put the kettle on before I'd finished my name.

She asked after him three times in an hour, in three different ways, as a mother asks when she already half-knows the answer will be managed rather than true and has decided to accept the managing. Working steady, I told her. Further south than he'd like. Not able to get away this year, though he'd sent what he could, and I set the coin on her table slowly, plainly, so it would read as what it was and not as charity. I did not tell her the name on the board three villages over, or whose death put it there, or that the son she was picturing at some honest trade had put his bare hand against another man's chest, in the end, and stopped it without leaving a single mark a coroner could name. I did not tell her any of it. I let her believe the only lie in this whole year that has never once cost anyone else anything, and if that makes me a little like the Church after all — deciding on someone else's behalf what she can and cannot be made to carry — I have decided I can live with being that much like it, provided I am careful about which lies I choose and who pays for them.

I went back south on the same coach road, past the same standpipe, and did not look at the roof again on my way out, because I did not trust myself to leave if I did.

He was waiting exactly where I'd left him, standing rather than sitting. That meant he'd been standing since before he could have heard the coach. "Is this real," he said, not as a question, in the flat voice he only uses now when he's been alone too long with nothing to argue him back down. I have learned not to answer that one quickly.

"Cup," I said, and touched it. "Window. Rain on it, see. Blanket, where you left it on the chair." His shoulders came down a half-inch with each object, the same slow unwinding I'd watched a dozen times now and never once gotten used to. "Your hand." I took it, the real one, warm, the elbow still wrong where it had always been wrong. "Mine."

He let out a breath that had clearly been waiting the whole four days for permission.

"Still here," he said. Not a question this time.

"Still here," I said, and sat down across from him at the table where his coin had been, and did not let go of his hand to do it.
