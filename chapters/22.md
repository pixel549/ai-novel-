# 22. The Argument

I told him I needed three days before I would answer him, and he said yes. He said yes to everything now that the saying was done — flat, immediate, no argument in it. That left the arguing to me. I seemed to be the only one of us still doing it.

Three days, I had decided, as though a number made it a plan and not simply a length of rope I was giving myself before I either cut him down or climbed up after him. Even saying it to myself I could hear what was wrong with the thinking. The rope was the same rope either way. The only question left open was which end I meant to be standing on.

The room helped nothing. It was the same room it had been the night before — the plan still on the table, the candle stub still holding it flat, the coal-yard wages still stacked in three small towers of coin because he counted them out that way every evening and I had never once asked why. Rain came and went against the one window without committing to either. Edie was elsewhere in the city, at whatever Ferrick currently had her doing, and for once I was glad of the distance. I did not want her competence anywhere near what I was about to try. She would have told me, correctly, which of my ideas were worth the walk before I wasted the leather on my boots finding out myself.

The first day I went to the law courts, because it seemed the respectable place to start, and respectability was doing a great deal of work for me that week, holding open a door I would rather not have examined too closely in daylight. A clerk behind a counter the length of a coffin told me, without any particular unkindness, that a private person could not lay an information against a sitting member of the Senate. The Crown Office alone could do that, and the Crown Office would want a great deal more than a flooded well and a column from an actuary's table before it moved against a man who sat on three committees and dined with two judges a month. He wrote nothing down. There was nothing yet, he said, to write.

The broadsheet's Carrowgate desk was two streets further on and a good deal less patient about it. The man whose name still ran above the Vantry story — the one that had made the three of us briefly famous to nobody who knew our faces — read my two pages standing up and handed them back before I had found a chair. Numbers, he said. A survey nobody outside a drafting office could parse, a shareholding tucked under two other men's names, a schedule that would not come due for years yet. No bodies. No register of the dead with a name a reader could put a face to. He had printed the Church because the Church had left him a wall of named files to hold up to the light. Crane had left a column of figures, and figures did not sell, and the last thing he had printed over an unnamed source had already been called a forgery once in the Senate that same month. He was not eager to be called that twice in the same year over the same kind of silence. I thanked him for his time. It cost me nothing; he hadn't given me much of it.

Lloyd heard both of it out that evening, over bread neither of us finished, and told me he already knew. He had asked a different clerk the same question eleven days after Vantry went to print, in the same coat he was wearing now. He had read two years of that same broadsheet's back issues in the public room of the library before he had ever written Crane's name on a floor plan.

"Years yet on the schedule," he said. "I knew that before you asked him."

He was not telling me this to win. He was telling me there was nothing in it I could find that he had not already found, and set aside, months before I thought to go looking. I sat with that for a while, turning the last heel of bread over without eating it, and found I was not even angry about it. He had been more patient with this than I had ever seen him patient with anything, and the patience itself was the part that frightened me, more than any single door he'd already priced.

There was an hour that first night, before either of us said anything else, where he mended the strap on his work bag and I darned a sock that didn't need darning, and neither of us spoke, and it was, for that hour, almost exactly like every other evening we'd had in that room for three weeks. I let myself have it. A person is allowed one hour like that per bad week, I have come to think, and spending it on purpose, rather than waiting for it to arrive by accident, is not a small kind of cowardice but a necessary one.

The second day was the sub-panel's offices — a converted townhouse with a brass plate too modest for what it claimed to oversee. A secretary took my two pages, read the heading only, and explained that submissions went into a docket, and the docket was read in the order it was built, and the chairman was presently occupied with hearings a year deep already into matters older and better evidenced than mine. He offered me a receipt. No piece of paper had ever told me so plainly how little weight my own carried.

That was the evening the waiting came up.

"We let it run," I said. "The seventh year comes. The claims come due. If the dying matches his own numbers to the week, nobody can call it a coincidence twice."

"No," Lloyd said.

"Why not? It's the same evidence either way, and nobody has to carry anything to get it. We just have to be patient."

"Because the seventh year is the plan. He built the wait in on purpose. Waiting's not us catching him out. Waiting's us doing what he's already paying for."

"Somebody still has to count the graves."

"Somebody always has to count the graves. That's not new. The new part would be us deciding we're fine with being the ones who did the counting instead of the stopping."

There was no answer to that worth looking for. The arithmetic behind it was his, not mine, and it already knew more about dying on a schedule than I wanted to. I thought of the poster we'd passed on the road out of Ammet Ford years ago now, THE LARROW INDEMNITY, claims payable in the seventh year, and how three strangers had read it approvingly in front of us like a kindness the Commonwealth was doing them, and I understood, properly, for the first time, that the kindness had a closing date stamped on it before anyone had died enough to collect.

The third day I did not go out. I sat with Edie's old ledger — mine now, she had said, for the next one, and had left it on the table without turning back to see whether I'd pick it up — and went through the Vantry tallies again, looking for the shape of an argument that might carry over: two institutions, two buried sets of numbers, one of them already proved in front of witnesses. If a man would not believe a column of figures on its own, maybe he would believe a second column, set beside a first he had already, however briefly, believed. I thought, once, of the letter still folded under the floorboard in the room we'd left in Vantry, and of going back to that well regardless of how dry I already knew it to be, and put the thought down again almost as quickly as it arrived. Whatever I still owed that building, it wasn't this.

Lloyd let me finish laying it out before he answered. The silence itself was most of the answer.

"They already called the first one forged," he said. "Nobody prints a second story out of the same silence once they've called the first one a lie. And if anyone ever does trace that first leak back to us — a senator has more reason to look hard than a bishop did — they'll find it sitting next to a motive they didn't have before."

I had nothing left after that. Four tries, four costs, and he had carried every one of them in his head for longer than I had known to ask the questions.

So I asked him the one thing I had not yet put into words, because it was the only argument that was not about Crane at all.

"Say none of it goes wrong," I said. "Say you walk in and walk out and no face in that building ever learns yours. Say every door is exactly where you've drawn it. You will still have killed a man, Lloyd. You will carry that every day for the rest of your life, and it will not give your mother a well. It will not undo four years already gone. It will not touch the next Crane the valley gets, because there is always a next one, and they will build the first one a statue, and nobody will ever think to ask who priced the water."

He thought about that longer than he had thought about anything I had said in three days.

"You're right," he said.

"Then —"

"It doesn't change anything."

I stayed anyway.

Not because he had won the argument. He had not. There was no argument left standing by the end of it. We had agreed on every point that mattered and arrived at opposite ends regardless. By any sane measure that should have finished things. I stayed because if I did not, he would be alone with it by morning, and alone he has never once correctly told fact from fever without someone standing in the room to say so, and I am the only one left standing in it. That is the whole of my reasoning. I made myself say it in those exact words, once, out loud, to the empty half of the room, so I could not later pretend I had found a better one.

It is also, I noted, with no warmth in the noting at all, exactly Vail's reasoning. She kept Maria because Maria was the one who would set a rule aside for the person in front of her. I am keeping Lloyd on precisely the same coin. The Church never once called that love. If I am honest with myself — something I am only now managing once a day, some days — neither should I.

I went back in. He was still at the table, the plan weighted flat under the candle stub exactly as it had been every night since I had found it, and he looked up like a man waiting to be told whether the room was real.

"There's bread left," I said. It was not an answer to anything. I sat down across from him and ate it anyway.
