The narrator, Maria, spends three weeks at the Carrowgate room Edie's address led them to, taking in mending and sitting up with a neighbor's overdue lying-in while Lloyd takes day labor at a coal yard, and for the first week lets herself believe the rest is simply what comes after Vantry, until a smear of dressed-stone dust on his boot tells her otherwise. Watching him more carefully, she realizes his evening walks never actually go anywhere but always end within sight of the Senate, and that the men he keeps falling into conversation with are not friends but clerks and doormen from the Indemnity Office, each one mined for a schedule, a latch time, a stair closure, while he gives away nothing of why he wants to know. She tests and discards three respectable explanations — boredom, job-hunting, a shared homesickness for anything permanent — and concludes, watching him sleep, that none of them fit a man who has never once found comfort in anything large. Going through his coat while he sleeps, she finds a session calendar matched against a watch roster, a traced floor plan with three doors marked, and, written in his own hand beside one of them, the name Halvard Crane — the man who, Lloyd once said in passing, owns the works and, in the sense that matters, the whole of the Larrow Valley.
