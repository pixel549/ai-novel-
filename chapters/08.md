# 8. Sweetwater

The road changed before the town did. Somewhere past the second milestone out of Hessel Bight the ruts smoothed into proper camber, crushed stone rolled flat and drained at both edges, and I found myself watching my own boots for a while because a road that didn't try to turn my ankle was a thing I'd stopped expecting anywhere south of Carrowgate. Edie noticed it too — she always notices ground before she notices anything standing on it — and said the culverts were new, poured concrete, not fieldstone, and didn't say anything else, which from her is a kind of wonder.

Ammet Ford announced itself with a rail spur before it announced itself with a name. Iron rails split off the main line and ran in over a low embankment toward a stack of new brick chimneys, and the smoke coming off them was the clean grey of coal burned properly rather than the black rag-smoke of a fire fed on whatever would take a flame. The houses along the approach road were new brick too, two rooms up and two down, slate roofs instead of thatch, and every third one had glass in its windows instead of oiled paper or a shutter kept closed. I counted the glass because counting is a thing I do with a fact I don't know what to do with otherwise, and I got to eleven before I made myself stop.

There was a school. I hadn't expected a school. It sat back from the road behind a low wall, single-storey, and every window in it was glazed, real glass, throwing back the grey morning light in a long unbroken row like the building was showing its teeth. In the yard behind the wall a dozen children were running some game with a knotted rag for a ball, shouting the rules of it at each other the way children do when the rules are being invented fresh each round to suit whoever's winning, and not one of them was thin in the way I knew thin, not one of them had the grey look about the mouth that a valley winter puts on a child by February. I had gone to a school with two windows and neither of them glass, in a room that took thirty children in the winter for the warmth of thirty bodies rather than for the lesson, and I stood there long enough, watching that game go on without me the way every game I'd ever half-watched from outside a wall had gone on without me, that Maria's shoulder came up against mine before I moved on.

A board at the fork of the road read LARROW CONSOLIDATED WORKS — AMMET FORD DIVISION, black letters on white, freshly painted, and under it, smaller, a name I already knew the shape of before I read it. I have carried that name a long time now, folded small and put away somewhere I don't take out in company, and seeing it stencilled clean and civic on a signpost did something to the folding — not loosened it, nothing so soft as that, but reminded me it was there, the way you feel an old break in the weather before you feel the ache of it. I put my hands in my coat pockets and kept walking. Nobody asked me anything, so I didn't have to decide what I'd have said.

There was a standpipe at the crossroads, cast iron, a tin cup on a chain long enough to reach the trough beneath it, and a man in works overalls filling a bucket who straightened up when he saw us look thirsty, which we were, four days out from anywhere with a pump that worked. "Go on," he said, working the handle for us without being asked, the water coming up clear and cold and loud against the tin. "Sweetest water in the valley, that. Company sank the well proper, forty foot down through the clay, none of your surface muck in it." He drank a cupful himself to prove it, wiped his mouth, filled the cup again and held it out.

Maria took it first and drank it down and said it was good, and meant it — I could tell the difference in her by then, the way she says a true thing plainly and a kind lie with an extra beat of warmth in it, and this one came out plain. Edie drank hers in three swallows the way she does everything, checking a box, and handed the cup back without comment beyond a nod, which for Edie is closer to praise than most people manage with a paragraph.

I took the cup because refusing it would have been a thing to explain, and there is no explaining I do easily. It was sweet. Not the mineral sweetness of a good spring, which I know from home, the kind that leaves your tongue a little cleaner than it found it — this had a thickness under the sweet, a hush at the back of the throat like something had been added to quiet the water down, make it sit still and agreeable in a way water doesn't sit on its own. I looked down into the cup before I drank the second half, and for a half-second, the length of a held breath, I thought the surface of it wasn't moving the way water moves when a hand's holding the cup that's holding it — flat, held, the way the chalice liquid had sat in its bowl at Stillwell not rippling for anyone. Then my own hand shook, ordinary hand tremor, and the water in the cup did what water does, and I told myself that was that. I didn't say anything. I have learned the exact weight a thing has to reach before it's worth the cost of saying it out loud, and a still cup of water didn't come close.

We walked on through the market square, where a woman was selling bread at a price that made Edie's eyebrows do something they don't usually bother doing, and past the works gate itself, where the shift was changing — men coming off in a slow tired column, men going on in a faster one, both columns better shod than any I'd seen since the war ended. A notice board stood by the gate under a little slate roof of its own, protected from the weather like something the company wanted read and kept readable, and on it, alongside the shift roster and a notice about boot allowances, was a printed sheet headed THE LARROW INDEMNITY, with a seal at the top that meant nothing to me and evidently meant something to everyone reading it, because two men had stopped in front of it on their way off shift and were reading it slow, the way men read something that concerns them directly.

"Seven years," one of them said to the other, tapping the paper with a blunt finger. "That's not long to wait, is it, when you think what they could've made us wait."

"My father won't see it," the second one said, without much heat in it, the way you say a fact about weather. "But my boy will. Company's put a number to it and everything — per household, registered against the works roll. Fair's fair."

"Fairer than nothing," the first one said, and they went on, not unhappily, the way men go when a thing has been decided somewhere over their heads and has, this once, decided in their favour, or looks to have.

Further on, outside the school wall, a woman waiting for a child mentioned the same thing to another woman waiting for a different child, unprompted, the two of them agreeing with each other in the easy way people agree about weather or bread prices — that whatever the seventh year brought, it was more than the valley villages upriver would ever see, and wasn't it something, the company doing right by people at all, when it needn't. I heard three of them say some version of the same relief inside of an hour and did not once hear anyone ask what the seven years were *for*, why a number that particular, why a company that could sink a well forty foot through clay needed seven years to work out what it owed people it had already decided to pay. I have no proof there's a question there. I have the shape of one, the way I have the shape of most things that turn out to matter, and no way yet to say it that wouldn't sound like a man seeing shadows on a wall lit plain as day for everyone else.

Edie had her ledger out by the time we reached the far side of the works, sitting on the low wall opposite the gate with the stub of pencil she keeps for exactly this, and she talked while she wrote, mostly to herself, the way she does when she's working a sum out loud to be sure of it. "Two hundred on the day shift, near enough, at wages I haven't seen paid since before the war. Houses with glass. A school that isn't a room over a stable. Water that doesn't have to be boiled." She totted a column and looked up at me, not unkindly, the way she looks at anyone she's decided to be plain with. "I've seen what the Reconstruction money does to towns it reaches, Lloyd, which is nothing, mostly, and I've seen what it does here, which is this. I can't make the arithmetic come out badly just because the man's name I don't care for. On balance, it's good. More good than I expected off any of Crane's money reaching past his own dinner table."

I didn't answer her. There wasn't a way to answer her that didn't require handing over more than I was willing to hand over standing in a market square with a bread-seller close enough to hear, and Edie's sums weren't wrong, exactly — that was the trouble in it, that her sums were the kind of right that makes the other thing harder to say, not easier. I have found, in nine years of not saying things, that the hardest silences to keep are the ones built on top of somebody else's honest arithmetic.

Maria didn't push it. She'd noticed — she notices most things, and she'd have had to be trying hard not to notice a silence as complete as the one I'd been keeping since the school wall — but she only touched my sleeve once, on the way past the standpipe again where the works man was still filling buckets for anyone who wanted, the same brief weight of her hand I remembered from the rail crossing, there and gone, no words in it, asking nothing and offering nothing beyond the fact of her hand having been there. I have never told her what that touch does for me and don't intend to start now. It is possible she knows anyway. She generally does.

Past the works gate the road ran along the river for a stretch, and I could see, on the far bank, the intake channel where the works drew its water before whatever happened to it happened — a long low weir of new concrete, water sheeting off it smooth as glass, no different to look at than any mill-race I'd passed in nine years of passing them. That was the trouble with all of it. Nothing about Ammet Ford showed its working. A haunted corridor at Stillwell at least had the decency to look wrong before it did something wrong to you. This looked like Sunday.

We didn't stop the night. There were beds to be had — a boarding house near the works gate advertised them, clean linen, company rate — but none of us said we should take one, and I think all three of us understood without discussing it that none of us wanted to sleep inside the sound of that water running. We filled what we were carrying at the standpipe before we left, because a full skin is a full skin whatever's dissolved in it, and I drank from mine twice more on the road out before dusk without once deciding to and without once being thirsty enough to need it, only wanting, the way you want a thing your body has quietly decided is good for it before your mind's caught up to ask why.

The chimneys were still smoking their clean grey smoke when the road bent and put a stand of birches between us and them, and behind the birches, further off, past where the works stopped and the valley proper began, ordinary hills sat the way hills sit, indifferent, going dark at the edges as the light went. Somewhere past those hills was Callow Ford, and my mother, and whatever four years of a sickness the doctors up there couldn't name had made of her by now, and I found I couldn't put a shape yet to what it would do to me, seeing my own village against what I'd just walked through — whether it would look poorer for the comparison or whether some part of me had already decided, standing at that gate reading a notice about a seven-year wait, that I would rather it looked poor and honest than looked like this.

I said none of that. I hadn't said anything since the school wall and didn't start now. The sweetness was still sitting somewhere behind my tongue, faint, and patient, the way a thing sits when it isn't in any hurry to be noticed.
