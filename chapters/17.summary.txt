The narrator, Maria, spends six weeks nursing Lloyd through the injuries from Ascension House in a rented room in a mill district, healing him in triaged stages — skull first, then ribs, then the arm last, which she can only argue into a crooked repair rather than set clean — each session costing her the rest of the day, while Edie passes through twice with money and news before leaving again with the stud book and requisition orders. Alone with him, Maria keeps up the habit of naming ordinary objects aloud the way she did in the Salts, still prays out of a trained body rather than a kept faith, and recognizes that what broke in her at Ascension House was not the Church's doctrine but Ondine Vail herself, who was sincerely good to her for twenty years and is also the woman who ran the ward. At night she reads Anse Duley's name in the intake register she kept hidden under the floorboards and reckons, without absolution, that the theft which qualified her to Vail's attention was the same theft that bought Anse only two years before the Church took her anyway. On the fourth day Lloyd wakes fully for the first time and asks whether the room is real; Maria's answer catches in her throat long enough to frighten them both before she gives him the ordinary objects of the room, one by one, and he believes her.
