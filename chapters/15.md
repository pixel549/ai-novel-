# 15. The Siege

Edie didn't wait for either of us to answer her. She was already working out loud, in the flat ordering voice she used for a job rather than a conversation: documents first, because paper doesn't survive what comes after it; fire second, because this building wasn't standing past tonight whatever happened to the papers; and a route, decided now, back through the anteroom and the service door and the lane behind it, because deciding a way out after the smoke starts is how people die in buildings they know. She wanted the six decades of files from the extraction theatre and the big chained register off its chain, both in Maria's arms, since Maria was the one who could drop them in half a second if her hands were needed for anything else. She wanted the stud book and whatever requisition paper carried a signature, because a stud book proves what happened and a signature proves who let it, and those were going into her own coat. She didn't ask whether I was carrying anything. She never had. My job was the stair, same as it had been going down. That was how Edie decided things. It always had been.

Maria asked her, lightly, whether anyone had thought to consult the person who actually makes fire about whether this was a sensible plan, and when neither of us answered she told us anyway, because that was always how it went with her — the joke first, then the truth standing behind it before anyone had braced for it. She couldn't burn this place in the shape either of us was picturing. A five-metre burst in a room with twelve occupied tanks was twelve dead or near enough to it; a burst anywhere near the breeding ward did the same arithmetic to people too sedated to run from a door, let alone a wall of flame; and every room in this building worth burning was a room with something in it she wasn't willing to burn along with it. She'd stood in the tank room twice now, she said, and counted the same twelve heads both times, and a woman who could count to twelve wasn't entitled to pretend afterward that she hadn't. She could light a fire. She couldn't choose where it stopped once it started, and tonight the thing she had to be was the one person in the building thinking about where it stopped. She said it flat, for her, without the usual lift under it, and went to the register.

We split at the stair as she'd laid it out. Maria went first for the chained register, working the lock with both hands and not looking twice at the name she'd already found; I went down with her as far as the extraction theatre, because the case files were heavy enough to want two people carrying between trips, and because my arm was good for exactly this — the high shelves the leather and brass restraints wouldn't let a smaller woman's reach manage, whole runs of folders coming off in armfuls rather than one careful page at a time. For a while it was almost ordinary work, the kind we'd have done for honest wages in another life — stack, carry, stack again — and I let myself have it for as long as it lasted, both arms steady, the shelves giving up their weight one folder at a time. We were on the fourth armful, maybe the fifth, when the lamp in the ring room went out.

It hadn't been lit by anything we'd brought in with us.

I heard Edie's voice from the breeding ward before I understood the words — short, flat, the register she used for a fact rather than a warning. A stated fact from Edie was worse than any warning from somebody else. Then the thing from the last room was in the corridor with us, loose of whatever the brass ring had been keeping in, and it moved like nothing I'd ever had to describe to Maria before, no shape that held still long enough to be a shape. The tank room was two doors down from where we stood, twelve people floating within reach of whatever this turned into, and that thought arrived and sat where I couldn't get rid of it for the rest of the fight. My arm went cold at the shoulder exactly as it had the first time, except this time the cold didn't stay polite about it; both of it reached before I told it to — the one that grips and the one nobody else can see — and I had hold of something that had no business being solid, and it made a sound that didn't come from a throat. Somewhere behind me a shelf went over. One of the grown things on it was not non-viable after all, on its feet and wrong-angled and coming, and Edie said one word, and there wasn't a second one worth waiting for.

Maria set the register down on the only clean surface left in the room and turned, and the corridor went white for the length of a held breath — one bolt, placed, through the thing closing on Edie's back rather than mine, because Edie hadn't seen it and I had, and there hadn't been a way to say so shorter than doing it. It went down and stayed down. Maria's hand was already shaking before the light had finished dying out of the air, and she didn't look at what she'd done, and I'd have given a good deal, standing there, to tell her it hadn't cost her anything. We both knew exactly what it had cost her. I told her, when I had breath for it, that it was a hell of a way to run an evening lecture on fire safety. She didn't laugh. There wasn't room in her for it yet, and I let the joke lie there unclaimed rather than try it again.

After that she kept a small flame lit steady over her own palm the whole way back — unbothered, unglamorous, the single most useful thing any of us did that night, a floor lit for three people who would otherwise have been guessing where the next step was. One of the grown things came in low from a side door Edie's count hadn't allowed for, and she had, I think, half a second's warning of exactly that and nothing to spend it on; she turned into the blow instead of away from it, because turning into it cost less than taking it square, and it caught her along the ribs and put her into the wall. She was up again before I'd managed two steps toward her, telling us she was fine in a voice that dared either of us to check. Nobody made anything of it. That was the whole of what competence looked like that night — getting hit and staying upright, not getting hit at all.

The stair was mine. Edie went first with the papers, Maria behind her holding the light low and steady, and I took the bottom step and didn't move off it again. Three of them came for that stair in the time it took the women to clear it, and I don't know, in the only honest accounting I've ever given anyone including myself, how many of the three either of them would have seen coming if they'd turned around. Both arms were working by then — one on a throat with a pulse in it, one through a ribcage with none to speak of — and I was right about both of them, every time, on a night when being right about anything felt like the last thing left that was mine to be. Once, for the length of a breath, I had both hands on the same single thing at the same time, one gripping and one not, and whatever it had been came apart in a way that left nothing on the floor for either woman to ask me about afterward. I didn't explain it. There wasn't anything brief enough to say. The noise mostly went away once my hands were full. That's the mercy of a fight, if it has one: no room left over for the rest of it.

Maria took the stairs last, backward, one more bolt spent on something I didn't see because by then I had an account of my own to settle. I heard her say, after, that she'd have given almost anything not to have needed it twice in one night. I heard it thin and delayed, arriving a half-breath behind itself, and some part of me that was still keeping time understood, a beat late, that I was already on the floor and hadn't decided to be.

Something had got past the two I was watching and under the one I wasn't. I knew it as a plain fact before I felt it as pain, the knowing arriving first and the hurt catching up behind — my right arm gone somewhere it hadn't been invited, and the sound in my own skull when I went down not a sound anybody else on that stair had made. Maria was over me before I'd finished landing. I heard her start the words that open a healing, slow and physical, the kind that takes hours and leaves her no use to anyone after. I heard Edie say, flat, that the fire she'd set upstairs on her way past wasn't going to wait on hours, that the building was already deciding for all four of us how long we had left inside it. Maria didn't answer that out loud. She got an arm under me instead, and a shoulder, and told me, in the voice she used for ruling on what was real, that I was heavier than I had any right to be and to stop making this harder than it needed to be. From somewhere a long way outside my own body by then, I understood exactly which choice she'd just made, and exactly what it would cost me that she hadn't made the other one.

I don't have much of the stair after that, or the anteroom, or the door. Fire makes its own weather in a building that size — heat where there shouldn't be any yet, a wrongness in the air that had nothing to do with whatever it is I see when nobody else does and everything to do with ordinary smoke finding ordinary lungs. I remember the specific cold of the yard more than I remember crossing the floor to reach it: smoke-cold air meeting a cut I hadn't known was open across my scalp, startling in how clean it felt against how little else did. I have the gaslamp haze over Vantry sitting low and lit orange from underneath for the first time since we'd come to this city, and Maria's arms not loosening even once we were clear of the worst of the heat, as if she thought the fire might follow us out personally to finish what it hadn't.

Vail was waiting in that yard. It should have been impossible. It wasn't, and she stood there anyway, in the cold, in no hurry, and she didn't run at us and she didn't call for anyone and she didn't raise her voice above the one she'd have used to ask after a headache. She asked Maria, plainly, whether she understood what she had just cost the world — not the building, not the Church, the world, said in the same tone another woman might use for a grocery bill. It was reported in the same spirit she'd have used to tell Maria, years ago, that she'd mislaid a key or left a letter unanswered — a fact passed along for Maria's own good, nothing more — and that was the whole of the horror in it: she still thought she was being kind. She didn't ask about me bleeding in Maria's arms. She didn't look at me twice. Maria didn't answer her. There wasn't an answer built that would have reached her, and I think Maria knew that before the question had finished, because she walked past Vail into the lane without slowing down, carrying me the whole way like I weighed exactly as much as the choice she'd made on the stair and not a pound more.

We got out with the papers. Edie counted them twice before she'd let herself believe it — once in the lane, once three streets further on, under a lamp, out loud, like a woman confirming a delivery. None of what was wrong with me got tended properly that night; there was only the lane, and Edie's voice counting papers, and Maria's hands not letting go of me even once we'd stopped moving. I got out with my skull cracked along the left side, two ribs gone, and the right arm, the ordinary one, the one that had done the gripping all night, broken in three places.
