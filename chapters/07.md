# 7. Wet Work

Four days on the road out of Stillwell and the money Edie had priced the journey against was already thinner than she'd said it would be, so when a rail porter at the Hessel crossing mentioned a landlord two streets down who'd pay in bread and a roof for a night's wet work, she went and asked, and came back, and told us to bring our boots.

The manse had burned somewhere in the middle of the war and nobody had got round to finishing the job. Half its roof was gone to sky. The stone steps down to the cellar were cracked through where the heat had got into them and split them along old fault lines, and green weed had come up soft and slick in the cracks, the kind that smells of standing water even before you smell the standing water. Maria went down first because she always did, one hand out to the wall, boots sliding on the moss until she caught the brick. Edie came behind her with the gear sack over one shoulder, head canted so her good ear faced the street. I came last. Habit, not rank — I checked what was behind us because somebody should, and it wasn't going to be Maria's job and Edie only checks what she can put a number to.

Nothing behind us. Just the crossing, and the porter's shape retreating toward better-lit work.

The cellar door had swollen in its frame and Edie had to lean her whole weight into it before it gave, sighing outward on a breath of standing air that had been down there since before any of us had a use for the word war. Eleven of them. I counted because counting is a thing to do with a fact that won't otherwise hold still — the way the cord counted itself out under the salts, the way Edie counts what a job is worth before she says yes to it. Eleven, face-up and face-down both, turning slow in grey water the colour of the sky outside, bumping the timber posts with a sound like a dock in a slow tide. Old dead. War dead, most of them, gone soft at the edges the way a thing goes soft when water's had long enough at it and nobody's come to argue.

Maria stopped at the threshold and got a hand up under her shawl, not for the smell — she's smelled worse and so have I — but because eleven is a number that asks something of you before you're ready to give it. She didn't go in. Edie stepped past her onto the stone ledge that ran the room's rim, above the water, and started walking it like she was pricing floor space, which she was.

"Landlord wants them out and the drain cleared," she said. "Latch is under, on the far wall. Somebody's rigged it so the tide does the flushing, only the tide's not getting in anymore." She didn't say anything about the eleven beyond that they were in the way of the water doing what the water was supposed to do. I don't think that's coldness. I think if you let yourself feel the weight of every dead thing you're paid to move you stop being able to move them, and somebody still has to.

I went in.

The water came up over my knees and then higher, cold enough that it stopped being cold and became something more like a fact pressing on the bone. I peeled the glove off my left hand first, because wet leather takes a week to dry and I only own the one glove worth owning, and put it inside my coat, and left the arm bare where it always ends up bare when there's work that wants both hands. The stink was old brine and older rot, the underneath-smell of a building that used to have people living upstairs who never once thought about what was in the ground below them. I found the latch by feel before I found it by sight — corroded, swollen shut, the kind of stuck that isn't going to answer to pulling. Two of the eleven had drifted in against my hip and stayed there, patient, the way anything patient makes you.

I put my right hand into the wood.

It still isn't a thing I've made peace with, doing it on purpose. In a fight it happens without asking me. Here, in the quiet, I had to decide to let my fingers stop being fingers that push against a surface and become fingers that go through one, and there's a half-second in the deciding where I always expect it not to work this time, and it always works. I felt for the latch mechanism through four inches of swollen oak like feeling through fog, found the pin, and closed my hand — the deciding again, solid this time, the wood suddenly a wall instead of a fog — and pulled. The latch let go all at once, wet iron shrieking once and then nothing, and somewhere under the floor a valve further down took the weight of moving water and started to earn its keep.

Edie was still working the ledge above me, head up at the joists, not looking my way. "Joists are sound," she said, mostly to herself, mostly to note it down later. "Rot's surface only. Whoever built over a flooded cellar at least knew to season the timber first."

The second one — the arm that isn't there for anyone but me, that I'm still learning to send anywhere useful — had drifted into the wall behind her while I waited on the drain to finish thinking about it, the way it goes when I'm not minding it closely, feeling for whatever the ordinary dark won't show a person. It found something. Not the latch. A shape set upright inside the stud bay behind the ledge she was standing on, cold and round and long, the kind of thing that shouldn't be there and shouldn't be a thing that arm is allowed to touch if it's only ever the eldritch it gets and nothing solid — except it closed around my grip like iron closes, and when I drew it back through the lath, careful, no mark left in the plaster, it came into my other, ordinary hand solid: three foot of scored rod, oiled once and a long time ago, with a weight to it that a thing made only of my own seeing has no business having.

Edie didn't look at it. She didn't look at the wall it had come out of, or at me standing in cold water up to my thighs holding a yard of pipe that hadn't existed as far as she was concerned two minutes before. She said the joists were good for the winter if the eaves held, and went on to the next span like the room hadn't changed under her hands at all.

I have turned this over more times than I'll say out loud to either of them. Her whole trade is knowing what's about to give. She reads a room the way I used to read a man's shoulders before a fight, and she is right more often than anyone has a right to be. If there'd been a threat in that iron — if it had been rigged to drop, or swing, or take a hand off — some part of her ought to have twitched at it the way she twitches at things half a second before they happen to her. Nothing twitched. Either there was never any violence in a length of dead pipe holding still in a wall, which is the plain explanation and probably the true one, or it was never there for her to miss, and I was the only one in the room doing any work that mattered. I don't get to know which. I've stopped expecting to. I set the rod down behind the woodbox upstairs where the shadow's deepest and told myself it was because a stranger's cellar isn't the place to be found carrying scrap iron, and that was true too, just not the whole of it.

The landlord met us at the top of the interior stair and didn't come down it. Big man once, going to fat and grey the way men do when the fat outlasts the work that built the muscle under it. He had a sack of bread that had been good three days ago and a blanket the colour wool goes when it's been washed in the same water too many times, and he put both down on the top step with his boot rather than his hand, which told me what he thought of what had been swimming in his cellar and maybe what he thought of anyone willing to wade in after it. "Upstairs is yours till light," he said. "Don't touch the second floor, it isn't safe." Then the door, and the bar dropping into its cradle above us, definite as a full stop.

Edie carried the bread. Nobody thanked him and he hadn't wanted thanks, only the job done and the doing of it kept where he didn't have to watch it.

The room he'd given us had one window boarded and one that wasn't, a stove with just enough coal left in the scuttle to argue with the cold rather than beat it, and a woman already sitting there on an upturned apple crate like she'd been waiting for company or had simply run out of anywhere else to be. She had the look of somebody who does the sums on every hour before she spends it. Her knuckles were scrubbed raw, the coal ground into the creases past what soap does anything about, and when she talked it was prices, straight off, like she'd been asked already and was only getting round to answering.

"Two pence the ton, shifting slack off the coal barges down the wharf. Four pence to hang a pair of shutters, if you've your own tools, three if you're borrowing mine. Bread's up again — three farthings since Tuesday, so don't let anyone tell you flour's holding." She said it without looking up, the way you'd recite something you'd stopped being angry about a long time ago because the anger never once changed the number.

Edie sat across from her and took the ledger out of the gear sack, the oilcloth one gone soft and dark at the corners from years of her thumb on it, and started writing figures down like the woman was a market she meant to come back to. Maria sat nearer the stove and let her shawl fall open to the heat and asked the woman's name, and got it, and used it twice in the next sentence, which is a thing Maria does that I've never once managed and have stopped trying to. I sat by the door because that's where I sit, and listened to two pence and four pence and three farthings stack up into a life, and thought that nobody in this room was doing anything wrong and nobody in this room was doing anything that added up to enough, and that both of those were true at once and neither one was going to change by my noticing it.

It was Maria who went out when the shout came from the alley behind the bakery next door — a boy, and a sound under the shout that was worse than the shout. A dray had backed a stack of squared timber into him and taken his hand out from under it wrong. I got there behind her. Twelve years old, maybe less, grey in the face already with the shock arriving ahead of the pain, his right hand flat in the dirt with the bones under it sitting at angles bones don't sit at.

Maria went down on her knees in front of him without a word about the mud on her skirt, which she'd have minded on any ordinary day, and put her bare hand straight over the break, and stayed there.

I've watched her do this before. It doesn't get easier to watch, and it isn't quick, whatever people want to believe about it after. She holds a break the way you'd hold a splint in place against a current trying to shift it, minute on minute, her eyes open on the dirt the whole time, not on his face, not on the hand — somewhere between the two, the way you look at nothing at all when the looking has to go somewhere and can't go anywhere useful. A thin grey breath kept coming up off her palm the whole while, and under it, slow as anything real ever mends, the bones found something closer to where they belonged and the split skin over them drew itself shut, inch by inch, with a sound too quiet for what it was doing. By the time she was done the light in the alley had moved a hand's width along the wall and she was grey herself, swaying slightly on her knees the way a candle sways before it decides which way to fall. I got a hand under her arm before she went all the way over. She let me, which told me more about what it had cost her than anything she'd have said if I'd asked.

The boy took his hand back the moment she let him have it, stared at the skin gone dark and scarred-over where minutes before there'd been open bone, and scrambled backward on his heels until the bakery wall stopped him.

"Witch," he said. Not a question. An accusation, or a warning to himself, or both.

Maria didn't correct him. She wiped her palm on her skirt, the same skirt she'd already muddied kneeling for him, and gave a short nod, the kind you give when someone's got a fact half wrong and it isn't worth the trouble of the other half. She used to wear a collar that told people what to call her instead. I've never seen it on her, not once since Stillwell, and I don't think she misses the answering it gave her more than she's said. Out here she's just a woman with a warm hand and no name for what she is, and the boy had picked the only word he had for that, and she let him keep it. I understood, without either of us saying so, that it cost her something to let it stand and cost her something worse to correct it, and that she'd chosen the cheaper of the two without needing to work out the sum the way Edie would have.

Edie had come out behind me and stood at the alley mouth with her hands flat against her thighs, watching the street both ways at once out of habit rather than need, and said nothing about any of it, which from her is its own kind of blessing.

We went back up to the cold room and the coal that wasn't quite winning and ate what the landlord's bread was still good for. The woman on the crate had gone to wherever she went at night. Maria slept sitting against the wall with her knees drawn up, the way she does when the day's asked more of her than she'll admit to in words. Edie took the first watch without being asked to, because somebody keeping a ledger of a room always ends up keeping the room as well.

I lay by the door with the length of iron behind the woodbox eight feet from my head, real or not real, and did not decide which, and the night came down slow over the burnt manse, the water still lapping at the cellar walls beneath us, patient, working at the dark the way it works at everything, given enough time and no one to tell it to stop.
