# 6. Overground

I stepped out of Stillwell onto the cobbled street and the sky was a harsh white, no sun in it anywhere, just flat light coming off the water from every direction at once. My boots hit the wet stone. The air was cold, carrying the salt tang of the drains and the burnt coal smoke from the lower districts, and the weight of my coat hung heavier than it should have, still damp through from the steam rooms below. My fingers didn't want to close all the way inside my gloves. My right arm swung at my side, the joint stiff and awkward where the elbow had been broken and put back together wrong, and I checked the balance of it out of habit, the way the weight hung from the shoulder, making sure it wouldn't catch, before I lifted my hand to pull my collar higher against the drift coming off the river.

I don't think about the chapel often. I've taught myself not to, same as I've taught myself most of what keeps me passing for ordinary. But my hand goes to the balance-check without my deciding to, every time, and some mornings that's as far as it goes and some mornings it isn't. This was one of the mornings it wasn't.

She closed a wound. I'll say that much plainly, because it's true and because it's the part I don't doubt: Maria put her hands on four inches of open stump and the bleeding stopped and the shock didn't take me, and a woman who can do that for a stranger in a dark room has done something real, whatever else is true alongside it. What I don't say to her, what I've never said to anyone, is that I felt the rest of it happen from the inside, and it didn't feel like closing. It felt like something arriving. Not grown. Delivered — the way the second arm arrived later at the chalice, already shaped, already knowing what it was for, except this one I could see with my own eyes finish itself under her palms, fingers coming up out of nothing the way frost comes up on a window, there and then there more. She thinks she grew me an arm. I've turned it over enough nights now to be fairly sure she didn't, that she closed what was open and something underneath the Salts did the rest of it, the same place the second one came from, waiting in that stump before she ever touched it. I don't know what that makes the arm I've been shaking hands with people on. I know I'm not going to ask her and find out whether she can tell the difference between what she did and what she thinks she did. Some mercies you don't correct.

My Insight pricks worse in daylight than it ever did below. Down in the Salts a man expects the walls to lie to him. Up here the city puts a clean coat over everything and dares you to call it a lie, and that's the part that gets under my skin and stays there. The buildings along the street looked solid enough — brick fronts, glass in the shop windows, paint that hadn't had time to peel — but behind the plaster I could see the damp courses rotting through the foundation timbers same as they rot everywhere else, and the stone sills were pitted where the acid runoff had been eating at them for longer than the paint had been lying about it. The people walking past us on the flags looked busy and clean in their woolen coats, and underneath that I could see the grey slackness sitting on their skin, the hunched set of shoulders in coats cut for a warmer winter than this one, moving like clockwork toys with the spring run down to where it only just carries them the length of the street. It's the same wherever I let myself look long enough. The wood under the varnish goes soft. The mortar goes to sand between the bricks. Nobody painted over it meaning to lie. They painted over it because paint is cheaper than timber, and the city's whole trick is that those two facts look identical from the street.

There was a rail guard down by the tracks, standing easy with one hand near his belt, and he was throwing three shadows instead of two. They came off his boots at angles that didn't agree with each other or with the flat white light that should have pinned a man to one shadow and left it at that. A dog walked at his heel that wasn't a dog — iron-ribbed, brass where its eyes should have been, its paws striking the stone with a dry, even click that had nothing to do with breath or weight. Neither of them did anything. That's most of what I see, if I'm honest about it: things standing still, not doing anything, just wrong in a way nobody else in the street seemed able to afford to notice.

Maria put her hand on the guard's sleeve. Once, lightly, and when he didn't look down she did it again in the same spot, like she was checking the wool was really there under her fingers and not just agreeing to be touched. He never looked at her. His eyes stayed on the empty rail line running north toward the yards, and she let her hand drop and pulled her shawl tighter instead, and didn't say anything else to him.

"He's not going to answer," I said.

"I wasn't asking him anything, Lloyd." She kept her thumb working at the worn seam of her sleeve, not looking back at me.

"He doesn't have anything to give you. None of them do."

"They have whatever they're standing there with." Her voice had gone small and dry, the way it does when she's spent too long in the damp. "Somebody has to look at them while they do it."

I didn't have an answer for that, so I let it sit, same as I let most of what she says sit when it's not really asking for one back.

We stood a minute by the curb with the carts going past on iron wheels, and I understood without anybody saying it that we'd come to the part of the road where the three of us stopped being an arrangement and started being a question. Maria had no parish now and no room waiting anywhere and nothing under her arm but the one bundle she'd carried the whole way down, tied in a knot she hadn't needed to retie since Stillwell. Edie had her eyes on the grey line of the horizon where the tracks ran out east toward the higher ground, and didn't say why that mattered to her, and didn't need to — her deaf ear was turned toward the noise of the drays so the good one could keep listening to whatever she was listening for out past the rooftops. I straightened my collar and said I was going home, to get my mother out before the passes closed for the winter.

"She's still in Callow Ford?" Maria asked.

"She's where she was," I said.

"And if she won't come?"

"She'll come. The roof's gone down half an inch since the thaw. You can see the light through the north gable."

Edie didn't turn from the horizon. "The bridge at the gorge is out," she said, flat as two stones sliding against each other. "Company engineers didn't shore the footings before the frost hit. You'll want the low track past the lime kilns."

"I know the track."

"It's flooded." Maria again, looking up from her boots, her face pinched pale at the edges from the cold. "A cart went into the ditch at the third weir last week. The horses didn't come up."

"They're horses. They don't care about the water once they're in it."

"The driver cared."

"He shouldn't have been hauling wet timber in October," I said, and that was as much sympathy as I had in me for a man I'd never meet.

Edie pulled a bound ledger from her coat, its cover gone soft and brown at the corners, and ran her thumb down a column of figures with the same flat attention she gives everything that costs money. She totted the rail fare, the coal allowance, the freight on whatever iron she was carrying, made one short tick mark at the bottom of the page in pencil, and closed the cover without a sound that carried past the three of us.

"That's for the rail," she said, and pressed a small purse into my palm — boiled leather, a hempen drawstring, heavier with copper and silver than it looked like it should be. "The rest comes when the shoring holds."

"And if it doesn't?"

"It holds." She said it the way she says most things, like the saying of it had already settled the matter.

She folded a second thing into her coat before I could ask what it was — a sheet of heavy parchment, yellowed at the creases, marked up in red ink with cross-sections and load lines, the plan of whatever work was waiting for her at the junction. She squared it against her thigh and put it away next to her spare pencils, unhurried, the way she does everything since the tunnels, like the next job is already half done simply by being filed in the right pocket.

Nobody asked whether the rest of us were coming. We just went, the three of us together down the main road, past the brick wall of the freight depot, the guard's three mismatched shadows twisting long and short across the uneven cobbles as we passed, reaching for our boots and snapping back to his heels a second later like they'd only been borrowed. The iron dog followed ten paces back, matching our pace step for step down the slope and up again without a tongue loll or a hitch in its breath, because it didn't have either to spare.

My Insight settled the longer we walked, not easing off so much as confirming itself — the ache behind my eyes dropping from a sharp throb to a flat, steady hum, the kind that isn't asking you to look anymore because it's already sure of what you'll see. Bright signs swinging smooth on their brackets. Girls laughing in a milliner's window at something down the lane. And underneath all of it the same soft, fungused wood, the same water out of the standpipes tasting of old iron and worse things from the upper reservoirs, the whole street held together with spit and fresh lime over a frame that wouldn't bear my shoulder if I leaned on it. Nobody on that road had the time to spare for three strangers walking north with their heads down, which suited me. We vanished down the road, the extra shadow of the guard stretching ahead of us like a dark omen.
