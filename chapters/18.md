# 18. The Rumours

Edie came up the back stair two days after Lloyd first stood on his own and crossed the room without the wall. She had been counting the same days I had. Neither of us said so.

She set her bag down flat against the door, within reach of her own hand and nobody else's, same as she always did, and said she needed the register before noon, because the man setting the type at the Vantry broadsheet would hold the page open for her and for no one else, and not for very long even then.

There was only the one register left to give her. The case files had gone out of this room in her coat weeks before, the stud book with them, and I had not asked what became of either. Asking was its own kind of hoping, and I had decided somewhere around the third week of his dressings that I could not keep spending that particular coin. So I got up, and knelt at the board under the window, and worked it loose with the kitchen knife. There it was, wrapped in a shirt of Lloyd's I had torn for bandages and never finished tearing. I had read Anse Duley's name in it most nights for six weeks. I had not decided, until Edie's hand was actually out for it, whether I could put it in that hand.

I put it in that hand.

"They'll print it whole," Edie said. "Photographed, not set. Every page, so nobody can say a compositor changed a word." She did not tell me it would be worth it. She never said that sort of thing, true or false, and she did not ask if I was sure, because she had already watched me decide. She put the register into her bag exactly as she had put the case files into her coat weeks before — no ceremony, the motion of a woman finished with a tool for the day — and I took the plainness of it as the only mercy going round the room, because it was the only one on offer.

She left before the bells. Lloyd, over by the window, said nothing about any of it. From him, silence that size meant something had landed and he hadn't decided yet what to do with it. He went on working his right hand open and shut around the chair rail, slow, the same motion the army surgeons at Sennet set the old soldiers to. Nobody had taught it to him. I had not had the courage to ask where he'd learned it on his own.

"You didn't have to give her that one," he said, eventually, not looking round.

"I know."

"Didn't ask if you wanted to keep it."

"I know that too." I put the board back over the gap, and it sat wrong, a half-inch high at one corner. The space had been cut to the exact size of what used to fill it, and nothing else fit a hole that particular shape. I left it crooked. There did not seem to be a reason to fix it.

---

It went out on a Tuesday. By Thursday the newsboy on the corner was calling it instead of the fire at the rail yard, his voice gone hoarse with the size of what he was selling, and by the following week Lloyd was walking three streets each morning to buy every paper the stall carried and walking home slower than he'd walked out, because three streets was further than he should have gone and he would not say so and I would not tell him I'd counted his steps from the window.

The landlady brought up her own copy on the Friday, unasked, and stood in the doorway turning it over in her hands as if the paper itself might be hot, and asked only whether we'd known people there. I said we'd known the building. She did not ask anything further. In that district, not asking was its own form of courtesy. She left two candles on the sill that she did not charge us for and never mentioned again. The room filled up with newsprint exactly as it had filled, those first weeks, with soiled linen — folded stacks of it along the skirting board, under the washstand, wherever there was a dry corner Lloyd hadn't already claimed for his hand exercises. I read every page he brought back. I told myself I was reading for Ferrick's wording, for whether the sub-panel would hold its nerve past the second week, and some of that was true.

One thing became the only thing in the entire Commonwealth, and I had never watched it happen that fast or that completely before. The ethics sub-panel's report ran across two columns under Ferrick's own name, dry as a ledger and twice as damning for the dryness. The Vantry broadsheet ran the register as plates rather than type, Anse Duley's name legible in a hand I would have known with my eyes shut, and beneath it a column drawn from the case files — sixty years of names, each with a folder number and a dated result, set out the length of a casualty list, because that was precisely what it was. For eleven days it was the only story in the Commonwealth. People I will never meet argued over tanks and intake and the word *ascension* at breakfast tables I will never sit at, and for eleven days I let myself believe this was the whole cost of the papers, and that it would hold at that.

Lloyd read the worst of it to me once, flat, no weight put on any word he didn't need to lean on. When he reached the paragraph about the stud book he stopped, folded the paper along its own crease, and said that was enough reading for one day. He did not pick it back up. I did not ask what he'd have done with the rest of it if he had. I already knew, had known since the siege, and knowing bought me nothing. I set the question down the day it arrived, same as I set down anything I can't carry on a given afternoon. That doesn't mean the carrying stops. It only changes hands for a while.

---

On the twelfth day the Sanctum answered, and it answered well, and I sat on the edge of the bed with the paper trembling faintly in my own hands and thought, with no pride anywhere in the thought, that I could have written every line of it myself.

Ascension House, the statement said, had operated outside the knowledge and the sanction of the wider Church: a rogue chapter, zealous past correction, now closed by order from Carrowgate. Two under-priors were surrendered to the civil courts inside the week, named, photographed going in, their faces already halfway to case files of their own. A bishop I had met twice and disliked both times wept in the Senate gallery — genuinely, I thought, watching the print of his face in three different papers, because Vail had taught me early that the grief does not have to be false to be used. The Church announced a programme of restitution for the families of Ascension House's registered dead, a figure so large and so exact that no paper printed a single word questioning the sum, because a number with that many digits reads as an apology on its own, whatever it is actually paying for.

Lloyd read the bishop's testimony over my shoulder, the one paragraph about the sleepless nights, and said he'd cried harder than that once over a dog he hadn't even liked that much, and he'd been nine. It was the first thing he'd said that made me laugh since Vantry. I let myself have it. There did not seem to be a great deal else on offer that week.

I had sat in rooms and watched Vail perform smaller versions of all three moves. Grief offered first, so there is nothing left over to accuse. Blame narrowed down to the two names furthest from the top of anything. A figure large enough that disputing it looks like disputing charity itself. I had thought, at seven and at seventeen and at twenty-two, that I was watching a tired woman manage her own grief in public. I understood now, reading the Senate transcript on a borrowed bed in a mill district, that I had been watching her train for exactly this, in front of me, the whole time, and calling it mothering because it was also that.

By the fourteenth day the story had stopped being about the ward at all. It had become a story about the papers instead. A churchman holding a doctorate in something nobody could quite name argued in print that a register reproduced as a photograph, rather than set in type by an honest printer, was on its face suspect. Another wondered, in the same column, whether a woman who had once admittedly emptied a poor box in her own youth could be trusted as to where old paper came from. He had not read the register. I would have staked the room on it. He had been handed one line about me by somebody who wanted that line handed around, and he had built an entire column on it, a wall raised on a single brick nobody had thought to check for cracks.

Edie told me the rest of it standing in the doorway with her coat still buttoned, in the same flat voice she used to tell me the bandage stock was running low, because that was the only voice she owned and I had come, by then, to be grateful there was one voice in the room that never changed shape to fit the news. Nobody senior was charged. Nobody above the rank of under-prior ever answered, in any court, for a single tank.

By the third week the newsboy on the corner was calling the rail-yard fire again, a second one, in a different yard, and I understood that the story had finished costing anyone anything further. It had simply become one more thing that had happened, filed under history by people who had not had to carry any of it home — the same filing the Attrition itself had gone into, four years reduced to a date on a plaque nobody read twice. Ferrick's name came up once more in a short paragraph praising his diligence, and then not again. The register plates, the case files, sixty years of names: all of it went into the same bound volume of back issues the mill-district library kept behind the counter, to be asked for by date. Almost no one ever asked.

---

The letter came inside Edie's bag, folded under a square of newspaper, three weeks after the register had left my hands for good. Edie set it on the table without a word. From her, that silence was its own kind of warning. She left again before I had decided whether I was going to open it at all.

It was Vail's hand. I would have known it blind — the same even slope she had set my own letters by, thirty years practised into something that had become kindness because at some point, long before I was old enough to watch it happen, it simply had. She had been transferred to the Sanctum at Carrowgate, she wrote, and promoted besides, to oversee intake across the three almshouses there. She gave me the news plainly, in the same sentence as a remark about the early frost, because hiding anything from me had never once in her life occurred to her as an available choice. She hoped the room I was keeping was warm enough for the season. She hoped Lloyd's arm was mending as straight as such a break allowed. She said she thought of me daily, and I believed her. I had never once caught her in a lie, in twenty-one years, and saw no reason tonight to go looking for the first one. She closed by asking, only, how my hands were.

There was no threat folded anywhere inside it. I read it four times hunting for one and did not find it. I had known I would not find it before I started looking, and somehow the knowing made the search worse, not shorter.

I did not write back. I did not burn it. I folded it along the crease she had already made putting it into the envelope, and I put it under the floorboard where the register used to lie, in the space its absence had left exactly the right size to hold.
