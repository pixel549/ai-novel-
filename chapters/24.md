# 24. The Ledger and the Killing

Edie came up the chandler's stairs without knocking first. In four months she had never once failed to knock, not even the morning she'd come to tell us Vantry's registers had made the Senate floor and not the front page, and so I knew what she was carrying before I saw it, much as you'd know a letter is bad post before you'd broken the seal, from the weight of the hand holding it rather than anything printed on the outside.

She set a folded sheet on our table, between the bread and the small stacks of Lloyd's day-wages still sitting from the night before, and turned it so the marks faced us both. Three doors. Three neat crosses, pencilled light enough to lift with a thumb if anyone had thought to try. Nobody had. Nobody but Edie had any reason to look at that particular plan twice.

"Mine," she said. "Kept in a drawer at the townhouse, marked up by my own hand and nobody else's, for two years. I put crosses on doors I've already decided are weak, so I remember to see to them. These three I hadn't got to yet." She didn't ask whose work the new marks were. She'd have had the answer before she finished climbing the stairs. "Somebody else had already decided they were the ones worth marking."

Lloyd didn't reach for the sheet. He looked at it as a man looks at a debt called in early — not surprised that it's owed, only that it's today — and said, "Those are mine," and nothing after it, because he has never once in the time I've known him tried to talk his way around a true thing once it was out on a table between people who mattered to him.

---

She made us both sit. She didn't sit herself, not at first — stood at the window with her back to the street, the ledger-keeper's stillness she gets when a column won't balance and she means to find out why before she moves again — and when she finally spoke it came out level and complete, exactly as she delivers everything she's already decided is true and doesn't intend to revise.

"Nine years ago I served under a colonel named Absolom Reyne, Fourth Tunnelling. He sealed a breach with ninety-one of his own sappers still inside it, to save the hour it would have cost to pull them clear first, and an inquiry that mostly believes colonels believed this one. Nine of us got out through a second heading nobody official knew was there. I was one of the nine. Eleven weeks later I found Reyne alone in a billet outside Carrowgate and I killed him, and no one has ever once suspected me of it, and I have never told either of you this before tonight."

Never, in all the time I'd known her, had she put together more than four sentences about anything that wasn't load-bearing or shoring. I did not move, and beside me Lloyd didn't either, and the quiet she left afterward was not the kind either of us was going to be the one to fill.

"It was murder," she said. "I've never once let myself call it anything softer, because the day I do is the day it stops meaning what it costs me to still be holding a line nine years later that most people never bother to hold at all — about what's owed, and what's taken, and what you're allowed to call even. If there's no line, Reyne and I are the same man, and ninety-one boys bought nothing at all, not even one bright thing on the other side of it to weigh against the dark."

She let that sit exactly as long as it needed to and no longer. "I'm telling you this so you understand precisely what kind of person is about to decide what happens next, and so neither of you mistakes anything I do tomorrow for permission I don't have the standing to give. Nobody goes near that townhouse again until I've had the night with it."

She left without another word, the sheet with its three crosses still open on our table, and I understood, watching the door close behind her, that she had told us the one fact she had spent nine years refusing anyone, and had done it in under two minutes, and had not once asked either of us to forgive her for any part of it.

---

Lloyd didn't speak again that night, and I didn't make him. We sat by the window with the lamp turned low enough to save the oil, not because either of us believed Edie could be seen coming from three streets off, but because sitting in the dark felt like something to do with our hands that wasn't picking the floor plan back up. Once, near midnight, he reached for his day-wages and started his stacking — a coin, another coin, a small column rising and falling under his fingers without his seeming to notice he'd begun — and I watched him do it for a long time and still did not ask him what the counting was for. There are things I have decided not to need explained. I thought, for a moment, of saying something about the two of us keeping the same hours as a pair of night watchmen with nothing worth guarding, and didn't, because the joke needed more air in the room than either of us had left to spare that night.

I did not sleep. I lay listening for a step on the chandler's stairs that would tell me, before any words did, which way the night had gone, and none came, and the not-coming was its own kind of answer, stretched out over hours I had no way to shorten.

---

She came back at first light, before the street below had properly started, and she had not slept either — I could tell from the particular flatness in her, the one degree past her ordinary flatness that she wears when something has cost her and she has decided not to spend any more of herself discussing what it was.

"I spent the night trying to build a version where clean hands come out ahead," she said, "and I couldn't do it, so I'm done trying." She set her ledger on the table, closed, and did not open it. "Vantry was as pure a good as either of you has ever done in your life. It burned a building to the ground with the worst thing in this country still inside it, and the programme that building served is running from a second address tonight, exactly as it was before you ever walked in. Ferrick is the purest man I have ever worked for. He has produced two excellent reports and convened four sessions and moved nothing. I have spent three years guarding a man whose decency is the exact reason nothing he decides matters to anyone outside that room." She looked at Lloyd, not unkindly, and not kindly either. "I'm not helping you because I've decided you're right. I'm helping because I've run out of a version where being right about anything changes what happens to the water."

She laid out her conditions flatly, exactly as she lays out anything she's costed in advance. Nobody else in that room was to be hurt, under any circumstance, whatever it cost either of them to manage it. It happened where her own people could be the ones who found the body first, not someone further down the chain who'd ask harder questions. Crane had stood up in open session six weeks before and called the Vantry inquiry a debt the Senate owed and hadn't paid, on the record, publicly, more than Ferrick had ever once managed, and Edie meant to leave a torn scrap of vestment thread caught on the window latch — nothing any barrister could make stand up under oath, only enough to put the obvious question in whoever found him first, before that person thought to ask a harder one. And it happened today, because the session was today regardless of anything either of us wanted, and a plan that waited for a better day was a plan that waited forever.

"You'll go in with the kitchen staff," she told Lloyd. "I'm short two men for the afternoon sitting, which happens to be true, because I made it true last week against exactly this. Maria goes in the front, as Ferrick's guest, because he already likes her and a guest who's liked draws nobody's attention away from the door." She looked at me last. "He'll ask after you. Let him. It's the one part of today that costs you nothing to give him."

---

The townhouse was smaller inside than its brass plate suggested a building housing the Commonwealth's business had any right to be — a converted private house still, at the bones of it, with a staircase built for a family rather than a government, and clerks moving along it with the particular quiet of people who have learned that the rooms above them do not like to be reminded how little gets decided inside them. Ferrick met me himself in the hall, delighted in the plain, undisguised way he is delighted by everything that costs him nothing to be glad about, and asked after my hands before he'd finished shaking the one I'd offered him.

"You must meet Crane," he said, already turning, already certain I'd want to. "Difficult man to like on paper. Impossible not to, in a room."

Halvard Crane was silver through all of it, not just the temples, with a soft, unhurried handshake and small round spectacles he took off to polish on a handkerchief while he spoke, as though cleaning them were simply part of greeting a stranger properly. He asked my name twice, the second time to be sure he had it right rather than because he'd forgotten, and said, when Ferrick mentioned I'd come up from the valley, that he hoped the works were treating my family decently, with no more weight on it than a man asking after weather he genuinely hoped was fine. I said they were. It was the only lie I told anyone that whole morning, and it came easier than I would have liked it to.

I saw Lloyd again only at the recess. He came along the service passage off the main hall with a coal bucket in his off hand, in a borrowed apron two sizes too large through the shoulders, and nobody in that corridor looked at him twice, because nobody looks twice at a man carrying coal.

---

Crane found me in that same passage a few minutes later, stepping out, he said, for air that didn't have quite so much paper in it, and he looked at me — really looked, as a host looks at a guest he's worried has gone quiet at the far end of a room — and asked whether I was quite well, because I'd gone very pale just now, hadn't I, and wouldn't I sit down a moment.

Lloyd set the bucket down beside the grate and came up on Crane's other side as though to steady him toward a chair that wasn't there, and his hand found the front of Crane's waistcoat flat and unhurried, exactly as a valet settles a coat before a meeting, and for three or four full seconds nothing in it frightened anyone, including Crane, who glanced down at where it rested with no more alarm than a man checking whether a button had come undone without his noticing.

Then something moved across his face that had nothing to do with pain yet — more the look of a man reaching for a word he knows perfectly well and finding, for one strange moment, that it isn't where he left it. "Forgive me," he said, mild, apologetic, the host apologizing for his own body's bad manners at his own gathering. "I seem to have —" He didn't finish it. His eyes went past Lloyd's shoulder to me instead, and whatever was happening to him, the whole of what was left of his attention went to the fact that I had gone whiter still, standing there watching him. "Is the young lady quite all right?" he asked, of no one in particular, courteous to the very end of himself, confused and asking after somebody else's safety instead of his own, exactly as a voice with no face on it once had, in a dark that belonged to neither of us. Then his knees went, unhurried, like a man finally agreeing to sit down after being asked one time too many, and Lloyd's hand came away from him with nothing on it at all, no mark on the waistcoat, no tear in the cloth, nothing a coroner would ever be able to point to and name.

Ferrick, three steps off, stood with his hand still half raised from some sentence he'd been in the middle of, and did not move, and did not make a sound, for exactly as long as it took me to understand that he had seen the whole of it and would carry that seeing for the rest of his life without ever once being able to say what he'd actually watched happen.

---

The first shout came from somewhere behind us, not from Ferrick at all but from a clerk who'd come round the passage corner with an armful of papers and dropped the lot of them at the sight of a senator on the floor, and after that the house filled up fast with the particular noise of people who have never once in their lives had to move quickly discovering, badly, how.

We didn't make the front door. Edie's own people were doing exactly what she'd trained them to do. They converged on the one exit a frightened crowd was already running for, and that correctness — hers, built over two years for a day she'd never meant to use it against — put us instead on the service stair with four faces already turned toward Lloyd's, four that would be able to describe him to somebody later if anybody troubled to ask them to. I did the sum on that without deciding to, same as Edie does, and did not share it with him.

A hand caught Lloyd's bad shoulder on the second landing and the stair took the rest of it out of my hands entirely — his feet going, the wrenching crack of something in the arm that had never properly healed since Vantry giving way a second time, and him down on the turn of the stair with nothing left in that arm to catch himself with and a corridor of clerks closing from above with no better idea between them than to grab whoever was nearest and hold on.

I had nothing in reach that was fire, and nothing that was lightning, and no five minutes to wait for either to come back to me even if I had. What I had was light, the cheapest thing I own and the only thing I had left, and I put all of it into that corridor at once with no more aim than throwing a bucket of water at a wall — crude, blinding, close enough to several of them that somebody screamed from the brightness of it rather than any hurt in it — and bought the two of us ninety seconds in a stairwell that had, until that exact moment, no reason on earth to think the Church of the Mercies had anything to do with a dying senator.

Nobody chased us past the alley.

---

We walked the first six streets, because running is the one thing that gets noticed on a street that hasn't yet heard anything is wrong, and nobody we passed looked at either of us twice. Lloyd didn't speak the whole way. His silence told me more than any amount of words would have, and when we finally reached the chandler's stairs he went up them one-armed, favouring the bad side so carefully that I understood, before he said a word, exactly how bad it was.

At the top he sat down on the floor rather than cross to the chair, because the chair was four more steps than the arm had left in it, and said only, "Gone again," and let me get down beside him to look.

The bread was still on the table where Edie had set her sheet down the night before, the three crosses facing up at nobody.
