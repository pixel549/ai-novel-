The narrator, Maria, watches from a remove as grief over Crane's death gives Ferrick the votes to do immediately what Crane would not — bringing the works-water schedule forward by years, testing the water, and capping wells before the first frost — while the Church, blamed for a murder it did not commit, loses the one spring hearing the real Vantry evidence could never win on its own. She visits Ferrick's house and watches him thank Edie, warmly and sincerely, for keeping his hands clean through the worst month of his life; Edie lets him believe it, having traded her old insistence on a moral line for simply counting what an action produces, and leaves her old knot-cord openly at her wrist rather than hiding it, which Maria reads as the first wholly honest position Edie has held since the tunnel. Barred from the valley by a likeness on every board, Lloyd sends Maria north alone to Callow Ford carrying his saved wages, where she finds his mother Ivet alive and moved to clean water and chooses, carefully, to tell her nothing true about what her son has become or why he cannot come home. Maria returns to find Lloyd unable to trust that the room around him is real until she names its objects aloud one by one, and the book ends on her hand closed over his and the plain, repeated fact of her presence rather than any statement of what it means.
