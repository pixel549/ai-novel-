# 1. Stillwell Hydro

The iron tires of the coach ground against wet river gravel, then shuddered to a stop where the track ended at the timber staging. I stepped off the iron footboard onto the slick pier. Tide was out, three feet of black mud exposed beneath the pilings. The timber deck was six-inch pitch pine, notched into greenheart posts and secured with square-headed iron bolts. Half the bolt heads had scaled over with heavy rust from the estuary salt, the iron bleeding dark orange streaks down the grain. Rain was held up in the low clouds over the water, but the river mist sat heavy on the wood, making every footing slick under boot rubber.

My left ear registered nothing—the dead side remained flat and empty, as it had since the camouflet at the third sap—but through my right ear and the soles of my boots I caught the low, steady thrum of working machinery somewhere inside the stone masonry ahead.

I walked down the length of the pier toward the hydro's stone entrance. The building was three stories of ashlar granite set into the embankment wall, built heavy to hold the weight of the water tanks on the upper roof deck. The mortar joints were four-inch coursing, weathered back a quarter-inch from the margin line where the damp had worked into the lime. Plaster had been laid over the stonework around the arched doorway to smarten it for callers, but the salt damp had blown it off in patches the size of spade heads. Beneath the peeling render, long strips of iodine-stained cloth hung from rusty iron eyelets driven into the masonry gaps. The rags were stiffened with dried salt and grey chemical residue from the exhaust vents above, slapping flat against the stone whenever the river breeze came off the channel.

The outer door was three-inch oak, hung on heavy strap hinges pinned into the granite casing with lead plugs. I pushed it open. The hinge pins lacked grease, grinding on iron scale as the door swung inward.

Inside, the hall was six feet wide, flagged with local slate set over river gravel. The air was warm, thick with damp sulfur and the bitter smell of boiled reagents. A partition wall of matched pine boards divided the passage from the main office. Halfway down, an intake window had been cut into the timber, fitted with a copper wire lattice and a narrow deal counter shelf worn smooth by coat sleeves.

Behind the lattice sat a clerk on a tall three-legged stool. He wore a black wool coat grease-stained at the cuffs, a steel nib pen wedged behind his right ear. On the desk lay a thick calfskin ledger, two inches deep, bound with brass corner plates.

I stepped up to the counter. I pulled a creased ledger slip from my inner coat pocket and laid it flat on the deal wood. The parchment bore the blue-ink mark of Senator Ferrick’s estate office and the small, blind-stamped crest at the lower margin.

The clerk did not look at my face. He picked up the slip by the corner, held it toward the oil lamp burning on his desk, and checked the punched code along the upper edge. He did not ask my name, my business, or my authorization. He pulled a iron lever press toward him, slid the parchment into the die, and brought his hand down hard on the handle. The stroke was short and heavy—a sharp *clack* of iron meeting steel. 

He slid the stamped slip into a brass box beneath the desk, opened the calfskin ledger, and dipped a steel pen into a well of dark gall ink. In a clear, practiced clerk’s hand, he recorded thirty-five shillings against Senator Ferrick’s household account under column four. He did not look up while he wrote. The quill scraped three quick strokes, then stopped.

He reached into a wooden tray divided into twelve square compartments. His thick fingers picked up a heavy brass disk, quarter-inch thick and two inches across, stamped with the numeral *1* inside a raised circular border. He dropped the token onto the counter shelf. The metal rang flat against the pine board.

"Run one," he said. His voice was thin, scratched dry from years of steam and sulfur vapor. "Run one takes three patrons at a time. The facility operates in batches of three. No single entries, no pairs. Three bodies to a run keeps the boiler pressure balanced across the manifold and stops the condensation traps from drowning."

"Where?" I asked.

"Down the passage," he said, pointing a finger stained black at the nail with ink and reagent. "Door at the end. Waiting room two. Wait inside until the bell rings."

I picked up the brass disk. It was cold, heavy enough to weigh down the pocket of my coat. I turned and walked down the passage.

The passage ran twenty yards into the embankment wall, the stone arches lower here, overhead joists five-by-ten rough-sawn pine laid on nine-inch centers to carry the brick vaults above. Moisture ran down the mortar lines in clear, slow drops that smelled of burnt lime.

At the end of the passage stood a low oak door with a brass plate marked *Waiting Room 2*. I turned the latch and stepped inside.

The room was twelve feet square, whitewashed over brick. A single oil lamp burned in an iron bracket on the north wall, its flame turned low to save fuel. On the east side sat a long deal workbench, four inches thick, supported on heavy trestles. On the west wall was a plain wooden bench.

Two people were already in the room.

A man leaned his lower back against the edge of the deal table. He was six feet tall, heavy through the shoulders and thick-set, though his waist showed the hollows of a man who missed meals when work was short. He wore a coarse grey wool coat with frayed buttonholes and heavy work boots caked with dry river silt. His hair was dark, cropped short with a knife, and his nose had been broken twice and set crooked, pulling his nostrils off-center. His knuckles were thick, pale, and distorted by old fractures. In his hands he held a twenty-foot coil of three-quarter-inch manila hemp rope. He ran the hemp through his fingers inch by inch, testing the lay for soft spots or rotting fibers with his calloused thumbs.

A woman stood near the wooden bench on the far side. She was small, wiry, built light in the frame, her fair hair cut short behind the ears for work. She wore a dark wool skirt and a brown canvas jacket with the sleeves rolled back to her mid-forearms. Both arms were covered from wrist to elbow in pale, smooth scar tissue—old burns that had healed tight and hairless over the muscle. She had taken three clean flannel blankets from a wooden crate under the bench and was laying them flat across the timber seat, pulling the corners taut and smoothing out the folds with quick, neat sweeps of her palms.

I closed the oak door behind me. The latch fell back into the strike plate with a dry click.

The man stopped running the hemp through his hands. He shifted his weight on the edge of the deal table, his hobnailed boots scraping slightly against the stone floor. He looked across the room at me. His eyes were dark, set deep under a heavy brow line, evaluating my boots, coat, and hands in one flat pass.

"Never been here before?" he asked.

"No," I said.

He nodded once, a brief drop of his chin, and turned his attention back to the rope coil. He gathered the hemp into four neat loops, passed the working end around the middle, and pulled a bight through to lock the coil flat.

The woman by the bench turned. She glanced at my face, then down at my right hand, where the brass token sat pressed between my fingers before I slid it into my coat pocket.

"First run," she said.

Her voice was light, clear, carrying the smooth cadence of the valley towns, though she kept it quiet. She gave a short nod toward the token in my pocket.

"They run the intake valves high on the first pass to clear the moisture out of the feed lines," she said, smoothing down the last corner of the flannel blanket. "The air comes in hot and sharp. Take small breaths through your mouth until your lungs get used to the salt."

"Understood," I said.

She pointed to the far end of the bench. "I left space on the end if you want to set your kit down. I’m Maria."

"Edie," I said.

She did not ask for a second name or a trade, and I did not give one.

A brass bell chimed once from the wall above the passage door—a flat, heavy stroke produced by a spring-loaded pull wire.

A second door on the south wall swung open. An attendant in a thick canvas apron, wet at the knees and stained grey around the hem, stepped into the doorway.

"Run one," the attendant said. "Intake chamber three. Move through."

The heavy man pushed himself off the deal table, hooked his rope coil through his leather belt, and walked toward the south door without speaking. Maria picked up her shoulder bag, stepped around the bench, and followed him. I fell in three paces behind her.

We walked into the intake chamber. The room was eight feet square, built with heavy granite ashlar blocks laid tight without mortar, backed by lead sheeting to keep the steam from driving into the earth behind the wall. The ceiling was low—barely seven feet to the slate flags overhead—and the air was already thick with warm, wet air that smelled of crushed resin and iron scale.

In the center of the room stood the primary control manifold: a five-inch cast-iron riser coming up through a hole in the stone floor, branching into three separate supply lines fitted with brass globe valves and heavy cast iron handwheels.

The heavy man walked straight to the main riser. He did not ask permission or wait for the attendant. He reached out with both hands—his knuckles pale against the dark iron—and gripped the eight-inch wheel of the main stop valve. He threw his weight against it, turning the wheel counter-clockwise. The packing gland was dry, screeching as the bronze stem backed out of the bonnet. He spun the wheel three full turns until the internal gate hit the top of the travel and locked solid.

Maria stepped to the east wall. A gas lighting jet was set into a brass socket on the masonry pier. She pulled a brass match-safe from her skirt pocket, struck a brimstone match against the rough stone block, and held the yellow flame to the jet. The gas caught with a quiet *whoosh*, burning bright blue around the ceramic tip before she turned the key down to settle the light into a steady, clean cone.

I stepped up to the three-way manifold beside the riser. Mounted on top of the central iron tee was a brass-cased Bourdon pressure gauge with a four-inch glass dial. The pointer rested at zero against the lower pin.

As the heavy man cleared the main stop valve, steam slammed into the cold manifold lines with a sharp, metallic clatter—water hammer rattling the iron pipe hangers along the wall. The needle on the Bourdon gauge twitched, swung past ten, and climbed steadily across the black numbers on the dial. Twelve pounds, twenty pounds, thirty-two pounds per square inch.

A small jet of high-pressure vapor hissed out from the packing gland on the center valve, filling the space between us with a white plume that smelled of pine oil and heated river mineral.

"Pressure is rising," I said, watching the needle settle at thirty-six psi against the red limit stroke. "Line is clear."

The attendant stepped past us to the heavy timber door on the far wall of the intake chamber. The door was four inches thick, built of double-lap oak planks faced on both sides with zinc sheet and edged with braided hemp packing to form a tight seal against the frame.

He threw the heavy iron latch bar out of the keeper and pulled the door open. A surge of thick, hot vapor rolled out of the opening, so dense it obscured the threshold in two seconds.

"In," the attendant said. "Ten minutes for the first charge."

We stepped over the high timber sill into the steam room.

The interior was six feet wide, ten feet deep, with a barrel-vaulted ceiling of dark blue engineering brick. Along both side walls ran low slate benches set on brick piers. The floor was made of heavy cast-iron drainage grates, perforated with three-quarter-inch square holes to allow the vapor to rise directly from the steam channels cut into the stone beneath.

The attendant swung the zinc-sheathed door shut behind us. The iron latch bar dropped into the exterior keeper with a heavy, hollow slam. A second later, the exterior deadbolt slid into place with a double click of the iron key.
