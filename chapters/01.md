# 1. Stillwell Hydro

I walked up the coast road to Stillwell Hydro. The track ran two miles along the cliff edge from the turnoff, crushed shale over clay, washed out in three places where the winter drainages crossed it. To the right, the drop went down sixty feet to the shale shelf and the grey water of the bay. The wind came off the water from the north-north-west, cold enough to freeze condensation in the rut lines.

The Hydro sat on the point. From fifty yards out, the front had a respectable look. Three storeys of course-cut granite blocks stood at the ground level, topped with two tiers of red stock brick and a steep slate roof. Six multi-paned windows flanked the entrance on each floor, lined up in tight vertical axes. Up close, the pine frames were soft. The cream paint was peeling in long, curled strips along the sills, exposing greyed wood beneath. Copper flashing along the roofline had run green down the masonry in dark streaks.

The smell reached me before I hit the bottom step. It was not just the sea wrack on the shale below or the soft damp of rotting kelp. Beneath the salt and the cold stone was a sharp, chemical tang—iodine, sulfur, and the sour oil smell of heavy steam plants running on cheap coal.

I went up the four limestone steps and stepped through the double entry doors. They were heavy slab oak, four inches thick, fitted with brass kick-plates at the bottom and iron latches. My left ear picked up only the dead thump of the wood closing behind me. My right caught the screech of the upper hinge where the pin lacked grease.

The lobby floor was dark slate quarry tile, nine-inch squares laid straight. The floor was wet. Condensation dripped down from the plastered ceiling, running in thin, vertical lines through the soot coat on the green paint. The air inside was twenty degrees warmer than the road, thick with steam that drifted in grey ribbons from the corridor archway at the back.

Along the west wall sat a pair of high-backed benches made of varnished ash. Three men sat on the front bench in wool coats, their hats resting on their knees. They did not look up when the door shut. Their faces were red, the skin around their eyes puffy and shiny with sweat. A cast-iron radiator beneath the window clanked three times as hot water knocked through the riser.

I walked across the damp tile toward the clerk's station. The counter was dark walnut, four feet high, topped with an iron grate painted black. Behind the grate sat a man in a grey wool coat with ink-stained cuffs.

I stopped at the counter and pulled the folded paper from my coat pocket. It was a signed slip from Senator Ferrick’s house account, printed on heavy rag bond, double-ruled in crimson along the margins. The Senator’s signet was stamped in dark purple wax at the bottom left corner, clear and unbroken.

I passed the slip through the arched opening in the iron grate.

The clerk picked it up with two fingers. He did not look at me. He held the slip up to the gas jet mounted on the wall behind him, checking the watermark against the light. The paper was clean. He laid it flat on a leather desk pad, took an iron ruler from a drawer, and measured the margin spacing against a reference sheet pinned to the wood.

He picked up a steel nib, dipped it into a stone inkwell, and recorded the account name, the voucher number, and the date into a thick pigskin ledger. His handwriting was small and tight. When he finished the entry, he took a heavy brass hand-stamp from a felt pad, lined it up over the bottom margin, and struck the wood handle once with the heel of his palm. The thud echoed off the damp slate tiles.

He slid a brass tag across the counter. It was three inches long, die-stamped with the number twelve and wired to a heavy lead seal.

"Diving preparation, bay three," he said. His voice was flat. "Present the metal to the deck tender."

I took the tag and clipped the iron wire through the strap loop on my leather belt.

A boy in a canvas apron came out of the archway at the rear of the lobby. He did not speak. He pointed down the corridor with his thumb and turned. I followed him.

The corridor pitched downward at five degrees toward the seaward side of the building. The walls were bare brick, painted with white lime that was flaking off in large scales from the dampness. Exposed lead steam pipes ran along the juncture of the wall and ceiling, wrapped in grey horsehair felt and wired every two feet with copper bands. Water dripped from every joint, hitting the concrete floor with a steady, hollow tick.

We passed three sets of reinforced oak doors fitted with rubber gaskets and iron latch-dogs. At the end of the hall, the boy pulled back a heavy curtain of waterproofed canvas.

I stepped into the dive preparation area. It was a concrete bay, thirty feet square, with a ceiling of vaulted brick seven feet above the floor. The floor sloped inward from all four walls toward a central brass drain grate six inches wide. The air was hot and thick, smelling of lime, boiled water, and copper grease.

A heavy wooden bench sat against the east wall, constructed of three-inch oak planks bolted to iron shoes set into the floor.

Two people were already there, sitting on the bench.

Lloyd Hale sat in the middle. He was six feet tall, broad across the shoulders, wearing an unbuttoned canvas vest over a coarse linen shirt. His dark hair looked as though it had been trimmed with a pocket knife. His nose had been broken twice and set off to the right, leaving the bridge thick and flat. His hands rested on his knees; the knuckles were enlarged and scarred white, and two fingers on his right hand did not lie straight. He looked soft around his waist, but his shoulders held the width of a man who had lifted stone.

Maria Vesk sat to his left. She was small, wiry, her fair hair cropped short around her ears. She wore a grey wool tunic tucked into dark trousers. Both her sleeves were rolled past the elbow, showing pale, raised burn scars that ran from her wrists up to the meat of her forearms.

I took the position on the right end of the bench.

The attendant dropped his clipboard onto a small iron desk near the drain and walked out into the corridor. The canvas curtain fell back into place behind him, cutting off the draft from the front hall.

The silence in the concrete room held for ten seconds. The only sound was the drip of water hitting the brass grate in the center of the floor and the steady, low hiss of high-pressure steam behind the brick walls.

Lloyd turned his head toward me. He did not turn his whole body, just his neck, his eyes steady under heavy brows. He looked at my boots, then at the brass tag hanging from my belt loop, then at my face.

"Ever been here before?" he asked.

"No," I said.

Maria moved her head. She looked past Lloyd toward the wall beside the door. A pine log board was bolted to the brickwork, fitted with brass hooks holding numbered wooden tally blocks and a small slate tablet for writing names and times.

She scanned the board, checking the chalk marks at the top column where the shift entries were recorded. A single line of white chalk marked our group entry against the number twelve tag.

She nodded once, a quick, sharp motion of her chin.

"First time for all of us," Maria said.

Her voice was lighter than Lloyd's, clear and dry despite the damp heat in the room. Her statement confirmed my answer. Nobody added anything to it.

We stood up from the bench. The preparation work needed doing before the tender returned, and three people who know job sites do not need to assign pieces of work out loud.
