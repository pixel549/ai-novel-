# 13. Vantry

Vantry got into my chest a full hour before it got into my eyes. The rail line ran dead straight for the last stretch, no bend left to hide behind, and the air coming through the carriage window turned from ordinary coal-smoke into something thicker, closer to the back of the throat, a taste more than a smell, something a man starts swallowing against before he's worked out why. Then the chimneys came up over the last rise — not scattered here and there among the finer buildings, apologetic about themselves, the way Carrowgate keeps a few, but ranked, dozens deep, shoulder to shoulder, standing in the kind of order nothing else in this country had bothered with since the war ran out of needing it. I tried counting them to give my eyes something honest to do and lost the number somewhere past thirty, not because it got away from me but because the counting had quit being mine by then and started running on its own, like a foot that keeps tapping long after the man it's attached to has forgotten starting it.

Sennet had a bell that rang only for meals. Vantry had no bell I ever heard, and no stretch of any hour quiet enough that a man would have noticed one missing.

The gaslamps on the long street up from the station were lit at noon, every one of them, the haze sitting low enough over the rooftops that noon and dusk had stopped bothering to tell each other apart. Nobody on that street so much as glanced up to ask why. I did, the first day. Nobody did it the second day or the third, and I took that for its own kind of answer — not about the lamps. About how long a city has to burn its own sky brown before nobody left in it still troubles to look up.

We found a room above a chandler's, two beds and a pallet again, close enough to the tallow vats that Maria said she'd be dreaming in candle-wax before the week was out. Edie had two days' loading work at the goods yard before she'd finished unrolling her blanket. She called it luck. I called it Edie making her own, same as always, with a flat stare and a willingness to lift what the last man set down. I was given nothing in particular to do. That suited me well enough: walking is work I can do badly without anybody standing close enough to notice, and Vantry gave a man plenty of ground to cover it on.

Ascension House sat three streets back from the mills, behind its own wall, new brick laid up over stone old enough to have gone the colour of a bruise healing out. A bell tower that never rang. A chapel door at the front, wide enough to march a wedding party through two abreast, flanked by steps nobody had worn a groove into — grass coming up stubborn and green between the flags where a congregation's feet should have kept it beaten down. Round the side, out of the way of anyone minded to look, a service door built wide enough for a cart, hinges kept oiled, the ground before it packed black and flat with traffic. In four days I never once saw the front one stand open.

---

Vail came for Maria the second morning. I didn't see her come. Maria met her at the gate alone and I was left with nothing of the woman herself to measure against the one Maria had been describing to us for two weeks — no face, no voice, nothing but the gap where those should have been. I told myself that was no loss. I half believed it.

She was gone the whole of that day and most of the evening. When she finally climbed the chandler's stair she didn't bother with any version of alright. She sat on the end of the bed, still in her coat, and set both hands flat on her knees like she meant to hold something down through them, and she said nothing at all for long enough that Edie put her cord away without being asked. Edie doesn't give up her hands' own work for nothing. It was about as plain as I've ever seen her be.

"There's a woman," Maria said, finally. "Two years gone from herself. Catatonic — doesn't eat on her own, doesn't know a voice from a draught across the room. Eight months carrying, and her hands are folded over the swell of her like somebody arranged them that way once and nobody's had cause to move them since." She looked down at her own hands while she said it, turning them over slowly, as if looking hard enough at them might keep her from having to look at anything else. "The room was clean as a chapel, Edie. Flowers changed every day. Somebody reads to her every afternoon whether she can hear a word of it or not, and I believe they do it because it's kind and not because anybody's watching to mark it down. Every single thing I could put my eyes on in that room was kind."

"And," Edie said.

"And Vail wanted me to put myself into her." The warmth dropped clean out of Maria's voice — not broke, just gone, flatter than it had ever gone telling us about the forty-one crowns. "Not heal her. She was particular about the word. Imbue. What she wants is what's inside me, put into the child before it comes, not spent on the mother at all. She asked it like you'd ask to borrow a kettle." A short laugh came out of her with nothing behind it. "I can hear her saying it. I expect I'll go on hearing her saying it. 'Your gift can't be taught, Maria, only born, and we've spent sixty years trying to stop needing luck for it. You're the least lucky thing to happen to this work in a generation. Put what you carry into that child before it's born, and we'd never have to go looking for another you again.'"

"She ask you plain," I said. Not a question. I wanted it not to need being one.

"Plain as anything. 'You may say no,' she told me, 'and I mean that exactly as I say it.' So I said no." Maria's hands had gone still in her lap. "And she didn't blink at it. Didn't sigh, didn't look away to compose herself, didn't do one single thing a person does when they're disappointed and covering it. She nodded, said she'd hoped I might say exactly that, thanked me for considering it as though I'd turned down a second helping of something, and took me to lunch."

"Lunch," Edie said, in the voice she keeps for a column that won't balance.

"Lunch. A proper tea room downstairs, linen on the table. She ordered me the good bread and a full pot and spent the whole meal on a leak in the Vantry roof and whether Sennet wanted new slate before winter, as if I hadn't ten minutes before told her I wouldn't be poured into a stranger's unborn child like water into a jug. And here's the maddening part." Maria pressed the heels of both hands briefly against her eyes. "The bread was good. I ate two pieces of it. Some fool part of me still wanted to be polite to the woman who raised me, even sitting there with that still ringing in my ears."

Nobody said anything for a while after that. I sat with my gloved hand loose in my lap and ran every ugly thing I'd ever privately decided the Church must be, one after another, up against what Maria had just laid out for us, and every one of them came up gentler than the truth and somehow smaller for it, because none of my versions had ever needed to raise its voice to get what it wanted.

"She isn't sinister, Lloyd." Maria said it straight at me, like she'd followed the thought across the room without needing it spoken. "That's the part I can't get past. If she were sinister I'd know what to do with her by now. I want to be told I'm wrong about her — that's the whole of what I'm asking. Somebody go and look, and come back, and tell me I've frightened myself half to death over a woman who loves me trying, clumsily, to do right by a dying patient."

"We'll look," Edie said. Not comfort. A job taken on.

---

What we found over the four days after that didn't tell Maria she was wrong.

Edie got most of it through the goods yard, where two days of honest loading turned into four of asking the kind of dull question nobody minds answering because nobody's ever troubled to ask it before. Ascension House took brine by the ton, barrels off a dedicated cart twice a week, more than any bath house in the Commonwealth could run through in a year — and the manifest she finally got a look at, folded once and handed back before anyone missed it, didn't list a bath house at all. It listed the chapel.

My own watching went on from the street, same posture I'd kept outside Sennet, except this time somebody was asking me what I'd seen instead of letting me keep it. Edie wanted it like a measurement off a cable — flat, exact, no feeling folded into it — and once I understood that was the whole of what she was after, I found I could hand it over without the usual cost of saying it out loud. I counted that cart on both its days for four days running, six barrels to the load, and gave her the number each time the moment she asked for it: sixty-odd barrels in through that service door before the week was out, and not one empty cask ever came back out again.

The chapel I watched longer than I'd meant to. Nobody worshipped in it that I ever saw. No candle lit behind the glass, no organ, no footprint on those front steps that the rain hadn't put there itself. Down at the base of the wall, where the new brick met the old stone, the joint sat wrong — fresh mortar packed hard against a foundation cut and dressed by other hands, long before anyone had thought to raise a chapel over the top of it. Edie ran the rest of it down in an afternoon at the parish records office, a clerk bored enough to fetch old deeds for the asking: that stone had stood on that ground before the Church had owned so much as the street it fronted. Whatever Ascension House was a roof for, the roof came a long time after whatever it was roofing.

Low in that old stone, below where any window had business being, there was a window anyway — barred, close to the ground, the glass behind the bars gone a thick, swimming green with age. I only caught it lit once, the third evening, long after the carts had stopped. Not lamplight. A pale standing gleam, the kind a great deal of still water throws back when somebody passes a light along above it, and against that gleam, once, a shape crossed that moved nothing like a man walks and everything like something keeping time to a rhythm I couldn't hear from the street. I stood there until it went dark again and told myself plainly that a cellar can hold water and a man can carry a lamp oddly, and that neither fact makes the other one false. I didn't believe myself any more than I usually do when I say that to myself. I reported it anyway, same flat words I'd given Edie for the barrels, and left her and Maria to make of it what they could, neither one having seen it for themselves.

Nothing I told them across those four days got dressed up and nothing got taken back, and neither of them once told me I'd got any of it wrong. Maria stopped ruling on any of it at all — not the barrels, not the bad joint in the wall, not the register of names Edie finally talked a clerk into letting her copy, intakes running back years with never a single discharge date set against any of them. She used to rule. Two priests on a north road and she'd had them judged real men inside the minute, because a ruling was a mercy she could still afford to hand out free. There was no ruling left in her to spend on this one.

Vail received her twice more that week, warm both times as the first, and answered every single thing Maria asked her as though the asking cost nothing at all. "Where do they go, after," Maria put to her straight, the third day, and Vail told her — Maria gave it back to us flat, no performance left in it by then — "Most of them don't, dear. That isn't a cruelty, whatever it sounds like from outside it. It's simply where the work begins. Not where it ends."

Nobody asked her anything plainer than that all week, and she never once failed to answer it.

On the fourth evening I came back from the chapel wall with the same report I'd given every night running, barrels and mortar and nothing new under either, and found Maria sitting with Edie's register open flat across her knees, one finger moving down a column of names she'd already read a dozen times over.

"Sixty-odd barrels," I said. "No bath house gets built on figures like that."

Maria didn't answer with a verdict this time either. She closed the register and set it down on the floorboards between the three of us, careful, as if it might still go off, and looked up past it at Edie instead of at me.

"Thursday," she said. "Whatever it turns out to be, I want to be standing inside it before I can talk myself back out of wanting to know."

Edie didn't look up from the thread she'd worked loose off her own sleeve, turning it once between two fingers before she knotted the end off short and dropped it. "Thursday," she said, and that was the whole of it decided.
