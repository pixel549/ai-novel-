# 12. Forty-One Crowns

Sennet announced itself with a bell that rang for meals and not for hours, and the first sound we got of the place was a flat iron clang at noon that meant nothing to Edie or me and sent half the children in the lane running the same direction at once. The almshouse itself sat back from the road behind a low wall, two storeys of grey stone with a bread oven's chimney smoking steady at the side and washing strung across the yard in long sagging lines, shirts and sheets lifting and dropping in a wind that never quite settled on a direction. Over the door somebody had carved, a long time ago and none too skilfully, a loaf, a roof, and a pair of clasped hands, worn soft at the edges by eighty years of hands going in and out beneath them. A box on an iron post stood just inside the wall, slotted at the top, FOR THE POOR cut into its face in letters gone green with age. Maria looked at it longer than she looked at anything else on the way in.

"You don't have to come to the door with me," she said, not to either of us in particular, her eyes still on the box.

"Wasn't going to," Edie said, which was true, and also the kindest thing she could have said to her just then, because it meant nobody was waiting to watch.

I said I'd be about. It was as much as I had to offer and Maria took it as she takes most things I say that aren't quite enough: a small flat smile that cost her something to produce, a hand laid briefly on my arm, there and gone, before she went up the path alone with her bundle still tied the same knot it had been since Carrowgate. She didn't knock. The door opened before her fist was raised, a woman already in it, already saying her name like she'd been saying it all morning in her head and had simply been waiting for a mouth to put it through. I didn't hear what came after that. The door closed, not hard, and that was the whole of what I got.

Edie had her ledger out before Maria was ten steps up the path, totting something that didn't need totting. "I'll see about a bed for the night," she said. "And ask after the Vantry road while I'm at it. No sense you standing here with nothing for your hands to do and me doing nothing useful either." She didn't ask whether I minded being left. She never asks that. She looked once at the almshouse, a long flat assessing look, pricing the walls before she'd decide what they were good for, and then she went, back down toward the town proper, her cord already out and working between two fingers before she'd gone a dozen yards.

I found a place against the wall opposite, in the shade of an elm gone half bare already though autumn was only getting started on it, and I watched the building the rest of the afternoon, because watching buildings was work I could do standing still and nobody could fault me for doing it badly.

---

It breathed. I want to put it plainer than that and I can't, because plainer than that isn't honest. The chimney smoke went up thick when somebody fed the oven and thinned when they let it be, in and out on no schedule I could follow, a chest rising and falling without anyone minding its own lungs. The washing on the lines filled and emptied with the wind, not all at once — one sheet caught a gust and sagged a beat after, like a held breath let go late — and a door at the side of the building opened every so often to let a sister out with a basket or a bucket and shut again behind her, in, out, in, out, the whole building taking its air through a dozen small openings instead of one mouth. I sat there turning my gloved right hand over in my lap, feeling where the elbow still sat crooked no matter how many times the bonesetter in Carrowgate had sworn it would settle, and told myself plainly: a building with a working kitchen in it breathes. That was all it was. I had seen worse things done by the Church than a dinner bell and a clean line of washing, and neither fact made the other one false.

My own mother's house had a bell too, smaller, a hand bell Da rang off the step when supper was on, and I hadn't heard it in nine years and had sent money home every one of them instead of the one thing that would have actually cost me something. I did not think I was better than Maria for having walked past my own door at Callow Ford without going in, two weeks gone now and still aching in me, a cracked tooth that hadn't finished deciding how badly it meant to hurt. She was in there handing hers over to a woman she hadn't seen in six years, on purpose, by her own choosing, with nobody holding a door shut behind her. I'd gone the other way. I turned that over in my lap alongside my hand, found no new angle on it I hadn't already found twice on the road from Ammet Ford, and put it down, because there's only so many times a man can weigh the same stone before he's just holding it.

The light had gone long and gold-coloured and the washing had mostly come in off the lines by the time the door opened again and it was her.

---

She came down the path at an ordinary pace, not fast, not dragging, her bundle under her arm exactly as she'd carried it in, and if I hadn't spent two weeks learning the particular way she holds her own shoulders when nothing's wrong, I'd have believed what the rest of her was telling me. Her eyes were dry. Her voice, when she reached me and I pushed off the wall, came out light, almost amused at herself for having kept me waiting so long.

"Well," she said. "That's done."

"You alright?"

"I'm alright." Quick, easy, already moving past it, reaching to pick a scrap of lint off my sleeve that hadn't been there a moment before. She does that when she wants her hands busy on something small instead of something true.

I knew it for a lie the second it left her, the same way I know when a man's about to swing before his shoulder's finished turning — not sight, nothing I could point to and name, just the plain fact of her voice sitting a half-tone above where it usually sits and her hand finding my sleeve instead of my eyes. I'd have put money on it in any room in the Commonwealth. I said nothing, because I am the last man alive with standing to go asking anybody else why their face doesn't match their mouth, and because some lies are the kindest thing a person's got to offer you and the only decent thing to do with one is let it stand where it's set.

"Edie's found us a bed for the night," I said instead. "And asked after the Vantry road."

"Vantry," she said, and something crossed her face there that wasn't the lie from a moment before — something closer to the truth under it, there and folded away again before I could get a proper look. "Yes. We'll be going to Vantry."

She didn't say why yet. I didn't ask. We walked down toward the town together, and she talked the whole way about nothing — the state of the oven smoke, a cat she'd seen asleep on a windowsill inside, whether the bread they'd fed the residents that morning had been proved long enough — the kind of talk a person makes when the silence behind it is doing all the actual work.

---

Edie had found us a room above a cooper's shop, small, two beds and a pallet, and a fire going by the time we were all three in it with the shutters closed against the evening. Maria sat on the edge of one bed with her boots off and her knees drawn up, and for a long while she didn't say anything at all, which was its own kind of unusual for her, and Edie didn't push and I didn't either, because neither of us has ever once gotten anything worth having out of Maria by pushing.

Then she started talking, and once she started she didn't stop until the whole of it was out.

She told it like she tells most things that matter to her — sideways first, the door opening before she was ready, Vail's face doing something complicated and glad all at once, saying her name twice because once wasn't enough to believe it with. "She didn't even let me get through the greeting," Maria said. "Pulled me in by both hands before I'd said a word past her name, and held on to them the whole time we talked, like I might go again if she let go, which — fair, I suppose, I did." She described the room they'd sat in, small and warm, a pot of actual tea, Vail's hands never still the whole time, folding linen even while she talked, flour still under one thumbnail from the morning's bread because she still baked it herself, every day, had never once stopped. Six years gone and the first thing out of the woman was not a reckoning. It was relief, plain and total, that Maria was alive and fed and in front of her.

"And then I told her," Maria said, and her hands went still in her lap for the first time since she'd started. "Forty-one crowns. The poor box, the one by the door — I didn't even have to say which one, there's only ever been the one. I told her for Anse Duley, three children and a paperwork refusal that would've put them on the road in a fortnight, and I told her I took it and ran because I couldn't stand to be in the building with what I'd done, and I'd carried it six years waiting to say it to somebody who had the right to judge me for it."

"And," Edie said, which was as much of a question as Edie generally asks.

"She laughed." Maria said it like she still didn't believe her own sentence. "Not unkindly. Delighted — that's the only word for it, she looked delighted, the way she used to look when I'd finally got a stitch right after failing it forty times. She said she'd been hoping for years I'd turn out to be someone who could set a rule aside for the person standing in front of them, because most people can't, and the work doesn't work without somebody who can. She forgave the theft in about as long as it takes to say it. Fifteen seconds, maybe less. Six years I'd been carrying it like a stone in my boot and she set it down in fifteen seconds like it weighed nothing at all, because to her it didn't."

"That's not a reckoning," Edie said. "That's a woman telling you the debt was never real."

"I know." Maria's voice cracked a little on it, the first crack all night. "That's the trouble with it. I built six years on it being real."

I didn't say anything to that, because I didn't have anything that wouldn't have been smaller than what she'd just said. Edie turned her cord over once in her hands and didn't say anything either, which from Edie is its own kind of statement — she holds to her lines hard enough that a theft forgiven out of hand, with no scale weighed and no price set, plainly sat wrong with her in a place she wasn't going to go digging at out loud, not tonight, maybe not ever.

"She asked me to come to Vantry," Maria said, into the fire more than to either of us. "To help with something. Said I've always had more of the gift in me than anyone she's trained, and she wants it put to use properly. I said yes before I'd finished hearing the whole of the question, because what was I going to say to her, after that."

She looked up then, and for a moment the whole evening's performance dropped away and what was underneath it was simpler and worse: not guilt, not relief, just a woman who had walked in carrying a weight she'd built her whole adult life around and walked out with nothing in her hands at all, and didn't yet know what to do with the room that left in her.

"I told you I was alright," she said to me, quiet, not quite an apology. "I wasn't. Seeing her again — before I'd said a word about the money, just seeing her in that doorway — that's what undid me. Not the confessing. The confessing was easy. It was already done the moment she said my name like that."

"I know," I said.

She looked at me a moment longer, like she'd expected to have to argue the point and found she didn't need to, and then she lay back on the bed with her arm over her eyes and let the fire do the rest of the talking for a while.

Edie banked the coals low before she took the pallet, and in the last of the light I watched the cord go still in her hands, finished for the night, forty-one knots or none, I never once asked her to let me count it.
