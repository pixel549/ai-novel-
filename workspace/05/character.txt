We stood three paces back from the stone pedestal at the far end of the chapel aisle. The aisle measured six meters wide between the granite pillars. The floor was flags of grey granite, laid tight with mortar that had turned to dry white dust along the seams.

Where the chalice should have sat, there was only the square top of the limestone block, sixty centimeters on each side and one meter high. The top face was flat, cut with a shallow circular depression two centimeters deep and twenty centimeters across.

The depression was empty. I looked at the stone. No metal, no glass, no liquid. A thin layer of grey dust sat in the bottom of the carving.

"It is not here," I said.

Lloyd stood to my right, half a pace behind my shoulder. My left ear was dead from the camouflet at the fourth sap, so I turned my chin two inches right to keep his mouth in my good ear. His right arm hung at his side, the elbow locked at its inward angle, five degrees off true. His fingers were still, the two bent knuckles hooked in toward his palm. He was not looking at the empty pedestal. He was looking three inches above it, his eyes fixed on the empty air.

"It's there," Lloyd said. His voice was low and flat. "On the block."

I stepped forward one pace. I reached out my left hand and swept my palm across the top of the limestone block. My fingers hit no resistance. The dry dust shifted under my skin and coated my palm flat across the cable scars. I pulled my hand back and wiped the dust onto my trousers.

"There is nothing on the stone," I said. "The surface is clear."

Maria stepped up beside Lloyd. Her shawl was drawn tight across her shoulders, tucked under her chin. She looked at the pedestal, then at Lloyd, then back at the empty limestone block.

"It is on the pedestal," Maria said. Her voice was plain and calm. "It is made of lead, four inches high, filled to the rim."

As soon as she said it, the light shifted on the stone.

The space three inches above the pedestal was no longer empty. A heavy cup sat inside the circular carving where my palm had swept two seconds before. It was dark grey, dull and unpolished, with two broad handles riveted to the sides. The metal was thick, beaten unevenly around the upper lip. Inside the bowl, a dark liquid sat level, six millimeters below the rim. It did not ripple. It reflected no light from the wall lamps.

I did not move toward it. I checked the distance between the base of the cup and the edge of the limestone block. It sat exactly in the center of the carved depression.

Maria walked forward two steps. Her boots made no sound against the granite floor. She stopped at the edge of the pedestal and reached out both hands. Her sleeves pulled back two inches, showing the slick red burn scars on her forearms where the old fire-work had caught her.

She grasped the handles of the lead cup. She did not hesitate and she did not look at Lloyd. She lifted the chalice three inches straight up off the stone.

The weight was real in her hands. Her wrists braced against the load. Lead runs roughly eleven pounds to the gallon, and the vessel held a pint at least. I watched the tendons tighten in her forearms as she bore the weight. She held it steady, level with her chest.

She tilted the rim to her mouth. She took one measured sip. Her throat moved once as she swallowed. The liquid in the cup dropped by three millimeters.

She lowered the chalice immediately. She set it back into the circular depression on the limestone block. The base struck the stone with a dull, heavy thump that carried through the flags beneath my boots.

She took one step back from the pedestal. She folded her hands into the front of her shawl.

"That is enough," Maria said. "No more."

Lloyd moved past her. He took two steps to the left side of the limestone block. He did not look at the lead cup. He looked down at the flat top of the stone bench built into the chapel wall eighteen inches to the left of the pedestal.

The bench was a slab of rough-cut schist, three meters long, supported by two masonry piers set into the floor.

Lloyd placed his right hand flat on the schist bench.

His right sleeve pulled up two inches. The skin of his forearm looked pale and thick. For two seconds, the image of his arm blurred. The outline of his forearm double-notched, a faint, translucent shadow shifting three inches to the right while his physical hand remained pressed against the stone. The light from the wall fixture behind his wrist showed through the shadow, clear and unbent.

He closed his eyes. His jaw set hard against his neck.

A thin, dark line rose from his palm where it touched the schist. It was the size of an iron nail, grey and fluid, drawn upward out of the stone into his hand. It passed into his skin without breaking the surface.

Then Lloyd pulled his hand back from the bench.

A pale shape came out of his chest, smaller than a thumb, clear as water in a bucket. It hovered above his palm for the space of a single breath. It had no sharp edges. It looked like the corner of a room I had never seen, a high window with three iron bars and a dark frame, condensed down into a scrap no bigger than a coin.

The shape expanded two inches, flipped once in the air, and dissolved into nothing.

It vanished before I could read the lines of the window or count the bars. There was no smoke, no sound, and no mark left on the air.

Lloyd dropped his arm to his side. The shadow around his wrist was gone. His right hand hung limply, the misaligned elbow locked at its usual angle.

"It is cleared," Lloyd said.

He turned away from the bench and walked toward the center of the aisle.

I checked my pocket watch. The hands showed four o'clock in the afternoon. We had entered the intake chamber at eight in the morning by the city clocks. Eight hours had passed on the dial.

My body felt no fatigue from eight hours of standing. My eyes were clear, but the back of my neck was stiff. I had missed the midday rest cycle at twelve o'clock and the second rest cycle at four.

In the Fourth Tunnelling Company, a sapper missed two rest cycles only during an active sap under fire. After sixteen hours without sleep, reaction time dropped by thirty percent. Timber framing errors increased. Measurements went wide by two inches in every ten feet. You made mistakes you did not notice until the shoring gave way.

I logged the loss in my head. Two cycles gone. Zero hours slept. The debt remained on the ledger, unpaid.

Lloyd walked back toward the stone bench on the left wall. He did not look where he was putting his feet. He reached out his right arm to steady himself against the edge of the schist slab.

He did not place his hand on top of the bench. He swung his right arm straight into the side of the stone slab, six inches below the upper edge.

The physical bench did not break. Lloyd’s arm did not stop.

His right hand and forearm passed through four inches of solid schist as if the stone were soft grease. The cloth of his sleeve disappeared into the grey rock. The misaligned elbow disappeared into the side of the pier.

As his arm crossed the stone, the three-meter bench trembled. A low, dull vibration ran through the schist slab and into the floor flags under my boots. A puff of dry white mortar dust rose from the join where the support pier met the floor.

Lloyd did not react. He pulled his arm back toward his body. The forearm emerged from the stone intact. His sleeve was dry. No dust adhered to his skin. There was no cut, no scrape, and no blood on his knuckles.

Maria stood three paces away, watching the floor. She did not look at Lloyd’s arm. She did not look at the bench.

"The way back is open," Maria said to the wall.

Neither of them spoke about the bench. Neither of them mentioned the arm passing through four inches of solid rock. I noted the displacement of the mortar dust on the floor flags. The dust lay in a fresh ring two millimeters thick around the base of the pier. The bench had shifted three millimeters to the south during the passage. I kept my mouth shut.

I stepped into the center of the aisle and pointed toward the archway behind us.

"We move now," I said.

We walked back through the chapel archway. The passage beyond was five meters long, walled in coarse granite blocks with no mortar in the joints. The height was two point two meters, tapering to one point nine at the far door.

The air grew thick with wet heat. The smell of cold lime faded, replaced by the heavy stink of boiled vinegar and wet iron.

We stepped through the inner frame. The granite blocks dissolved into soft plaster walls painted with green lime-wash. The flagstone floor gave way to narrow tongue-and-groove pine boards, warped upward along the nail lines from constant steam.

We were back inside the hydro steam room.

The steel door behind us was shut tight, its heavy iron latch dropped into the keeper. The brass pressure gauge on the wall beside the frame read ninety pounds per square inch. Steam leaked from the packing around the main valve stem in a thin, whistling jet that hit the pine wall two feet away.

A nurse stood beside the bench on the north wall.

She wore a grey canvas apron over a dark wool dress. The hem of the apron was stained black with coal soot and yellowed along the seams with dried sulphur paste. A pair of heavy copper scissors hung from a leather strap at her hip.

She held a coil of thick hemp cord in her left hand. The cord was bound at six-inch intervals with brass wire ferrules, four turns of wire per ferrule.

She stepped toward the wooden bench where Lloyd had sat during the initial intake. She did not look at us as we entered. She moved with short, fast steps, her hard-soled shoes clattering on the wet pine boards.

She took the free end of the hemp cord and looped it twice around the iron leg of the bench. She pulled the knot tight, threw a half-hitch over the standing line, and cinched it down against the iron.

She reached up to the wall fixture above the bench. Attached to the pine casing was a heavy brass housing containing a spring-driven mechanical clockwork timer. A white card was slotted into a frame on the front of the casing.

The nurse picked up an iron key attached to her apron strap by a chain. She inserted the key into the side of the brass casing and turned it three times to the right. The internal spring caught with a heavy, metallic clatter.

A small wooden shutter on the face of the display dropped open.

Behind the shutter was a white painted paper roll marked with black ink numerals. The top line read: EXTRACTION TIMER.

Below the heading, two sets of numbers sat side by side in carved brass windows.

I looked at the wall clock mounted on the opposite side of the room, six feet above the steel door. The clock had a white enamel dial ten inches across, with black steel hour and minute hands. The hands read four fifteen. Four hours had elapsed since we walked through the steel door into the steam chamber.

I looked back at the display on the mechanical timer.

The paper roll in the left window showed the figure: 04 HOURS.

The paper roll in the right window showed the figure: 19 DAYS REMAINING.

I checked the two displays twice. The wall clock ran on a heavy lead pendulum behind glass. The pendulum beat sixty ticks to the minute, steady and true. The mechanical timer beside the bench ticked four times as fast, a sharp, buzzing vibration that rattled the brass casing against the pine wall.

The nurse turned from the wall. Her face was thin and pale, her grey hair pinned back tightly under a white linen cap that had turned yellow at the edges from the steam.

She looked at Maria first, then at me, then down at Lloyd.

"Did you enjoy the treatment?" she asked.

Her voice was high and flat, without any cadence at the end of the sentence. She asked it like an inspector reading an inventory sheet at a gate.

"It was adequate," I said.

Maria pulled her shawl tighter around her chest. "The air was warm," she said.

Lloyd said nothing. He sat down on the wooden bench beside the iron leg where the nurse had tied the hemp cord.

The nurse waited three seconds. She did not ask Lloyd a second time. She smoothed the front of her soot-stained apron with both hands, turned on her heel, and walked toward the small service door in the east corner of the room.

She lifted the iron latch, stepped through into the dark corridor beyond, and pulled the door shut behind her. The latch dropped into the keeper with a sharp click.

The room was silent except for the whistling leak at the main steam valve and the rapid, buzzing tick of the extraction timer on the wall.

I stood by the steel outer door and checked the bolt. It was slid back six inches into its housing, unlocked from the inside. The room was six meters long by four meters wide. The ceiling was low, pine matchboarding stained dark by steam and grease.

Maria walked to the center of the room. She stood under the single gas lamp mounted in an iron bracket on the wall. The flame was turned low, a blue cap two inches high behind a cracked glass chimney.

Lloyd did not stand up. He sat slumped forward on the wooden bench. His hands rested on his knees. His head hung down so that his chin almost touched his collar.

His eyes were wide open. He was not looking at his hands, his boots, or the wet floorboards. He was staring straight ahead at the lower hem of Maria's skirt, five feet away.

His shoulders were tight, hitched up toward his ears. His right arm, resting on his right knee, was trembling—a tiny, rapid spasm that ran from his shoulder down into his misaligned elbow.

He did not turn his head when I shifted my boots on the pine boards. He did not look at the extraction timer on the wall.

"Maria," Lloyd said.

His voice was hardly louder than the steam leaking from the valve. It was dry and cracked at the edges.

Maria did not move toward him. She kept her hands folded under her shawl, standing straight under the blue flame of the gas jet.

"Yes, Lloyd," she said.

Lloyd did not lift his head. His eyes remained locked on her hem.

"Is the room here?" Lloyd asked. "Is the bench real?"

He did not raise his hand to touch the wood he was sitting on. He did not shift his weight on the pine seat. He sat entirely rigid, his knees locked, his chin dropped against his chest, waiting.

Maria looked down at him. Her face showed no surprise. She did not smile, and she did not reach out to lay a hand on his shoulder.

"The bench is pine," Maria said. Her voice was firm and plain, carrying clearly across the five meters of steam. "The room is four meters wide. The door is steel. We are standing in the hydro."

Lloyd took one long, slow breath. His shoulders dropped two inches. The trembling in his right forearm slowed and stopped.

He remained slumped in the steam, his hands resting heavy on his knees, his eyes fixed on Maria’s skirt, waiting for her next word before he moved a single muscle.
