Three weeks passed in the staging cell below the dry level. Down here, time was not measured by sun or shift bell. We tracked it by tallow burn, hard bread, and the drop of water in the lead-lined catch-tin. The air stayed heavy with soot, sweat, and rotting pine.

I sat on a hemlock bench against the south wall. My left side was pressed to cold masonry, where the dead silence sat in my left ear—the permanent souvenir from a camouflet at the Red Redoubt four years back. In my right ear, the cell held flat noises: the drip of mineral water from a roof joint, the rustle of wool, the scrape of iron against leather. Maria sat three feet off on an overturned nail keg, using an iron pick to pare the grey rind off a slab of salt pork. Lloyd lay on a greasy blanket near the timber collar, his heels resting on an empty powder crate.

I opened the canvas supply sack between my boots. Rations were down to grit and hard rind. I scraped crumbs from the rations out of the bottom corners of the bag with my thumb, piling the dry yellow dust onto a slate. I swept the dust into my palm and ate it. It tasted of jute fibre and stale flour.

I picked up a tallow candle, measured the remaining length against my middle knuckle—two inches—and jammed it into the rusted socket. I wiped grime from my hands against the knees of my sapper trousers, the cable scars on my palms catching on the grey wool.

A sudden clatter echoed down the vertical shaft from the upper drift. Iron on stone—a piton slipping or a loose bolt falling through the grate sixty feet up.

My heart hit my ribs before my head processed the sound, a spike of terror that sent cold down my spine for a breath. My right hand locked on the haft of the trench spade leaning against the bench.

The spasm passed. My fingers relaxed off the wood. I let my breath out through my nose and wiped my damp palm against my thigh. It was simple jumpiness. No line had been cut.

Maria did not look up from her pork rind. Lloyd shifted his left shoulder against the crate, eyes open, staring up at spruce lagging black with mold.

"Rations for four days if we cut bread to three ounces," I said.

Lloyd did not turn his head. "We go down today."

"The drift is clear," Maria said. She set her pick on the keg and wiped pork grease from her knuckles with a scrap of linen. "The air smells of wet lime, but it stays in the lungs."

I stood up, hooked my leather tool belt around my waist, and pulled the iron buckle to the third hole. I checked my gear: five iron pitons, a two-pound steel hammer, a ball of chalk line, a brass rule, and my oilcloth notebook with the charcoal stub.

Lloyd pulled his boots on, tied the leather thongs tight in double knots, and stood up. He picked up the six-foot iron crowbar from the corner timber and rested the square shank against his right shoulder.

We stepped out of the staging cell into the east drift.

***

The chapel sat forty yards along the east drift, down a five-degree slope cut through solid limestone. The tunnel opened into the chamber without a timber collar.

We entered in single file. I went first with the oil-wick lamp. Lloyd followed three paces behind with the bar. Maria took the left flank with her iron hand-lantern.

I stopped at the threshold and raised the lamp to sweep the vault.

The room was rectangular, thirty-two feet long east to west, eighteen feet wide north to south. The roof was vaulted, formed by four semi-circular stone arches springing from low wall piers. The masonry was smooth grey limestone, laid dry without mortar. At the far end of the chamber, raised on a two-inch stone dais, sat an altar. It was a single hewn block of dark granite, six feet long, three feet deep, and three feet high. Its top was flat and bare.

"Check the piers," I said.

I walked to the northwest pier and placed my palm flat against the ashlar. Cold and dry. I drew my brass rule, measured the pier face at eighteen inches, and marked a chalk cross at the base block.

I moved south across the floor. The floor was built of limestone flagstones, two feet by three feet, laid in alternating courses. I counted twelve flags from the west wall to the center aisle.

Lloyd walked to the altar. He set the iron shoe of his crowbar on the floor and leaned against the shank.

"Nothing on it," Lloyd said.

Maria walked the southern wall, holding her lantern eight inches from the masonry to track the horizontal bed joints.

"No fissures," Maria said. "No timber decay. The arches take the load cleanly."

I walked the perimeter of the chamber once—thirty-eight paces around the walls. I returned to the entry threshold, opened my notebook, and wrote down the survey: thirty-two feet length, eighteen feet width, four stone arches, granite altar against east wall, floor intact.

"First sweep complete," I said. "We run it again."

***

We stepped back into the drift, waited sixty seconds by my pocket watch, and entered the chapel a second time.

I held the oil-wick lamp ahead, tracing my previous footings. Four paces forward, I raised the light toward the altar.

The granite block stood in the same position against the east wall, but on its flat top, three tallow candles were set in a straight line along the front edge. They had not been there six minutes ago. The wax was yellowed and unlit. Two iron candle-holders lay on their sides beside the center candle, rotated ninety degrees from the altar axis.

"The altar," I said.

Lloyd stepped up onto the stone dais and touched the cold wax of the center candle.

"They weren't here," Lloyd said.

"They were not here on the first pass," I said.

I swung the beam to the south wall between the second and third vault arches.

A stone column stood four feet off the south pier. It was three feet thick, built of stacked drum sections of coarse grey sandstone. The middle drum section was fractured vertically, a split two inches wide running down into the floor flag. Sharp flakes of grit rested on the base ring. The air smelled of damp sand and fresh lime dust.

On our first sweep, that wall bay had been open masonry.

I stepped to the column, extended my brass rule, and slid it into the split. It went in four inches before hitting solid stone.

"This column was not in this bay," I said. "The south wall was open."

Maria walked up behind me and raised her lantern over the sandstone drums. She touched the smooth face of the column and looked up at the arch.

"The room is unchanged, Edie," Maria said. Her voice was flat and warm. She adjusted her wool shawl. "The column has been here. The arches rest on it."

"It was not here three minutes ago," I said. "I logged four wall piers and open bays. There were no sandstone columns."

"You missed it on the draft," Maria said. She turned back toward the altar, her face steady in the lantern light. "The room is as it was."

I looked at Lloyd. He was staring at the split in the sandstone drum. His face was set hard, his broken nose crooked against the lamplight. He did not speak. He kept his left hand tight on his crowbar.

***

"We sweep a third time," I said.

We returned to the west portal, waited until the second hand on my watch made one full revolution, and stepped back into the chapel.

I dropped my eyes to the floor flagstones in front of my boots, pacing forward in three-foot increments and counting the courses. One, two, three, four.

At the fifth course, two feet left of the center line, sat a border of small ceramic mosaic tiles embedded between flagstones. On the first pass, the border had been a continuous pattern of blue and white squares.

Now, a tile was missing from the inner row.

I knelt on the stone. The missing square left a shallow depression, two inches across, filled with dry grey mortar. The mortar bed showed a crisp square imprint where the tile had sat, the edges sharp and uncrumbled. No loose ceramic debris lay on the surrounding flags.

I pulled my charcoal pencil from my notebook binding, set the paper on my knee, and wrote: *Pass 3. Chancel floor, fifth course, left of aisle. One blue-glazed mosaic tile missing. Mortar bed exposed, dry, crisp imprint. Discrepancy logged.*

I stood up and pointed down with my boot toe. "Look here."

Maria stopped beside me. She looked down at the empty square.

"It is a standard floor," Maria said. She did not look at my notebook. "The tile was lost years ago, Edie. The floor has not altered since we stepped down from the drift."

"The mortar is fresh grey dust," I said. "No foot-grime in the pocket. It was covered sixty seconds ago."

"It is unchanged," Maria repeated. She looked at me, her scarred forearms bare where her sleeves were pinned. Her tone remained quiet and simple. "We are looking at the same masonry we surveyed when we entered."

I closed the notebook and pulled the strap tight, contradicting her insistence. "It is not the same. I have it written."

***

Lloyd moved away from the altar, walking down the center aisle between Maria and me. He carried the iron crowbar across his chest, eyes fixed on the ceiling vault.

"There is no sound," Lloyd said.

He took one step forward with his right foot.

Half a second before his boot landed, the pulse came through my teeth—the mechanical jump in the jaw muscle that means violence is already in the air. No voice, no picture. Just knowing.

There was no time to pull him back.

Without warning a searing force passed through the air. No sound of cracking stone, no rush of wind, no flash of flame. An invisible plane cut across the space six inches to the right of Lloyd's body, five feet off the floor.

The searing force sliced Lloyd's right arm clean at the elbow.

His lower right arm detached four inches below the joint. The severed limb dropped, hitting the flagstones with a wet thud, the fingers flexing once before going limp.

Blood gushed from the open stump, splashing his trousers and pooling on the dry limestone.

Lloyd did not fall or scream. He shifted his weight, caught the falling iron bar with his left hand, and clamped the stump of his right arm tight against his ribs with his left forearm to check the bleed.

His face turned grey as slate. Sweat broke out across his forehead. He stood rigid, jaw clamped, holding the stump steady while saying nothing.

"Lloyd," I said.

I dropped my lamp onto the flagstones. The oil spilled and burned in the bronze cup. I reached into my belt pouch for a field dressing and tourniquet rod.

Maria was already there. She dropped her hand-lantern and stepped across the open floor. She did not look at the severed arm on the flags.

She placed her hands on the wound, grabbing Lloyd's right upper arm and driving her bare palms into the flesh around the severed artery.

"Hold still," Maria said, her voice rapid and firm. "Hold steady, Lloyd."

Lloyd did not answer. He pressed his back against the stone pier and kept his eyes on the west doorway.

Maria closed her eyes, drew a breath, and began to murmur a low chant in her throat. Spoken flat, without emphasis.

A warm, steady golden glow emanated from beneath her hands. The light grew stronger, shining through her sleeves and illuminating the blood on Lloyd's coat. The smell of burnt copper rose heavy and hot.

Under her hands, flesh and bone stitched back together. The severed blood vessels pulled back into tissue. The white circle of sliced bone extended outward, building a new shaft down from the elbow. Muscle redrew itself over bone like red thread through canvas. Nerve bundles knitted in fine white webs. Skin folded pale and pink over the end of the new forearm.

The golden light flared once and died.

Maria let her breath out in a gasp and pulled her hands back. Her shoulders dropped, her hip leaning against the pier to stay upright.

"It is done," Maria whispered.

Lloyd lowered his left arm.

A new right arm extended from his elbow—forearm, wrist, palm, five fingers. The skin was pale and smooth, clear of old knuckle scars.

The new arm surfaced crooked.

The forearm bones were set at an inward five-degree angle toward his hip, the elbow joint misaligned and thick. The arm turned inward, hand resting across his thigh.

Lloyd raised the crooked limb. He flexed his five fingers. They opened and closed slowly, knuckles popping with a dry sound. He rotated his wrist halfway left, then right. The movement jerked where the misaligned joint bound.

He stared at the crooked limb for five seconds, watching the fingers move. He offered no comment. He did not ask Maria what she had done, nor look at the severed arm lying in the blood pool three feet away.

He reached down with his new right hand, gripped the iron crowbar by its shank, and lifted it. His grip held, though his wrist stayed bent at the odd angle. He rested the bar back against his shoulder.

"The drift shaft," Lloyd said.

***

I turned from the blood pool and walked back to the west portal. The vertical shaft opened eight feet outside the chapel entry, dropping into the lower drift through a timber collar.

I unbuckled the knot-cord reel from my belt.

The knot-cord was five-millimeter braided hemp with a brass sink-weight at the end. Brass wire ferrules were whipped into the braid at one-metre intervals as mechanical depth stops.

I braced my boots against the oak curbing and lowered the brass sink-weight down the shaft.

The cord ran out through my fingers, ferrules ticking past the cable scars on my palms. One metre. Two metres. Three metres.

Beside the shaft frame, a pulley was mounted on a cast-iron bracket. The cord passed through its grooved wheel, turning an internal brass clicker-gauge with a three-digit counter window.

I watched the counter wheel turn. Click. Click. Click.

Prior to entering the chapel, I had calculated and surveyed this shaft twice. The distance from the upper timber collar to the lower stone landing was twelve metres. I had logged twelve metres in my notebook on two separate shifts.

The cord kept running.

Eight metres. Nine metres. Ten metres. Eleven metres. Twelve metres.

The brass weight did not strike the bottom landing. The cord stayed taut under the weight of the lead core.

Thirteen metres. Fourteen metres. Fifteen metres. Sixteen metres. Seventeen metres. Eighteen metres.

The line slackened as the brass weight struck stone below.

The mechanical clicker-gauge on the iron bracket stopped turning. I pulled the knot-cord down the shaft and watched the numbers settle. The gauge clicked nineteen metres—a length that did not match my earlier calculations.

I held the line still, raised it three inches, and dropped it back to feel the thud against the floor. Exactly nineteen metres.

I looked down into the dark opening. The air rising from the shaft was cold and stale, carrying dry dust and iron scrap.

"The shaft was twelve metres," I said.

Maria stood behind my left shoulder.

"The gauge says nineteen," Maria said. "The line is resting on the bottom."

"The shaft has not been recut," I said. "The timber sets are six feet apart. There are two sets below us. That is twelve feet of timbering, not sixty."

"The weight is on the floor," Maria said. Her voice was flat and unhurried. "The reading is nineteen metres."

I pulled the oilcloth notebook from my belt and opened it to a blank page.

I took my charcoal pencil and pressed the point to the paper. I wrote a note questioning the cord's reading: *Shaft depth measured at 19 metres by brass clicker-gauge and knot-cord. Previous survey established 12 metres. No structural excavation present. Line length inconsistent with physical timbering.*

I stared at the written entry, the number feeling out of place in the stale air.
