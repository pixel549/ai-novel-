The weeks passed in the staging cell below the dry level. Time did not move by sun or shift bell down here. We measured it by tallow burn, by the consumption of hard bread, and by the drop of water in the lead-lined tin. The air was heavy with damp soot, stale sweat, and the sour tang of wet timber.

I sat on a hemlock bench against the south wall. My left side was to the cold masonry, where the dead silence sat in my left ear—the old souvenir from the camouflet at Red Redoubt. In my right ear, the small sounds of the cell stayed constant: the drip of mineral water from a roof joint, the scrape of iron against leather. Maria sat three feet off on an overturned nail keg, using a small iron pick to pare the grey rind off a slab of salt pork. Lloyd lay on a greasy wool blanket near the timber collar, his boots off, his heels resting on a pine crate.

I opened the canvas supply sack between my feet. The rations were down to dust and hard rind. I pulled out three hardtack biscuits, two strips of salted beef, and a tin of axle grease used for the pulley bearings. I scraped the crumbs out of the bottom corners of the canvas bag with my thumb, piling the dry yellow grit onto a flat piece of slate. I swept the dust into my palm and ate it. It tasted of jute fibre, damp wool, and stale flour.

I picked up a tallow candle, measured the remaining length against my middle knuckle—two inches—and jammed it into the rusted iron wall socket. The room filled with the smell of burning mutton fat. I wiped my hands on the knees of my sapper trousers. The flat scars across my palms from the winch cables snagged slightly on the coarse grey wool.

A sudden clatter echoed down the vertical shaft from the upper drift. It was iron on stone—a piton slipping or a loose bolt falling through the iron grate sixty feet up.

My heart hit my ribs like an unseated prop before my head could name the noise. A spike of cold terror went straight down my spine into my heels. My right hand was already on the ash haft of the trench spade leaning against the bench leg before my eyes had cleared the doorway.

The spasm passed. The spade stayed where it was. My fingers relaxed off the wood, one by one. I let my breath out through my nose and wiped my damp palm against my thigh.

Maria did not look up from her pork rind. Her small thumb pressed steady against the iron pick. Lloyd shifted his left shoulder against the pine crate, his eyes open, staring up at the raw spruce lagging above his head, black with mold.

"Rations for four days if we cut the bread to three ounces," I said.

Lloyd did not turn his head. "We go down today."

"The drift is clear," Maria said. She set the iron pick on the keg, picked up a damp scrap of linen, and wiped the pork grease from her knuckles. "The air is cool enough at the bottom. It smells of wet lime, but it stays in the lungs."

I stood up, hooked my leather tool belt around my waist, and pulled the iron buckle tight into the third hole. I checked the gear hanging from the frog: five iron pitons, a two-pound steel hammer, a ball of chalk line, a brass rule, and my oilcloth notebook with the charcoal stub tucked into the binding.

Lloyd pulled his boots on, pulled the leather thongs tight across the instep, and tied them in double knots. He stood up, six feet tall, his broad shoulders crowding the five-foot clearance of the staging cell ceiling. He took the six-foot iron crowbar from the corner timber and rested the square shank against his right shoulder.

We stepped out of the staging cell and into the tunnel heading east.

***

The chapel sat forty yards along the east drift, down a five-degree slope cut through solid grey limestone. The air grew colder as we descended, damp with groundwater that trickled down the rock walls in thin, dark veins. The tunnel opened into the chamber without a timber collar or an iron set.

We entered in single file. I went first, holding the oil-wick lamp high in my right hand. Lloyd followed three paces behind with the bar. Maria took the left flank, keeping two paces off my elbow, carrying her own iron hand-lantern.

I stopped at the threshold and held the lamp up to sweep the beam across the vault.

The room was rectangular, thirty-two feet long from the west portal to the east wall, and eighteen feet wide from north to south. The roof was vaulted, formed by four semi-circular stone arches springing from low wall piers. The masonry was smooth grey limestone, laid dry without mortar, fitted tight at the joints. At the far end of the chamber, raised on a two-inch stone dais, sat an altar. It was a single hewn block of dark granite, six feet long, three feet deep, and three feet high. Its top surface was flat, unpolished, and bare.

"Check the piers," I said.

I turned left and walked to the northwest pier. I placed my right palm flat against the ashlar. The stone was dry and cold as ice. I drew my brass rule from my belt, measured the pier face—eighteen inches wide—and marked a small chalk cross at the center of the base block.

I moved south across the chamber floor. The floor was made of rectangular limestone flagstones, each two feet by three feet, laid in alternating courses. I counted twelve flags from the west wall to the center aisle.

Lloyd walked to the altar. He stood on the south side of the granite block, set the iron shoe of his crowbar on the floor, and leaned his weight against the shank. He looked down at the stone surface.

"Nothing on it," Lloyd said.

Maria walked the southern wall. She held her hand-lantern eight inches from the masonry, tracking the horizontal bed joints between the courses.

"No fissures," Maria said. "No timber decay. The arches are taking the load cleanly."

I walked the entire perimeter of the chamber once, taking thirty-eight paces around the walls. I returned to the entry threshold, opened my notebook, and wrote down the dimensions: thirty-two feet length, eighteen feet width, four stone arches, one granite altar against the east wall, floor intact.

"First sweep complete," I said. "We run it again."

***

We stepped back out to the tunnel mouth, waited sixty seconds by my pocket watch, and entered the chapel a second time.

I kept the oil-wick lamp ahead of me, tracing the exact line of my previous footings. I walked four paces forward, stopped at the first set of floor flags, and raised the lamp toward the altar.

The granite block stood in the same position against the east wall. But on the flat top of the altar, three tallow candles were set in a straight line along the front edge. They had not been there six minutes ago, nor sixty seconds ago. The wax was yellowed, unlit, with short black wicks pointing upward. Two iron candle-holders lay on their sides next to the middle candle, rotated ninety degrees from the altar axis.

I stopped. "The altar."

Lloyd walked past my right side. He stepped up onto the stone dais and reached out his left hand, touching the cold wax of the center candle.

"They weren't here," Lloyd said.

"They were not here on the first pass," I said.

I swung the light to the right, toward the south wall between the second and third vault arches.

A stone column stood four feet off the south pier. It was three feet thick, rounded, built of stacked drum sections of coarse grey sandstone. The middle drum section was fractured vertically, a jagged split two inches wide running from the joint down into the floor flag. The stone around the fracture was splintered, with sharp flakes of grit resting on the base ring. The air around it smelled of damp sand and fresh lime dust.

On our first sweep, that space between the second and third arches had been an open wall bay with bare masonry.

I stepped closer to the column. I extended my brass rule and inserted the tip into the split. The rule went in four inches before hitting solid stone.

"This column was not in this bay," I said. "The south wall was open."

Maria walked up behind me. She raised her hand-lantern, casting light over the sandstone drums and the splintered split. She stepped past me, touched the smooth face of the column, and looked along the vault arch above it.

"The room is unchanged, Edie," Maria said. Her voice was flat, warm, and even. She adjusted the wool shawl wrapped around her shoulders with her left hand. "The column has been here. The arches rest on it."

"It was not here three minutes ago," I said. "I logged four wall piers and open bays. There were no sandstone columns."

"You missed it on the draft," Maria said. She turned back toward the altar, her face steady in the yellow lantern light. "The room is as it was."

I looked at Lloyd. He was staring at the splintered split in the sandstone drum. His face was set hard, his broken nose crooked against the lamplight. He did not agree with her, but he did not speak. He kept his left hand tight on his iron crowbar.

***

"We sweep a third time," I said.

We turned and walked back to the west portal. We stepped into the drift, waited until the second hand on my pocket watch made one full revolution, and stepped back into the chapel.

I did not look at the arches first. I dropped my eyes to the floor flagstones directly in front of my boots.

I paced forward in three-foot increments, counting the courses of limestone flags. One, two, three, four.

At the fifth course, two feet to the left of the center line, there was a border of small ceramic mosaic tiles embedded between the flagstones. On the first pass, the border had been a continuous geometric pattern of salt-glazed blue and white squares, six inches wide.

Now, a tile was missing from the inner row.

I knelt on the cold floor. The missing square left a shallow depression, two inches by two inches, filled with dry, powdery grey mortar. The mortar bed showed the square imprint where the tile had sat, but the edges of the imprint were sharp, uncrumbled, as if the tile had been lifted clean out a second ago. There was no loose ceramic debris on the surrounding flags.

I took my charcoal pencil from my notebook binding. I placed the notebook on my knee and wrote: *Pass 3. Chancel floor, fifth course, left of aisle. One blue-glazed mosaic tile missing. Mortar bed exposed, dry, crisp imprint. Discrepancy logged.*

I stood up and pointed down at the floor with my boot toe. "Look here."

Maria stopped next to me. She looked down at the empty square in the mosaic border.

"It is a standard floor," Maria said. She did not look at my notebook. "The tile was lost years ago, Edie. The floor has not altered since we stepped down from the drift."

"The mortar is fresh grey dust," I said. "It has no foot-grime in the pocket. It was covered sixty seconds ago."

"It is unchanged," Maria repeated. She looked directly at me, her scarred forearms visible where her sleeves were pinned back. Her tone did not rise. It was clear and simple, the voice she used when speaking to patients in the ward. "We are looking at the same masonry we surveyed when we entered."

I closed the notebook, slipped the pencil back into the leather loop, and pulled the oilcloth strap tight.

"It is not the same," I said. "I have it written."

***

Lloyd moved away from the altar, walking back toward the center aisle between Maria and me. He was carrying the iron crowbar across his chest with both hands, his eyes fixed on the ceiling vault between the first and second arches.

"There is no sound," Lloyd said.

He took one step forward with his right foot.

Half a second before it hit, the pulse came through my teeth—the sudden, mechanical jump in the muscle behind my jaws that means violence is already in the air. It was not a voice and it was not a picture. It was just the certainty of iron dropping through timber.

There was no time to pull him back. I could not move my hand six inches before the line was cut.

A shearing force passed through the air.

There was no sound of cracking stone, no rush of wind, no flash of flame, and no movement in the masonry. A flat, invisible plane cut across the space six inches to the right of Lloyd's body, five feet above the floor flags.

The force struck Lloyd's right arm.

It sliced through his heavy wool sleeve, through the muscle, tendon, and bone of his forearm, four inches below the elbow joint. The cut was instant and clean, as if a two-ton hydraulic shear had dropped through the air and lifted clear in the same millisecond.

Lloyd's lower right arm and hand detached from his elbow.

The severed arm dropped straight down. It hit the flagstones with a wet thud, the fingers flexing once against the stone before going limp.

Blood gushed from the open stump above the elbow, splashing across Lloyd's trousers and pooling on the dry limestone floor.

Lloyd did not fall. He did not scream or drop the crowbar. He shifted his weight, caught the falling iron bar with his left hand, and brought the stump of his right arm tight against his ribs. He clamped his left forearm over the exposed bone and artery, pinning the meat against his side to check the bleed.

His face turned grey as slate. Sweat broke out across his forehead and upper lip in heavy drops. He stood rigid, his jaw set so tight the muscles in his cheeks twitched, but he held the stump steady and said nothing.

"Lloyd," I said.

I dropped my lamp onto the flagstones. The oil spilled, burning with a sharp smell of paraffin, but the flame stayed lit in the bronze cup. I reached into my belt pouch for a linen field dressing and my iron tourniquet rod.

Before I could take a step, Maria was already there.

She dropped her hand-lantern. She moved with absolute speed, jumping across the two paces of open stone floor between them. She did not look at the severed arm lying on the flagstones.

She grabbed Lloyd's right upper arm with both hands. She pressed her bare palms directly over the raw, bleeding stump, driving her fingers into the flesh around the severed artery to stem the spurting blood.

"Hold still," Maria said. Her voice was rapid, firm, and entirely quiet. "Hold steady, Lloyd. Stay on your feet."

Lloyd did not answer. He pressed his back against the nearest stone pier and kept his eyes fixed straight ahead at the west doorway.

Maria closed her eyes. She drew a deep breath through her nose, held it for two seconds, and began to murmur a low, rhythmic chant in her throat. The words were flat and repetitive, spoken without emphasis.

A warm, steady golden glow began to emanate from beneath her scarred palms.

The light grew stronger, spreading through the fabric of her sleeves and illuminating the wet crimson on Lloyd's torn coat. The smell of burnt copper and iron rose heavy in the air between them, hot and sickeningly sweet.

Underneath her hands, the flesh of the stump began to churn.

I stepped closer, keeping my boots clear of the blood pool. I watched the wound the way I watched a timber frame taking a fresh load.

The severed blood vessels pulled back into the muscle tissue. The white circle of the sliced humerus bone began to extend outward, granular calcium forming in seconds, building a new shaft of bone down from the elbow. Muscle fibres redrew themselves over the new bone like red thread pulling through canvas. Nerve bundles knitted together in fine white webs. Skin folded out over the growing tissue, pale and pink, stretching forward until it capped the end of the new forearm.

The golden light flared once, bright enough to throw hard shadows of the arches against the east wall, and then subsided.

Maria let her breath out in a ragged gasp. She pulled her hands back from Lloyd's arm. Her shoulders dropped, and she leaned her hip against the stone pier to stay upright. Her face was pale, covered in a thin sheen of sweat.

"It is done," Maria whispered.

Lloyd lowered his left arm from his chest.

A new right arm extended from his elbow. It was complete—forearm, wrist, palm, and five fingers. The skin was smooth, pale, and free of the old scars he had carried on his knuckles.

But the new arm surfaced crooked.

The forearm bones—the radius and ulna—were set at a visible five-degree inward angle toward his hip. The elbow joint was thick and misaligned, the bone cap protruding slightly too far to the lateral side. The forearm did not hang straight from the shoulder; it turned inward, the hand resting unnaturally across the front of his thigh.

Lloyd raised the new right arm. He flexed the five fingers. They opened and closed slowly, stiffly, the knuckles popping with a sharp dry sound. He rotated the wrist halfway to the left, then back to the right. The movement was jerked, the misaligned joint binding at the limit of its turn.

He looked down at the crooked limb for five seconds. He watched the movement of the fingers as they closed again.

He offered no comment. He did not ask Maria what she had done, nor did he look at the old severed arm that still lay in the pool of blood on the floor flags three feet away.

He reached down with his new right hand, gripped the iron crowbar by its middle shaft, and lifted it off the stone. His grip was solid, though his wrist stayed bent at the odd angle. He rested the iron bar back against his right shoulder.

"The drift shaft," Lloyd said.

"Yes," I said. "We check the depth."

***

I turned away from the blood pool and walked back to the west portal. The vertical shaft opened eight feet outside the chapel entry, dropping straight down into the lower drift through a cut timber collar.

I unbuckled the leather strap holding the knot-cord reel to my belt.

The knot-cord was five-millimeter braided hemp, fitted with a heavy brass sink-weight at the lead end. Along its length, brass wire ferrules were whipped into the braid at precise one-metre intervals to serve as mechanical depth stops.

I stepped to the edge of the shaft opening, braced my boots against the oak curbing, and lowered the brass sink-weight into the dark opening.

The cord ran out through my fingers. The flat scars across my palms felt the brass ferrules tick past one by one. One metre. Two metres. Three metres.

Beside the shaft frame, a mechanical pulley was mounted on a cast-iron bracket. The cord passed through the pulley's grooved wheel, which turned an internal brass clicker-gauge with a three-digit counter window.

I watched the brass counter wheel turn as the cord dropped.

Click. Click. Click.

Prior to entering the chapel, I had surveyed this vertical shaft twice. The distance from the timber collar of the upper drift to the stone landing of the lower drift was twelve metres. I had recorded twelve metres in my notebook on two separate shifts. The timber framing of the shaft was rigid, bolted into solid limestone with four-inch iron lag screws.

The cord kept running.

Eight metres. Nine metres. Ten metres. Eleven metres. Twelve metres.

The brass weight did not strike the bottom landing. The cord stayed taut, pulling downward under the gravity of the lead core inside the brass weight.

Thirteen metres. Fourteen metres. Fifteen metres.

I did not release the line. I kept my right thumb pressed against the hemp, feeling the brass ferrules slip past the skin.

Sixteen metres. Seventeen metres. Eighteen metres.

The line slackened. The brass weight struck stone below.

The mechanical clicker-gauge on the iron bracket stopped turning. The black numbers in the brass window settled into alignment.

Nineteen metres.

I held the cord still. I pulled it up three inches until the weight cleared the bottom, then let it drop back to feel the thud of the lead against the floor below. The line hit bottom at exactly the nineteen-metre mark.

I looked down into the dark shaft. The air rising from the opening was motionless, cold, and stale, carrying the smell of dry dust and iron scrap.

"The shaft was twelve metres," I said.

Maria stood behind my left shoulder. She did not lean over the timber collar to look down.

"The gauge says nineteen," Maria said. "The line is resting on the bottom."

"The shaft has not been recut," I said. "The timber sets are six feet apart. There are two sets below us. That is twelve feet of timbering, not sixty."

"The weight is on the floor," Maria said. Her voice remained steady, flat, unhurried. "The reading is nineteen metres."

I pulled the oilcloth notebook from my belt. I opened it to the last blank page.

I took the charcoal pencil and pressed the point against the paper. I wrote down the number: *19m*.

Underneath the number, I wrote: *Shaft depth measured at 19 metres by brass clicker-gauge and knot-cord. Previous survey established 12 metres. No structural excavation present. Line length inconsistent with physical timbering.*

I stared at the cord's nineteen-metre mark, the number feeling wrong and out of place in the stale air.
