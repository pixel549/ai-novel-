I stepped through the peeling doorway of the Stillwell Hydro sanatorium. The smell of iodine cut the air. The sea wind brushed past the open frame.

I handed the clerk a slip of paper signed by Senator Ferrick. I watched him open the ledger on the desk. He counted numbers on a lined paper. He wrote the sum with ink. He closed the ledger. I pocketed the receipt. The clerk nodded and left the desk.

I walked down the slate corridor. The floor was gray. The walls were metal panels. The lights flickered. I walked toward the east end. The corridor ended at a dock. Three divers waited there. They had packs on shoulders. They were already ready. The dock was wooden. It leaned against the sea. The brine-filled entry tube stood in the center of the dock. The tube was tall. Its surface was slick.

I was introduced to Lloyd Hale. He was broad-shouldered. His right arm appeared intact. He wore a work shirt. His boots were heavy. I met Maria Vesk. She was wiry. Her forearms had faint scorch marks. Her hair was short. She carried a small lantern. The lantern glowed faintly. I watched her scan the intake log on a board. The log was in black ink. The numbers were clear.

Lloyd asked me if I had ever been in a hydro-treatment before. I replied "No." The answer was simple. Lloyd nodded once. His eyes flicked to Maria. Maria confirmed my answer by marking the log. The confirmation was a small tick. The tick was in red ink.

The three of us lined up at the entry. Lloyd took the lead. He secured heavy shoring gear. The gear was made of steel. It weighed many kilos. He strapped it to his shoulders. Maria stepped to the side. She carried the lantern. She held it above her head. I carried cable spools. The spools were wrapped in rope. I also carried the spare breather mask. The mask was plastic. I attached the mask to my belt.

I strapped the cable reels to my belt. I tightened the straps. I checked the pressure gauge on the dive suit. The gauge read 4 psi. The gauge was a small brass dial. I stepped into the water-filled chamber. The hatch opened. The hatch was metal. The hatch was heavy. I pressed the latch. The hatch sealed. The seal made a click. The water rushed in. I felt the cold.

The divers descended in a synchronized rhythm. They moved in step. Their breathing was regular. The brine swirled around my boots. The brine was thick. The tube narrowed. The walls of the tube were metal. The tube was long. It was dark inside. The tube had a small light at the bottom. The light was dim. I felt the water against my feet. I kept my breath steady.

I surfaced in the main treatment pool. The pool was large. It was metal. The water was still. The other divers were already beginning the first assessment of the patient’s condition. They checked the patient’s vitals. They noted the patient’s breathing. They measured pressure inside the patient’s suit. They made a small mark on a sheet. The sheet was in a clip. The sheet was white.

The brine settled. The cold light reflected off the metal. The metal was bright. The light was sharp. The light came from a lamp at the side. The lamp was a fluorescent bulb. The light was low. The light made a cold shine on the metal. We waited for the next patient to be lowered. The door to the dock opened. A new patient was ready. The patient wore a new suit. The patient had a new mask. The patient was ready to enter. The patient was in line.
