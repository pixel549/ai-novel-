I stepped through the stone arch of Stillwell Hydro. The arch was eight feet tall, four feet wide, made of rough limestone. The paint on the outer face peeled in flakes, exposing mottled stone. A gust of wind cut across the opening and carried a sharp iodine scent that clung to the air. The air beyond the arch was humid and cool, the scent of old pipes and iron.

The hall beyond the arch was a long rectangle, about one hundred feet in length and thirty feet wide. Floor panels were laid in a checkerboard pattern of dark oak and light oak. Light filtered through high windows in the west wall, slanted in from the sky. In the centre of the hall, a straight desk stood. It was a long oak desk with a brass lamp on a side table and a ledger box on the right. A man in a navy shirt and a cap sat behind the desk. He was the clerk.

I approached the desk, placed a signed voucher on the surface. The voucher was thick paper, embossed with a seal. I slid the voucher under the clerk's right hand. The clerk opened the ledger box, pulled a pen, and scrolled through the entries. He checked an account number, then tapped a button on the ledger machine. A line of numbers appeared on the screen. The clerk pressed the debit key, and the screen blinked. The clerk did not look up. He closed the ledger box. The clerk turned back to the desk and returned my voucher to the stack on the left. The clerk did not speak. He had completed the transaction, debiting Senator Ferrick's household account. He had not mentioned the Senator. The clerk did not notice my face.

The clerk lifted a hand and pointed toward a long lounge to the right of the hall. The lounge was a room of about thirty by forty feet, its walls lined with lockers and a long table. A large digital schedule board hung on the east wall, showing a timetable in large black digits. The board read: Run 1: 10:00, Run 2: 10:15, Run 3: 10:30, Run 4: 10:45, Run 5: 11:00. The board showed three lines of patients: a first line, a second line, a third line, each consisting of five people. The lounge had a long line of chairs, each chair set at a distance of ten feet from the next. The air in the lounge smelled faintly of antiseptic.

In the first line, a man stood at the head. He was broad-shouldered, his right arm resting on the back of his chair. His nose was scarred, the scar a jagged line that ran from the bridge to the base. He had a thick beard, and his left hand held a clipboard. He was Lloyd Hale. He stepped out of the line, the right foot lifting before the left. He moved a distance of two feet forward and then took a step of about one foot. He moved toward the group that was forming in front of me, the group I was about to be in.

Following Lloyd stepped a woman. She was wiry, with burn scars on both forearms that ran from elbow to wrist. She wore a simple shirt and trousers, her hair short and tied back. She was Maria Vesk. She moved at a pace of about one foot per second, her eyes fixed on the floor. She joined Lloyd and stood beside him.

A staff member approached us. He was a tall man with a shaved head and a badge. He had a clipboard in his right hand. He moved forward, a distance of about one foot, and stood between Lloyd and myself. He spoke. He said, "You are assigned to my group for this run. This is a prearranged pairing." He did not repeat the words for the run schedule. He did not mention the run number. He did not ask any question. He turned back to the lounge.

Lloyd turned his head toward me. His voice was low and direct. He said, "Never been here before?" I looked at him, did not respond. I answered, "No." He nodded, a slight upward tilt of his head. He accepted my response, did not ask a follow‑up question. He turned to Maria and left. He walked a distance of three feet to the side of Maria.

Maria then turned to me. She spoke in a quiet voice, "First time?" I answered, "No." She lifted her right hand, held a small pen, and wrote my answer in a ledger in front of us. She looked at me, then at the ledger, then at Lloyd. She pressed the pen down again, confirmed the pairing. She did not say a word about the clerk or the voucher. She moved a small step back, a distance of two feet.

A staff member stepped forward. He was a younger man, in a lab coat. He held a set of harnesses in his left hand and a coil of cable in his right. He walked toward the preparation room. He moved a distance of about ten feet to the doorway. The doorway was a steel door of thirty by forty feet, its frame black and rusted. He opened the door with a push of his right shoulder. He entered the preparation room with the door closing behind him. He turned to me and nodded.

The preparation room was a square of twenty by twenty feet. The floor was bare concrete. A large steam basin sat in the centre, a cylinder of steel six feet tall and four feet wide. The basin was surrounded by a wooden frame. Two heavy iron doors flanked the basin. A harness rack held five harnesses in a row. A lamp with a yellow bulb hung from a metal arm above each harness. The room was dim, lit only by the lamps.

Lloyd entered the room with a heavy iron door in his left hand. He held the door at a distance of two feet from the wall. The door was six feet tall and three feet wide, its surface polished black. Lloyd hefted the door, exerting a force that matched the weight. He held the door at arm's length, the metal edges rubbing against his left forearm. He set the door down at a distance of one foot from the side wall.

Maria moved to the lamps. She was wiry, her movement efficient. She stepped forward, a distance of about two feet. She reached for a lamp, used her right hand to tighten a screw. She repeated the action for the lamps on the right side of the harness rack. She turned her head from the harness rack to the lamps, then back. She moved a distance of about one foot each time.

I entered the preparation room. I moved forward a distance of three feet from the doorway to the centre of the room. I looked at the harnesses. I inspected each harness for holes, for broken straps, for loose screws. I moved my hands through the harnesses, feeling for any damage. I marked a small notch on a harness with a pencil, a small dot that would not be seen from outside. I walked to the side of the harness rack, then stepped back, a distance of two feet.

I approached the consent form. The consent form was a piece of thick white paper, two pages, with printed lines and a space for my signature. I held the form with both hands, placed my right hand on the signature line. I signed the form with a quick motion of the pen. I did not write a reason. I wrote only my name and the date. I slid the signed form into a folder, closed the folder, and placed it on a small table near the harness rack.

The staff member returned. He stood at a distance of two feet from me, held a pad of ink. He lifted the pad, pressed it to a small paper. He slid the paper to me, and I took the paper in my left hand. I looked at it for a moment, then slid it into the folder. I closed the folder. The staff member left the room, moving a distance of five feet to the door. He closed the door with a push. The door shut with a thud, the metal frame rubbing against the floor.

The door sealed. The steam basin began to billow. Steam rose from the basin, a cloud of white that filled the room. The heat rose from the basin, a temperature of about sixty degrees above ambient. The heat pressed against my skin. The room filled with a steady hiss of steam. Steam swirled around us, and the first pulse of heat pressed against my skin.
