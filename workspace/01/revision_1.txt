The outer framing of the Stillwell Hydro sanatorium was grey pine, three-by-eight joists set into rubble masonry that had begun to settle toward the channel. Salt damp had taken the bottom four courses of stone. The mortar was soft enough to pick out with a knife blade. White lead paint on the double doors was peeling in long strips where the sea wind caught the grain.

I stepped through the doorway. The wind off the water was six knots out of the east, carrying salt spray and coal smoke from the harbour tugs. Inside, the air smelled of iodine, carbolic, and wet wool.

The entrance hall was thirty feet deep by twenty wide. The floor was laid in grey slate flags, four inches thick, set on a bed of crushed furnace slag. Two gas brackets burned on the north wall, the flames turned down low to save gas. The ceiling was lath and plaster, water-stained around the brass downpipes from the roof gutters.

I closed the door behind me. The latch fell into the keeper with a flat iron clank that sounded double in my right ear. The left ear gave back nothing. That was from a five-pound camouflet charge under the redoubt at Carrowgate seven years back.

The clerk sat behind an oak counter six feet long, topped with galvanized iron sheet to protect against the salt water off the wet coats. Behind him, three shelves held pine boxes marked by month and year.

I walked up to the counter and pulled the paper from my inner pocket. The slip was four inches by six, heavy vellum, stamped with Senator Ferrick’s mark in red ink at the bottom corner. The ink was dry. Ferrick had signed it two hours ago in his office on South Street.

"Ferrick," I said. "Household account."

The clerk was forty, pale, with ink stains around his thumbnail and a pair of steel-rimmed spectacles tied to his buttonhole with black thread. He picked up the slip, held it under the gas jet, and checked the red mark with his thumb.

He opened a leather ledger three inches thick. The pages were ruled in blue ink for pounds, shillings, and pence. He ran his index finger down the column under the letter F until he hit Ferrick’s line.

"Eighteen shillings for the entry fee," the clerk said. "Two shillings for the mask rental. Fourpence for the brine clearance."

He wrote the date in the left margin with a dip pen. He took a brass stamp from the ink pad, pressed it into the ledger page, then stamped the corner of my slip. He tallied the three sums in the right-hand column: twenty shillings and fourpence.

"Draw against the Senator’s quarterly retainer," I said.

He checked the line again, nodded once, and made an entry in red ink. He blotted the page with a square of felt, tore a slip of yellow receipt paper from a pad on his right, and stamped it twice.

"Receipt," he said, and laid it on the zinc counter.

I picked it up, folded it across the middle, and slid it into my breast pocket beside my pay book.

A door behind the counter opened onto a slate corridor three feet six wide. The walls were painted white to five feet, red lead from there to the ceiling joists. Exposed copper pipes ran overhead, five-eighths inch diameter, carrying warm water down from the boilers in the cellar. Every twelve feet, a brass pipe strap held the line against the timber headers.

I walked down the hallway. My boots made a steady dry thud on the slate. The floor sloped five degrees downward toward the sea face.

At the end of forty yards, the corridor opened onto the immersion dock.

The dock was sixty feet wide and forty deep, built over the high-water line on eight-inch creosoted pine piles. The smell of iodine was stronger here, mixed with the greasy iron smell of steam pumps. A two-inch steam line hummed in the corner where it entered a cast-iron heat exchanger.

In the center of the floor stood the entry tube. It was six feet across, constructed of half-inch rolled steel plate held together by double rows of five-eighths rivets, three inches on center. The top rim sat three feet above the floorboards, fitted with a hinged cast-iron hatch frame and eight heavy swing-bolts.

Three divers stood by the lip. They wore three-ply canvas suits treated with rubber solution, lead-soled boots with brass toe-caps, and heavy leather weight belts fitted with five-pound lead blocks. One diver had his brass collar already bolted down. The second was pulling the rubber wrist gaskets over his cuffs. The third held a heavy canvas pack across his thighs, checking the copper valves on his breathing hose.

They moved together without speaking. The diver with the collar stepped onto the ladder inside the tube rim, lowered his weight into the brine, and let go. The water slapped against the steel plate. The second diver followed six seconds later. The third cleared his hose, stepped over the lip, and dropped down.

Two people stood near the gear bench on the south wall.

The man was six foot, heavy through the shoulders, wearing a brown corduroy coat with the elbows patched in calfskin. His hair was dark and cropped short, cut close to the scalp with a pocket knife by the look of the edges. His nose was broad, broken twice, the bridge set slightly to the left. His hands were broad across the back, the knuckles swollen from old work or fighting, two fingers on the right hand not straightening all the way.

His right sleeve was down to his wrist. The hand looked intact from six feet away—five fingers, fingernails trimmed short, skin dark from weather.

The woman beside him was smaller, five foot two or three, wiry through the arms and shoulders. She wore a grey wool dress tucked into a leather apron, her fair hair cut short at the jaw to keep it out of gear straps. Her sleeves were rolled back three turns below the elbow. Pale, shiny burn scars ran across both forearms from the wrists up toward the joint, the skin puckered in tight white ridges where old heat had tightened it.

I walked over to the bench.

"Edith Marrow," I said. "Ferrick’s detail."

The man turned his head. His eyes were small, dark, set deep under heavy brow ridges. He looked at my boots, then my hands, then my face.

"Lloyd Hale," he said.

His voice was low, flat, with the thick back-valley sound in the vowels. He didn't offer a hand. He stood with his feet eight inches apart, his weight balanced over his heels, his arms hanging straight down at his sides.

The woman stepped forward. She had a wooden clipboard under her left arm and a pencil stuck behind her right ear.

"Maria Vesk," she said. She smiled, but her eyes stayed on the checklist attached to the board. "You're the sapper."

"Fourth Tunnelling," I said. "Four years."

She nodded once, writing a mark on the top sheet. Her fingers were quick, thin, the skin around her nails clean and short.

I looked at Hale's right hand again. It hung resting against his thigh. It didn't shake. He didn't tuck it into his pocket or hide it behind his hip.

Hale looked down at the floorboards between us. A drain grate sat two feet from his left boot, cast iron with six-inch slots to clear overflow water.

"You ever been down in a hydro-treatment before?" he asked.

"No," I said.

Hale nodded once. It was a short, sharp movement of his chin that stopped before his neck moved. He turned his eyes sideways toward Maria.

Maria reached for the pencil behind her ear, pulled it down, and ran the blunt end down the column of names on the log sheet.

"Edith Marrow," Maria read off. "Fourth Company, Sappers. Clearance signed by Ferrick’s desk this morning. Paid for entry, mask, and line."

She made a heavy checkmark in blue lead pencil in the margin next to my name.

"Log is clear," Maria said to Hale. "She’s down as third hand for the run."

Hale didn't look back at me. He looked at the equipment rack behind the bench.

"Right," Hale said.

"We take the line in order," Hale said. "I take the head. Maria takes flank and light. You take the spools and the spare."
