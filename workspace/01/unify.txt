I stepped through the peeling doorway of the Stillwell Hydro sanatorium, the smell of iodine sharp against the cold sea wind. 

I handed the clerk the slip signed by Senator Ferrick. He checked the household account, tallied the sum in his ledger, and gave me the receipt. I pocketed it.

The slate corridor led down to the dock. Three divers were already shouldering their packs and slipping into the brine-filled entry tube. I was introduced to Lloyd Hale—broad through the shoulders, heavy-built, with a right arm that looked whole enough from where I stood—and to Maria Vesk, a wiry woman whose forearms bore faint scorch marks under her sleeves. 

Lloyd asked if I had ever been in a hydro-treatment before.

"No," I said.

Lloyd nodded once, his eyes flicking to Maria. She scanned the intake log clipped to her board and confirmed my answer with a mark.

We lined up. Lloyd took the lead, securing the heavy steel shoring gear across his back. Maria stepped to the side with a small brass lantern, and I took the cable spools and the spare breather mask. I strapped the reels to my belt, checked the pressure gauge on the dive suit—four pounds—and stepped into the chamber. The dog-latches dropped and the hatch sealed.

The divers descended in a synchronized rhythm. The brine swirled around my boots, cold and dense, as the iron tube narrowed into the dark.

I surfaced in the main treatment pool. Lloyd and Maria were already at the edge, beginning the first assessment of the patient's condition.

The brine settled. Cold light reflected off the metal as we waited for the next patient to be lowered.
