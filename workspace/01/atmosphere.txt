I stepped through the stone arch of Stillwell Hydro. The limestone was grey and salt-bitten, the outer coat of lead paint peeling away in long, pale curls. A sharp wind cut off the river, carrying coal ash and a wet iodine scent that clung to the back of the throat.

At the desk, the clerk sat behind a heavy oak rail worn smooth by grease and coat sleeves. An ink-stained ledger lay open before him, ruled in red. I handed over a signed voucher on thick cream stock. He took it, checked the embossed wax seal against his index card, and debited Senator Ferrick’s household account without his knowledge. The steel nib scratched twice into the parchment.

The clerk pointed a yellowed finger toward the lounge. On the far wall, a framed board of dark pine listed the schedule in white chalk, showing that runs would start in threes. Three separate lines of patients sat or stood along the damp pine wainscoting. The gas brackets hissed overhead, sputtering against a draft from the alley door.

Lloyd stepped out of the first line. He was broad-shouldered, thick through the frame, his dark hair hacked short with a blade. His nose had been set wrong, crooked twice across the bridge. Maria followed him—wiry, small-built, wearing a plain grey habit with the sleeves turned back to reveal old burn scars running flat and pale across her forearms.

A staff member in a greasy linen apron stepped between us with a cracked slate clipboard. He checked three names against a column, assigned Lloyd and Maria to my group, and stated it was the prearranged pairing for this run.

Lloyd turned his head toward me, his boots heavy on the wet flagging. "Never been here before?" he asked.

"No," I said.

He nodded once, accepting the answer, and looked down at his own thick knuckles.

Maria looked up from her paper slate. Her eyes were dark-rimmed with tired skin. She repeated the question: "First time?"

"No," I said.

She recorded my answer on her sheet with a stub of black charcoal, confirming the pairing.

Two attendants escorted us into the preparation room. The space smelled of boiled zinc and damp masonry. In the centre, two men were setting up the central steam basin, tightening the iron unions with heavy wrenches and fitting the braided copper feed pipes. Lloyd went to the doorframe, bent his legs, and hefted the iron door onto its heavy pintles. Maria moved along the wall brackets, adjusting the glass oil lamps so the yellow light fell square across the stone work floor. I walked over to the timber rack to inspect the harnesses, checking the canvas webbing for fraying, testing the iron buckles against my thumb, and running my palm down every load-bearing seam to feel for soft spots in the stitch work.

An attendant held out the consent form on a birch lapboard. The paper was coarse, smelling of vinegar and wood pulp. I took the steel pen and signed my name on the bottom line, noting only that I was entering. I left the lower column blank, stating no reason for wanting a chalice.

The iron door swung home and sealed shut into the mortared jamb with a dull thud. The central steam basin began to billow white vapour through the floor grate, wet and heavy with the taste of crushed minerals. The heat pressed hard against my skin as the run started.
