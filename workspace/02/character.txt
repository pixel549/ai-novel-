I stepped off the bottom stone stair onto the floor. White salt had crusted along the flags, calcified in grey ridges where moisture had pooled and dried over time. Low light came from gas jets mounted high on the walls, burning dim and yellow. Above us, the stone arches caught the sound of our boots and sent it back in flat, heavy echoes. Lloyd walked two paces behind my right shoulder. Maria stood off my right elbow, her dark coat pulled tight across her chest.

We followed the stone-lined passage. The walls were constructed of dry-laid ashlar blocks, six inches thick, fitted without mortar in the vertical joints. The corridor ran straight for forty yards before opening into a broader space.

It was a vaulted chamber, built in the shape of a small chapel. The ceiling rose twenty feet into a pointed groin arch supported by four square pillars of red masonry. At the far wall, a heavy brass door six feet wide and eight feet high blocked access to the next level down. The brass was green with verdigris around the hinge pins and structural rivets. There was no latch handle on our side, only a raised central boss with a keyway three inches across.

I walked up to the brass door and placed both palms flat against the metal. The scarred tissue across my hands registered the cold before the weight gave way. I pushed. The door swung inward on grease-slick hinges without a sound.

Beyond the threshold, the floor did not drop. A long, straight corridor extended into the dimness ahead. We stepped through into the passage.

As soon as my boots crossed the frame, the geometry of the hall changed. The side walls remained ten feet apart, but the far end of the corridor receded instantly. I kept my stride steady at three feet per step, eighty steps to the minute, but the wall joints stopped passing at a normal rate. We walked for two minutes by the count of my pulse, yet the journey felt like two full days. A sharp, gnawing hunger opened in my stomach as if I had gone without food for forty-eight hours. The air grew dense around us. Time blurred at the edges of my vision, turning the movement of Lloyd’s boots into long, smeared tracks across the flagstones.

I slowed my pace and held up my right hand to halt the line.

Ten yards ahead, something slid along the left wall. It was thin, six feet from end to end, covered in a grey carapace that looked like wet slate. It had six jointed limbs, each fitted with two elbows that bent in opposing directions, allowing it to move flat against the vertical masonry. It did not touch the floor. It shifted sideways across the stone courses, its weight distributed so evenly that the ashlar did not creak under the load. A quiet, steady hiss came from a split in its forward head as it drew itself down toward us.

Lloyd did not wait for an order. He stepped past my left shoulder—my deaf side—and lunged straight at the wall.

He went in bare-handed, his shoulders hunching as he closed the gap in two heavy strides. He drove his right fist forward into the creature's central plate. The thin limbs writhed instantly, four jointed elbows snapping around to pin his forearm, the needle-sharp tips scraping against his heavy wool sleeve. Lloyd grunted, set his rear boot against a flagstone seam, and jammed his fingers into the soft joint beneath its main shell.

Maria moved up on Lloyd's right flank, stopping four feet back from the masonry.

She lifted her right hand, her sleeve pulled back where pale burn scars covered her skin from wrist to elbow. A sudden pulse of white light flared from her open palm. The light struck the creature squarely in its core. The writhed limbs buckled under the glare, thrashing backward and uncoiling from Lloyd's arm. The force of the light drove the creature three inches off the wall, pinning its upper joints flat against the stone and giving Lloyd room to turn his wrist and lock his fingers into its chest frame.

I stepped back two paces into the open space near the right wall to keep our retreat line clear. I watched the fight unfold. Lloyd held the core; Maria held the light.

My Still Mind clicked. A half-second before the blow came, I knew a blade was moving across my space.

The warning gave me the trajectory, but my physical body was half a step behind the calculation. One of the creature's lower limbs, tipped with a flat bone spur shaped like a sapper's wedge, whipped sideways past Lloyd's shoulder. I tried to pull my right arm back, but the edge brushed my forearm. It sliced through the sleeve and left a shallow red cut across the skin. The blood ran cold against my arm before it began to bleed warm.

Lloyd took advantage of the opening Maria created. He shifted his stance, braced his boot against the wall, and twisted his hand deeper inside the creature’s core.

The outer shell cracked with a sound like dry timber snapping under load. The creature’s limbs went slack all at once. The body slipped off the stone wall and collapsed onto the floor in a tangled pile of black whorls and broken joints. Within three seconds, the solid form broke down into grey grease, then dissipated completely into a thick plume of white steam that rose toward the vaulted ceiling.

The steam cleared slowly into the upper arch.

Maria let her hand fall back to her side and exhaled hard. She wiped her forehead with the back of her wrist.

"I have run this sequence dozens of times," Maria said. "I know the timing."

Lloyd stood over the patch of wet stone where the creature had dissolved. He rubbed his right knuckles against his trousers to clear his grip.

He nodded once. "Third time I've met that thing in the hydro," he said.

I turned away from them to survey the ceiling.

Ten feet back from where the creature had dropped, an iron bracket was anchored into the keystone of the arch. A thick knot-cord, frayed at the ends, hung down from the ceiling fitting, its lower end tied into a four-inch loop that hung six feet above the flags. Heat was building inside the passage, the air thickening as fresh steam hissed from the lower wall vents. As the internal system pressure rose, the tension on the overhead line shifted, and the loop at the bottom of the knot-cord slackened, dropping two inches closer to the floor.

I stepped directly beneath the iron fitting. I reached up with my right hand, caught the thick hemp loop, and set my weight back onto my heels.

I pulled down hard. The line went taut, and the weighted counter balance inside the ceiling housing resisted the draw. I braced my boots against the flags and pulled a second time, putting forty pounds of force on the line until the internal catch released with a heavy iron thud.

Above us, heavy locking pins shot home inside the wall channels. I heard the lock click and the chamber seal, trapping us for the next run.
