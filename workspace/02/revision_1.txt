I stepped down the salt-crusted stairs into a corridor whose arches curved like a chapel.

Dressed granite, sixteen risers at eight inches each, dropping ten feet eight inches below the intake room floor. White salt crusted four inches deep in the corners where the broom missed. The vault overhead rose seven feet, red brick arches springing from square ashlar pillars every four paces. Mortar joints squeezed out in bead form, white salt crystals bleeding through the lime like frost on lead work. Lloyd walked three paces ahead, broad through the shoulders, coat stretched tight over his back. He moved with a heavy, flat-footed stride, his right arm hanging close to his side. Maria took my deaf side, two paces back, keeping clear of my left ear where no sound came through. Iron dishes in wall brackets held small, yellow oil flames that threw our shadows long down the flags, stretching thirty feet into the dark before fading out.

The main line ran straight for eighty yards toward a timber bulkhead fitted with twin iron dogs and a felt seal, but a narrow branch opened on the right wall twenty paces short of the wood. I stepped through the turn first. Three feet wide, six foot two overhead. Dressed stone gave way to coarse red brickwork, soft-burnt and wet to the touch. The ceiling dropped four inches at the lintel. I had not eaten since noon yesterday—four ounces of salt beef and a hard rye crust taken in the yard behind the rail depot. Empty stomach, belt pulled two notches past the third hole. Cold air pressed out from the dark, smelling of wet stone, coal gas, and old brine. We kept moving down the branch. Step after step, forty paces, eighty, one hundred and twenty. The flags underfoot were uneven, laid without sand bedding, tilting two inches under a boot if you took them off-centre. The walls showed no side vents, no weep holes, no drain grates. Lloyd’s tread sounded right behind me, two beats to my three. Maria was behind him, her shoes making almost no sound on the wet flags.

The passage ought to have hit the outer foundation wall of the hydro eighty feet out, matching the footprint on the surface. We passed eighty feet, then two hundred, then three hundred. Driven by hunger, the walk stretched until it felt like two days, pulling far past the surface footprint of the building before flattening out at a T-junction. Space inside the Salts did not lay flat against the earth above it. Air grew thicker, colder, dripping from the arch crowns. My throat tasted of iron and dry salt. To the left, the junction dropped six inches into black shadows; right was blocked solid by dead spoil—shale, crushed lime, and broken pine lagging wedged from floor to ceiling.

Lloyd spotted a thin, many-jointed thing coiling along the far wall and lunged at it with bare fists.

It clung to the damp mortar six feet up, directly opposite the turn—four feet of pale length, no thicker than three-inch haulage cable, six elbowed legs twitching against the brickwork with a sound like dry husks rubbing together in a grain bin. Segmented plates ran down its back, grey as wet ash. Lloyd did not call out. He did not pause to assess the masonry or the reach. My mind twitched half a second before his foot left the flags—that sharp, cold premonition that arrives before iron drops or a timber snaps. I knew the blow was coming before he shifted his weight. He took two long strides across the junction, right boot hitting the centre flag with a heavy thump that cracked through the corridor, and drove his fist straight for the middle segment, aiming to pin it against the mortar.

The creature darted, its limbs snapping, and Lloyd landed a punch that cracked one of its joints.

It reacted before the full weight of his shoulder carried through. Its body kinked like a broken wire. Its six legs snapped against the brickwork, joints popping in rapid order—clack, clack, clack—as it shifted four inches along the mortar line. It was fast, but Lloyd did not pull the strike or adjust his grip. His fist caught the rear segment where the third joint met the main cable of its body. The punch met stone and shell with a dull, wet crack. One of the elbowed joints fractured under his enlarged knuckles, spattering clear gelled fluid across the lime mortar. The creature shrieked—a high, thin hiss like steam escaping an iron relief valve—and coiled back on itself, its remaining legs scraping wild circles against the vertical joints. Lloyd shifted his boots to set up a second blow, his left heel slipping three inches on the wet flagstone.

Maria stepped forward, raised her hand, and a flash of light steadied the creature, pinning it against the stone.

She moved before Lloyd could plant his left foot. She cleared two flags in a single stride, passing my right elbow. Her sleeves were rolled to the mid-forearm, the heavy burn scars from her early unschooled fire-work grey-white against her skin. She raised her right palm flat, thumb tucked tight against the knuckles. No words, no gesture from a book. Arcana came off her like dry heat off a iron stove plate. A sharp, blinding burst of white light erupted from her hand. It carried no flame and no scent of burning, but the radiance flooded the T-junction, cutting away every shadow for ten paces in both directions. The white light struck the creature squarely where it clung to the brick. The creature froze. The glare steadied it where it hung, locking its segmented body in place as if driven through with three-inch masonry pins. Its twitching legs went rigid, pressed flat against the lime mortar, unable to coil or dart.

I watched the strike land, my own reaction lagging, and the shock of the impact reverberates through the corridor.

My mind had registered the violence half a second before Lloyd swung his arm and half a second before Maria raised her hand, but my body stayed anchored two paces back, right boot planted on the flag, hand resting flat on my belt buckle. The gap between knowing a strike is coming and getting a hand up to meet it is always there. It does not shorten, no matter how many years you carry it. Lloyd took advantage of Maria’s light. With the creature pinned rigid against the brickwork, he drove his left fist squarely into its centre segment. The blow landed with full force. The shock of the impact reverberated through the corridor, bouncing off the brick vaulting, rattling down the narrow branch, and striking my good ear like a sledgehead hitting an anvil. Salt flaked off the upper arches and drifted down through the white radiance, settling on our coats like fine dust. Damp mortar dust puffed outward from the brick face where the creature's shell sheared into the lime.

The creature collapsed into a pile of ash, and Lloyd wiped the blood from his knuckles.

It did not bleed further. Under the weight of his second punch and the hold of Maria’s light, the pale shell caved inward with a dry, crushing sound. The grey skin turned friable in a fraction of a second. Its legs let go of the mortar. The entire four-foot length dropped from the wall and struck the flags, disintegrating as it hit the stone. It did not hit as flesh or cable. It struck as a heap of grey ash and coarse slag, six inches deep against the base of the brickwork. A thin cloud of dust rose from the pile, smelling of old lime, coal gas, and scorched wool. Lloyd stood over the heap, his chest working hard, breath whistling through his twice-broken nose. He looked down at his right hand. The enlarged, stiff knuckles of his index and middle fingers were skinned raw where the shell had sheared against his skin. Dark blood welled up across the joints. He brought his arm up and wiped his knuckles clean against the thick wool of his trouser leg, leaving a dark smear four inches long above his knee.

Maria says she has run the Salts many times, her voice flat as she mentions the routine.

She lowered her hand. The bright white light died immediately, dropping the junction back into the dim, yellow flicker of the oil lamps hanging from the wall brackets. Her face looked small and pale in the grease-smoke. She looked down at the pile of grey ash on the flagstones, then at the dark drop to the left, then back along the narrow branch we had walked from the main stairs.

"I have run the Salts many times," Maria said.

Her voice was flat, level, stripped of heat or emphasis. She spoke as if reading off a tally board at a rail siding or giving the depth of a drain trench to a foreman.

"It is always like this down here," she said, pulling her wool cuffs down over her burn-scarred forearms. "A passage that stretches farther than the building above it. A wall that shouldn't be laid where it is. Things in the mortar that have too many joints. You walk until you hit what stops you, you clear it, and you keep moving until the steam clears in the room above or the line pulls you back to the bench."

Lloyd adds that he has been through more runs than anyone else here, counting them silently.
