The oak door dropped into its frame behind us with a dead thud.

The stairs went down twenty-two steps before the angle broke. I counted them by the timber risers. The tread was narrow, cut into sandstone that had worn hollow in the middle where thousands of boots had taken the turn. Salt had crept into every joint in the stone. It looked like hard hoar-frost on cold brick, but it didn't melt when a hand went near it. It was crusting in thick white rims along the mortar lines, crusting heavy enough to pinch the corners off the ashlar blocks.

I stepped off the bottom riser onto the slab floor. The entrance hall opened into a long gallery that arched like a chapel, thirty feet high at the key, twelve feet wide at the spring. The vaulting was plain stone ribbing, set on corbels six feet above the flags. No shoring. Whoever dug this gallery knew his stone, or he trusted the bed enough not to care.

My left ear was dead to the room. The silence on that side felt like a cold draught against the side of my neck, the way it always does when the walls are thick and the space opens out. I turned my head four inches to the left to let the right ear catch the echo off the wall.

Lloyd was two steps ahead of me. His right shoulder was down, his left arm held loose at his waist. He walked heavy on his heels, the way men walk when they aren't sure of the floor beneath them. Behind him, Maria had her lantern turned low, the metal casing clanking against her belt buckle with every second stride.

The air smelled of old brine and burnt grease. The moisture was heavy enough to settle on the skin like oil, but the air itself felt dry in the throat.

I checked the masonry where the wall met the vault. The salt was pushing through the vertical joints. That meant water behind the face, holding pressure against the ashlar. Not enough to blow a stone out yet, but enough to make the mortar soft as wet curd.

"Watch the footing," I said. "The flags are sloped toward the drain."

Neither of them answered. They knew how to walk on wet stone.

We moved down the main corridor.

The width stayed at twelve feet for fifty paces, then narrowed to eight where a transverse wall had been set into the original excavation. The ceiling dropped four feet. The vaulting gave way to flat stone lintels, six inches thick, carried on brick piers that had been pointed with lime mortar sixty years ago and never touched since.

The air turned thick and dead. It stayed at the bottom of the drift like marsh gas in a sumphole. It didn't circulate. There were no vent-drifts cut into the side walls, no chimney flues in the roof, no blowers working anywhere in the level. Every breath tasted like dust and old wool.

My stomach pinched. It was a sharp, dry cramp high up under the ribs, the kind that comes from three days without hot food or six hours on a hand-winch without water. I hadn't eaten since the bacon at the staging post before dawn, but this wasn't hunger. It was the air pressure. The salt in the steam was pulling the moisture straight out of the membranes inside my nose and mouth.

Fifty paces felt like two miles under heavy spoil load. My boots weighed twelve pounds each. The leather across the instep had gone stiff from the damp, pinching the bones of my toes together until the feet went numb inside the socks.

I stopped at the corner of the brick pier to let my lungs settle. Lloyd didn't stop. He kept his pace, seven feet between us, his broad back cutting through the fog like a barge hull through ditch water. His coat was dark across the shoulders where the steam had soaked through the wool.

"How far to the chapel head?" Maria asked. She was behind my left shoulder, her voice small and muffled by the dead air.

"Thirty paces," I said. "Maybe thirty-five."

I knew the distance because the piers were spaced six feet apart on center. Five piers to the bay. Six bays to the turn. That was standard work for the Vantry rail contractors when they cut the drainage levels under the lower works.

The mortar was flaking under my palm. I pulled my hand back and wiped the grit on my trousers. The grey powder left a streak like dry ash along the canvas.

We reached the end of the narrow passage where the vault opened back out into a broad, rectangular chamber.

The room had been cut out of the solid sandstone bed, forty feet across, thirty feet from floor to crown. At the far end stood a raised dais made of two tiers of dressed granite slabs, shaped like an altar platform in an old chapel. The side walls were lined with stone niches, six to a side, each five feet high and two feet deep. Most were empty. A few held smashed iron oil lamps and stiffened rags of tallow-soaked hemp.

A half-second before the thing moved, the skin across my chest tightened.

It wasn't a sound and it wasn't a sight. It was the shift in the room before the air moves. The same feeling you get in a narrow gallery two seconds before the face blows out from a missed charge—a sudden, cold draw against the skin, like someone pulling a wet sheet off your front.

Options laid out instantly: step left into the shadow of the door-jamb, draw the short steel from the belt sheath, or hold fast and keep the line clear for Lloyd. The steel wouldn't do much against what was coming. I stayed where I was, three feet behind the jamb, my weight balanced on the back heel.

The thing flickered into sight at the base of the altar dais.

It was thin as a drain-pipe and six feet long, pale grey like boiled leather that had laid in a sump for ten years. It had too many joints. Where an arm should have one elbow, this thing had three, snapping and folding against themselves like the legs of a skinning-spider. It didn't walk. It coiled along the stone flags, its long joints clicking against the granite like dry knuckles on a table.

It had no face that I could see. Just a pointed head that tapered down to a split maw, wet with clear stringy slime that trailed across the flagstones behind it.

It turned toward Lloyd.

Lloyd didn't draw a blade. He didn't have one out.

He took two long, heavy steps forward, his boots cracking against the salt-crusted flags, and lunged straight at it bare-handed.

He bent low at the waist, his broad back humped like a draught horse pulling a load out of a ditch. His left hand went out to take the creature by what passed for its neck, his big, scarred fingers spreading wide to get a grip on the wet leather skin. His right hand came up behind it in a short, hard hook.

The blow landed with a crack like a dry pine plank breaking over a knee.

His knuckles buried two inches into the shifting mass of the thing's side. The creature whipped round, three elbows snapping at once, its pale joints wrapping around Lloyd's forearm like iron hoops. It didn't bleed red. Where Lloyd's fist struck, a thick grey fluid bubbled out, thin as vinegar and smelling of wet lime.

Lloyd didn't yell. He grunted once through his nose, his teeth set hard enough to make the muscles at his jawline bulge out like walnuts under his skin. He drove his right fist in a second time, then a third, short, heavy blows delivered from the shoulder with six inches of travel.

Every time his fist hit the thing, the impact sounded like a wet sack of grain hitting a stone floor. The creature twisted violently, its thin body thrashing across the flagstones, its long, jointed limbs beating against Lloyd's thighs and chest.

He didn't yield an inch of stone. He planted his boots five feet apart, his knees bent, taking the weight of the creature on his left arm while he pounded the flank with his right.

Maria moved past my right elbow before I could put a hand on her shoulder.

She didn't run. She walked three fast steps to Lloyd's flank, her boots hitting the floor with a clean, light rhythm. She didn't reach for a knife either. Her hands were bare, the burn scars across her forearms showing white against her weathered skin under the dim light of her lantern.

She lifted her right hand six inches above Lloyd's bent shoulder.

Her palm glowed. It wasn't a burst like a powder charge or a sudden flare of pitch light. It was a smooth, steady arc of soft pale yellow light that spread out from her fingers like oil spreading over quiet water. It took the darkness out of the corners of the chapel-shaped hall and made the wet salt on the walls shine like mica.
