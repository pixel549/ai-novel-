I stepped from the doorway into the salt-crusted hall. The door behind us was four inches of solid oak bound in iron straps, set into an ashlar wall three feet through. Rain had been coming down straight outside the entry, flat and cold off the hydro roof, smelling of soot and wet slate. Inside, the heat hit first—eight degrees warmer, thick with the sour stench of damp sodium, boiled lard from the pipe packing, and old slate. The hall floor was twelve feet across to the head of the stairs. Dry white salt lay three-eighths of an inch deep across the flags like crushed quartz, drifted to an inch and a half along the skirtings where the draft from the outer jamb didn't reach. My boots left flat, square heel-marks in the crust, the dried brine crunching under the leather with a sound like dry bone breaking.

Lloyd stood two feet to my right. His shoulders took up thirty inches of the passage width, his heavy coat dark and stiff at the seams where the rain had soaked in. He didn't look at the walls or the lime-washed vaulting overhead. He looked straight ahead at the dark below the top step, his hands hanging down at his sides—the right hand stiff, the index and middle fingers locked half-bent from old breakages that had healed thick and square. 

Maria stopped eighteen inches behind my left shoulder. Her boots clicked once on clear stone where the salt had flaked off to the bare flag, then settled silent. She pulled her wool coat tight across her chest, her scarred forearms tucked in out of the draft. The burn tissue on her skin showed pink and taut beneath her cuffs.

"Stairs are wet," I said.

"They're always wet," Lloyd said.

"Watch the third step down. The nose is chipped."

"Seen it," he said.

I went down first. The stair dropped fourteen steps at forty degrees, cut right out of the grey limestone bedrock. Each riser was six inches, each tread eleven deep, hollowed out half an inch down the middle by eight years of boot traffic. Standing brine filled the hollows like grey oil, catching the yellow glare of a gas jet burning high on the wall above. An iron rail ran down the right wall, coated in black tar that had flaked away near the turns, set into the mortar joints with lead plugs. I kept my right palm four inches off the iron, ready to slam down flat if my heels went out. My left ear was completely dead—breach charge at the fourth redoubt had blown the drum out twelve years back—so I kept my chin turned ten degrees right to catch the scrape of Lloyd’s boots off the arching overhead.

At the foot of the fourteen steps, the space opened into a chapel-shaped corridor eight feet wide and twelve high to the crown of the parabolic arch. Granite blocks, eighteen inches to the course, square-dressed and dripping. Water ran down the vertical joints every four feet, green-tinged with copper salts, draining into two shallow channels cut into the floor along the skirtings. The channels were three inches wide, carrying a slow, clear trickle of salt water deeper into the stone. 

A two-inch wrought-iron steam line ran along the left wall at collar height, held by iron straps every six feet. Red lead putty sealed the joints, but a coupling twelve feet in was weeping a pale, sharp feather of vapor that smelled of sulfur and flattened against the damp roof.

We turned into the main run, thirty paces down the slope. The floor dropped an inch in ten. Humidity came up off the stone until moisture sat in small, cold beads on my wool sleeves. Lloyd walked with a heavy, short step, his right arm hanging unmoving while his left thumb was hooked into his belt. Maria stayed two paces behind his shoulder, taking two quick steps to his one long stride, her short hair damp against her neck.

***

The chapel corridor ended at a low structural arch five feet thick. Beyond it, the roof dropped to five feet ten inches and the timbering pinched in to thirty inches across. I had to duck my chin to keep my crown off the collar-sets. Lloyd had to bend at the hip and knee together, taking six inches out of his height so his short-cut hair didn't drag on the split-pine lag boards holding up the loose roof shale.

The geometry shifted the moment my lead foot crossed the sill. The masonry ceased to match the ground plan of the hydro above.

We went single file. I took the lead, keeping the flat scar tissue across my palm sliding along the timber props to feel for split grain or shifting weight. Lloyd came four feet behind, his heavy boots dragging on the narrow track. Maria was three feet behind him.

I counted paces to keep the rhythm steady. One hundred. Two hundred. The timber props came every three feet, keyed into the loose shale ceiling with oak wedges. The air went dry and warm—seventy-four degrees—and the smell of wet brine gave way to stale, burnt lime that coated the back of my tongue like chalk.

At five hundred paces, the internal stretch of the Salts began to bite into the meat of the legs. Five hundred paces in open ground is four hundred yards—three minutes' work for a line company. Here, the legs did the work of six miles while the eyes watched the same grey pine timbers, the same dark shale seams, and the same white salt rings around the iron spikes repeat in a three-yard loop.

My stomach pulled tight on the first turn of the interior clock. It was the hard, dry cramp that comes after thirty-six hours without rations—a sharp knot high under the ribs that pulls the breath down thin. Saliva dried up in my mouth, tasting of bitter iron and dry dust. I swallowed twice to wet my throat, but there was nothing to swallow.

"Edie," Maria said from the rear. Her voice was light, but it had the breathy thinness of someone who had walked half a day on an empty belly. "Is the timber bowing?"

I didn't stop. "No. Settled eight years ago. It’s just the loop."

"Feels like two days," she said.

"It isn't," Lloyd said from behind me. His voice was a dry scrape, reduced to the minimum effort needed to get the word out. "Keep walking."

We kept walking. Lloyd’s breathing behind me took on the heavy rasp of a man hauling eighty-pound spoil-bags up an incline. His heels hit the floor every three-quarters of a second. I kept the count moving in my head: one-two-three-four, one thousand paces; one-two-three-four, two thousand paces.

By three thousand paces, the internal duration had stretched into what the gut registered as a second full day of starvation. The sharp cramp settled into a heavy, cold ache behind the navel. My thigh muscles burned from the repetition, the small stabilization tendons around my kneecaps twitching every time my heel struck the wet shale. My palms were cold, the thick flat scar tissue across the centers pulling tight in the dry air.

The timber shoring ended at pace four thousand two hundred. The track swung fifteen degrees left, then nineteen right through dark, unworked basalt covered in a thin skin of wet salt crystal that glistened like fish scales under the faint light. The ceiling dropped to five feet eight.

I held up my hand, stopping the line. A longitudinal seam ran twelve feet down the crown of the rock. I reached up and dragged my fingers along the split. No grit fell. The roof was structural.

"Clear," I said.

"Good," Lloyd said. He didn't add to it.

My stomach rumbled again—a long, hollow vibration against my belt buckle that felt as if it had been thirty-six hours since I’d last seen bread. My coat felt loose around my ribs, though I knew the brass buttons were still pinned to the same cloth we’d worn into the intake room twenty minutes ago.

At pace nine thousand one hundred, the basalt walls opened out at forty-five degrees. The ceiling shot up out of sight into the dark, venting cold air from somewhere far above. We stepped off the narrow shale track onto a flat limestone ledge eight feet above a lower vault.

***

The lower vault was circular, twenty-five feet across. Three wide stone steps went down from our ledge to a floor of grey flagstones laid around a four-foot iron drain grate. Four vertical steam pipes rose from the floor at the compass points, venting pale grey clouds through clusters of half-inch holes drilled into their end-caps with a steady, low hiss.

The air was dense with mist, cutting visibility to twelve feet and carrying the greasy smell of hot mineral salt. Yellowish light came from nowhere in particular, reflecting off coarse salt crystals embedded in the wall mortar until the stone seemed to sweat needle-points of light.

On the far side of the vault, fifteen feet from the bottom step and six feet off the floor, something was attached to a rusted iron pipe bracket.

I stopped on the middle step, extending my left arm sideways to bar Lloyd’s chest. He hit my arm hard, his broad shoulder brushing my blade before his weight stopped. Maria pulled up on the top step, her hand going to the collar of her wool coat.

The shape on the wall was four feet long, six inches thick through its central mass. It had no skin, no eyes, and no mouth. It was made of nine thin, black, jointed segments—no thicker than iron telegraph wire—connected by double-hinged elbows that bent at forty-five and ninety degrees. The segments flexed, snapped, and refolded against the granite in rapid, mechanical twitches, opening and shutting like a cluster of iron folding-rules worked by an unseen gear.

It made no sound. The dark wire-work didn't catch the yellow salt-light; it absorbed it, appearing as a sharp black hatch-mark drawn against the wet ashlar. It twitched three inches to the left, the jointed elbows snapping flat against the stone and then opening wide again with a quick, dry shudder.

I stood on the step and waited. The Still Mind stayed dark inside my head. There was no intent coming off the thing, no target vector opening toward us. It was simply cycling through its angles against the rusted bracket.

"There," Lloyd said. His voice was flat as a board.

"I see it," I said. "No timber above it. Solid rock cap. Floor is slick with salt sludge."

"We need the iron behind it," he said.

"It isn't looking at us," Maria said, her voice quiet behind my shoulder. "It doesn't have anything to look with."

"Doesn't matter," Lloyd said.

***

He moved before I could give him the mark. He went down the last two stone steps in one heavy leap, his boots slamming into the salt sludge on the flagstones with a wet report that threw grey liquid six feet across the floor.

He covered the fifteen feet to the far wall in three long, low strides, his head down, his shoulders hunched broad under his coat. He didn't reach for a blade or a timber iron. His left hand was clamped into a fist at his hip; his right hand—the wrecked one with the enlarged knuckles and the stiff fingers—was open, reaching straight for the central joint of the black wire-work.

A half-second before his fingers reached the wall, the Still Mind snapped taut in my chest like a struck cable. It wasn't a vision or a voice; it was the immediate, cold certainty of a physical blow coming down a line. The vector opened from the wall straight toward Lloyd's chest.

"Lloyd, hold," I said, but he was already committed, his boots sliding through the sludge.

Maria was off the steps behind him. She didn't jump; she moved with the quick, low efficiency of someone used to navigating narrow sick-bays, covering eight feet in two silent paces. She planted her heels shoulder-width apart on the flagstones five feet behind Lloyd’s left hip.

She raised her right hand, palm open toward the middle of his back.

A beam of dense, pale white light shot from her hand. It wasn't fire—it had no heat and made no sound—but it was thick as a fence rail, cutting through the steam like solid timber. The light struck the top of Lloyd’s right shoulder and ran down his arm in a bright, steady sheath, wrapping his forearm, his thick wrist, and his swollen knuckles in a white glare that rendered every scar and pore on his skin visible.

At the same instant, Maria stepped forward one pace and slammed her left palm flat against the heavy wool between Lloyd’s shoulder blades. Her grip locked. She took his forward drift into her own frame, her knees bent, her heels wedged into a mortar joint to anchor him against the wet stone.

The light running down Lloyd’s arm stiffened the air around his skin, turning the damp wool of his sleeve rigid. His open right hand slammed down onto the central cluster of black joints.

***

The moment his skin touched the black segments, the entity reacted. All nine jointed limbs snapped straight out at once, expanding to six feet across with a crack like a high-tension steel guy-wire breaking under strain.

The central mass twisted forty-five degrees. The black wire-work was slick as wet ice and colder than the stone. Lloyd’s thick, stiff fingers tried to lock around the middle hinge, but his enlarged knuckles couldn't take a bite on the smooth metal. His palm skidded across the dark angle with a dull scrape. His hand dropped four inches down the wall, dragging across the granite blocks.

His left boot lost its footing on the salt sludge. His weight threw him six inches to the left, his broad shoulders tilting toward the wet masonry as his balance broke.

Maria didn't let go of his coat. She leaned back against his drift, her heels locked into the mortar line, her small frame going taut as she took his weight. 

With her right hand still extended, her fingers flexed inward. The beam of light sheath-wrapped around Lloyd’s arm expanded into a concussive pulse of white radiance four feet across. It struck the entity flat against the ashlar wall with a dull, heavy thud that shook the steam pipes and sent a spray of condensation off the ceiling.

The force pinned the creature. The nine jointed limbs were slammed back against the stone, held flat like pressed tin against the granite blocks. The pulse locked the twitching elbows in mid-flex six inches above the rusted pipe bracket, freezing them solid against the wall.

Lloyd didn't waste the two seconds. He dragged his left boot back eighteen inches, wedged his heel against a raised flagstone lip, and drove his broad frame up through his hips. He brought his right hand up again, open-palmed, and drove the heel of his hand hard into the center of the pinned mass.

***

The impact of his hand against the pinned entity didn't make a sound. It created a displacement of air—a sudden, violent spike in pressure that struck the chamber like a fallen timber.

The air in my good ear slammed shut with a sharp, high ring. The floor tilted thirty degrees to the left. My knees hit the flagstones hard before I could set my feet, the wet salt sludge soaking through my trousers to the bone with a bitter, freezing bite. I threw both hands out to break the drop, my scarred palms slamming into two inches of grey liquid. 

The vault swung around me. The light from Maria's hand fragmented into yellow streaks that spun across the damp ceiling blocks like steam-spouts. My chest locked; the pressure in the room was so dense my lungs couldn't expand to take in air. 

I kept my palms flat on the wet floor, my head down between my shoulders, waiting for the structural weight of the roof to drop. The Still Mind was dead quiet—no second strike, no target line, just the physical shockwave of something being forced out of a shape it wasn't supposed to have.

I stayed on my knees for four seconds, counting the pulses of blood in my good ear until the room settled back onto its haulage lines.

***

The dark wire-work beneath Lloyd’s palm didn't fracture. It lost its lines. The sharp, ninety-degree elbows turned grey and soft, the thick black wire expanding into coarse white grain that broke apart under the pressure of his hand.

The central mass collapsed inward, turning to a dry, salt-heavy mist that hissed like red-hot iron dropped into a slack-tub as it hit the wet granite. The nine jointed limbs dissolved segment by segment, flaking away from the wall like dry whitewash and rising in thin, pale ribbons toward the upper dark of the vault.

In three seconds, there was nothing left on the granite blocks except the rusted iron pipe bracket and a smear of fine white powder where the central joint had been pinned.

Lloyd stood with his right palm still pressed flat against the wall. His shoulders rose and fell under his heavy coat, his breath coming in short, harsh rasps that rattled in his throat. He looked at his hand, turned it over once to look at the stiff, bent fingers, then let it drop to his side. He didn't say anything.

Maria let go of his coat. Her left hand dropped, her fingers trembling slightly before she tucked her palm into her sleeve. The white light on her right hand faded out, leaving only the dim, sour yellow glare from the salt crystals in the walls.

I pushed up off the flagstones, my knees wet and cold, my palms grey with salt sludge. I wiped my hands on the sides of my trousers, checking the ceiling joints overhead. The basalt cap was intact. No fresh fractures, no fallen spall.

"Floor's clear," I said. My voice was flat, the ring in my right ear still buzzing like a fly against glass.

Lloyd didn't turn around. "Line's open," he said to the wall.

Maria stepped around him, her face pale in the yellow light, her short hair sticking to her forehead with steam. She looked at the iron bracket, then down at the floor where the mist had cleared.

"That was faster than the last one," she said. She didn't sound pleased; she sounded like someone who had spent four hours turning a heavy winch and knew there was another turn coming.

"It was smaller," Lloyd said. He rubbed his right wrist with his left hand, his thumb digging into the tendon above the joint.

***

Maria pulled a length of knotted cord from inside her wool coat. 

It was thick hemp, four feet long, bound at six-inch intervals with heavy brass wire ferrules. She stepped up to the rusted pipe bracket where the entity had hung, took one turn of the cord around the iron, and pulled the main bight through the eye. She finished it with a double hitch, drawing the knot tight against the iron until the brass ferrules clinked against the stone.

The cord hung down three feet, marking the breach point for the next run down the line.

"That'll hold," she said. She touched the knot once with her thumb, checking the tension, then stepped back onto the clear flags.

I looked at the drain grate in the middle of the floor, then back toward the stone steps leading up to the narrow basalt passage. The return count would be nine thousand paces through the tight shoring, double-time on an empty belly with the legs working against the internal stretch.

"We have twenty minutes of steam left in the line before the Hydro drops pressure," I said. "Move out."

Lloyd turned without a word, his heavy shoulders swinging as he took the first step back toward the ledge. Maria fell in behind him, her boots clicking once on the dry stone at the base of the stair before the salt got under her soles again. I took the rear, my head turned ten degrees right, keeping my palm four inches off the basalt as we entered the cut.
