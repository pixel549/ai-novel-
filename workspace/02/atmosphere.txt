I step down the salt-crusted stairs into a corridor whose arches curve like a chapel.

The stairs are made of dressed granite, sixteen steps total, dropping eight feet below the floor of the intake room. White salt crusts the edge of every riser, four inches deep in the corners where the broom does not reach. The passage at the bottom measures five feet across and seven feet high at the centre of the vault. Brick arches spring from square pillars every four paces. The mortar between the bricks is grey and dry near the top, but white salt crystals bleed through the lower joints.

Lloyd walks three paces ahead of me. His broad shoulders fill half the width of the hallway. Maria walks two paces behind my left shoulder, her boots striking the flagstones with a lighter sound than ours. Light comes from iron oil dishes set into wall brackets every eight paces. The flames are small and yellow, casting sharp shadows forward along the flags.

I count the pillars as we pass. Four on the left, four on the right. The floor drops two inches across every ten yards, sloped to drain moisture toward the far end.

I turn down a side hallway and, driven by hunger, the walk stretches until it feels like two days.

The main passage continues straight toward a timber partition, but a narrow branch opens on the right side. I step through the turn first. The side hallway is three feet wide, just wide enough for one person to walk without rubbing shoulders against the damp brickwork. The ceiling drops to six feet two inches. 

I have not eaten since noon yesterday, when I had four ounces of salted beef and a crust of rye bread in the yard. My stomach is empty and tight against my belt. We keep walking. The hallway goes straight for forty yards, tilts down at a five-degree angle, then turns through a shallow curve to the left. 

The brickwork here is older. Six courses of stretchers, one course of headers, bound with dark lime mortar. The layout does not vary. Step after step, forty paces, eighty paces, one hundred and twenty paces. The air turns cold and heavy with the smell of wet stone. There are no doors, no side vents, and no drain grates in the floor. 

Lloyd's heavy tread sounds right behind me, four feet back. Maria follows behind him. The walk continues down the straight shaft. Without food in my stomach, the distance feels wrong, stretching out far beyond the perimeter of the building on the surface. We cover two hundred paces before the passage flattens out onto level flagstones.

The hallway ends at a T-junction. The left path goes down into darkness; the right path is blocked by fell masonry.

Lloyd spots a thin, many-jointed thing coiling along the far wall and lunges at it with bare fists.

The creature is cling-mounted on the vertical mortar six feet off the ground, directly opposite the junction. It is four feet long and no thicker than a three-inch cable, divided into seven pale segments. Six thin, elbowed legs stick out from the sides, each leg broken into three short joints that twitch against the brickwork.

Lloyd stops six feet back, sees it, and moves without calling out. He lunges straight from the corner. He takes two long strides across the junction, his right boot hitting the centre flagstone with a heavy thump. His right arm comes back, fist doubled, and drives straight forward toward the middle segment of the body.

The creature darts, its limbs snapping, and Lloyd lands a punch that cracks one of its joints.

The creature shifts sideways four inches before the blow lands. Its six jointed legs snap against the brickwork like dry ash twigs, kicking its body upward toward the archline. Lloyd adjusts his footing. He plants his left heel, rotates his torso through thirty degrees, and throws a hard right hook upward into its third segment. 

His fist hits the creature squarely against the wall. A sharp, dry crack echoes down the three-foot corridor as the outer shell of the third joint splits open under his knuckles.

Maria steps forward, raises her hand, and a flash of light steadies the creature, pinning it against the stone.

The creature loses its grip with two forward legs and swings down, its rear legs scrabbling against the damp mortar to regain purchase. Maria moves past my left side, stepping into the junction three feet behind Lloyd. She raises her right hand, palm open, fingers spread toward the wall.

A bright, solid flash of white light bursts from her palm. The light fills the junction from floor to ceiling, turning the red brick white for a second. The creature stops moving instantly under the flare. Its legs lock stiff against the wall, held rigid four feet off the floor as if nailed to the stone joints.

I watch the strike land, my own reaction lagging, and the shock of the impact reverberates through the corridor.

My Mind knew the violence was coming half a second before Lloyd planted his foot. I saw the line of his shoulder shift before his boot hit the flag, but my own body stayed set in my stance. My hand did not reach for my belt; my feet remained two inches apart on the stone. 

The delay sits between the knowing and the moving. I watch his knuckles hit the hard shell, and the shock of the strike travels down the line of the wall. The impact shakes the flags under my boots, a heavy, dull vibration that runs up through my ankles into my shins. The air in the narrow junction snaps from the force of the strike and the sudden heat of Maria's light.

The creature collapses into a pile of ash, and Lloyd wipes the blood from his knuckles.

The cracked third joint gives way entirely. The pale shell crumbles inward, and the rest of the six-limbed body breaks apart into light grey dust. The dust drops straight down, forming a small pile two inches high against the base of the wall. Nothing remains on the brickwork except a smear of white salt where its legs had been pinned.

Lloyd steps back two paces into the middle of the passage. He lowers his hands and looks at his right fist. The skin over his middle two knuckles is torn open, showing red meat beneath the grime. He reaches down with his left hand, pulls the cloth of his trouser leg tight against his right thigh, and wipes the blood from his knuckles in two firm, downward drags.

Maria lowers her hand to her side. She turns her neck once to the left, then looks down the open hallway.

"I have run the Salts forty-two times," Maria says. Her voice is flat, even, and carries no weight as she states the number. "The lower runs always have these along the damp mortar. They do not stay if you keep moving."

Lloyd looks down at his hand, flexing his fingers into a tight fist, then opening them again until the skin stretches over the broken knuckles.

"This makes forty-seven for me," Lloyd says. "I keep count. Forty-seven runs since Carrowgate. More than anyone in the lower wards."

He does not elaborate. He turns his head toward the left path of the T-junction, where the passage slopes downward at a steeper angle.

We walk thirty paces down the left hallway. The air gets colder, carrying a strong smell of iron and wet sand. The brickwork gives way to rough-cut stone blocks, three feet long by eighteen inches high, stacked without mortar. 

At the end of the passage stands an attendant. He wears a heavy grey wool jacket and a thick canvas apron tied at his waist with twisted copper wire. On the stone floor beside his boots lies a coiled knot-cord, four feet long, made of six-strand braided hemp with iron ferrules crimped on both ends.

An attendant hands us a coiled knot-cord, and I begin to loop it around my wrist, the rope tightening against the stone floor.

The attendant picks up the cord by the centre ring and hands the bitter ends to us. Lloyd takes the right strand. Maria takes the left. I take the middle section of the rope.

I pull five inches of slack through my palm, wrap the stiff hemp once around my left wrist, and slip the end under the standing part to form a running hitch. I draw the loop down snug against the skin, right over the flat scars from my cable work. The heavy rope drags across the flags, rustling against the grit. I pull the knot tight until the hemp bites into my wrist.

The knot‑cord snaps shut, a faint metallic chime echoing in the salt‑lined stone.
