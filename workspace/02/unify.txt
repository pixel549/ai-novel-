I stepped through the iron threshold into the salt-crusted hall. The warm, oily vapor of the intake chamber died instantly at my left shoulder, cut off by a draft that smelled of cold limestone, wet soot, and stagnant sea brine.

The floor beneath my boots was no longer dressed slate. It was rough sandstone slabs covered in a three-inch skin of dry white salt that cracked like bone under my heels. The door behind us was gone, replaced by the mouth of a dark, narrow stairwell cutting straight down into the bed.

I stopped on the top landing to check our footing. My left ear was dead space, filled with the same heavy, lead-weight silence that had stayed there since the camouflet blew out four years ago. My right ear took in the crunch of salt, the heavy drag of Lloyd’s boots, and the slow, two-second drop of water hitting stone somewhere below.

Lloyd stopped two paces back on my right, his broad shoulders hunched to clear the low masonry. Maria stepped up beside him, her small frame pulled tight against the damp, her hands buried deep in the pockets of her wool coat.

"Watch the edges," I said. "The salt’s loose on the slate."

"I see it," Lloyd said.

I looked down the stairwell. The treads were cut six inches high and nine deep, worn down four inches in the center by old foot traffic that had scraped the salt clear of the stone. The side walls were double-course brickwork, arched into a pointed ceiling six feet six inches overhead. Salt hung from the mortar joints in long, brittle needles that snapped when my sleeve brushed them.

I took the first step down. The heel of my boot slid two inches before catching the lip of the slate. I planted my right palm against the brick wall to brace the load, feeling the flat scars across my skin press against the rough edge of the course. The stone was cold enough to bite through leather work gloves.

I counted fourteen steps to the turn. The stair swung ninety degrees left, opening into a wide, chapel-shaped corridor. It was twelve feet across at the base, rising nine feet to a barrel-vaulted brick ceiling. Sandstone flagstones lay unevenly along the floor over a bed of wet crushed shell. Brine pooled in every low joint, three inches deep along the centerline, reeking of sulfur and old iron rust.

I stepped off the last riser and took three paces forward to clear the foot of the stair.

"Clear," I said.

Lloyd came down off the bottom step and stopped six feet to my right. He wiped his palms down his trousers, his enlarged knuckles making a dull scraping sound against the wool. The knees of his trousers were already wet from the vapor hanging under the vaulting.

Maria came last. Her boots made a thin, wet splashing sound as she hit the brine pool at the stair base. She took her position three feet behind Lloyd, her back to the return angle.

I surveyed the gallery. The vaulting ran sixty yards straight before disappearing into dense grey fog. Four brick piers supported the left wall at eight-foot intervals, mortared with lime that had turned green from damp. The right wall was solid brick, marked by three vertical settling cracks running from the floor plates up to the arch crowns. The fissures were an inch wide at the base and pinched out near the ceiling pin. The mortar inside had failed, leaving raw brick edges exposed.

"Load is holding," I said, pointing to the fissures. "Settled old. Keep off the right pier line anyway."

"Which drift?" Lloyd asked.

"Straight," I said. "Stay on the sandstone spine where the bed is sound."

I moved down the corridor. My boots turned the standing brine into grey froth. The air grew colder with every ten yards, dropping until my breath came out in thick white plumes that hung in the dead air.

We reached the end of the vaulted chapel after sixty paces. The brickwork ended dead against a face of raw, unworked granite. The sandstone flags gave way to dark clay and wet river gravel. A narrow passage was driven straight into the stone—four feet wide, six feet high, shored with rough-sawn oak bents set at four-foot centers.

I stopped at the timbering to check the joinery. The header was an eight-inch square beam of green oak, checked for soft rot at the mortise cuts. The side posts sat directly in the clay without footings. The timber was soaked black, slick with a white fungus that looked like salt spores.

"Clearance drops," I said. "Mind the timber legs. Don't grab the posts."

I turned my shoulders sideways and stepped through the frame into the narrow drift.

The passage ran straight, but as soon as my boots hit the gravel inside, the spatial scale slipped.

I took ten paces. Then twenty. The oak bents repeated every four feet with mechanical precision, but the end of the tunnel stayed sixty yards out. I counted the steps aloud to keep my grid straight.

"Forty. Forty-one. Forty-two."

The passage stretched outward ahead of us like an uncoiling spring. The air shifted—hot and dry for twenty paces, smelling of parched slate, then dropping back to freezing damp.

My stomach gave a violent, tearing twist. A cramp locked my belly so hard my knees buckled. I drove my right fist into the side post to keep from dropping, the rough bark scraping through my glove.

It was not a muscle spasm. It was the sharp, hollow agony of two days without rations. My mouth went bone-dry, my tongue coated in a thick, bitter film of salt. My ribs felt pressed flat against my spine, as if I had walked forty miles through spoil without a scrap of bread or a drop of clean water.

I forced my lungs open and straightened up. My thighs felt hollow, the muscles trembling with sudden fatigue.

"Edie?" Maria’s voice came from three feet back. It sounded thin, deadened by the timbering.

"Stomach," I said. "Time's slipping in the drift. Keep moving."

I pulled my pocket watch from my waistcoat. The second hand was pinned at three minutes past four. It did not tick.

I took another step. My boots dragged through the gravel as if I had been on my feet for forty-eight hours without sleep. Every pace was a calculation of weight and leverage. My belly ground against itself, screaming for food that did not exist. I kept my eyes on the oak headers, counting each frame as it passed overhead to anchor the line.

"Eighty. Eighty-one."

The gravel underfoot turned to hard clay rutted with old wheel tracks. The ruts were four inches deep, full of dry salt dust that puffed up in grey clouds around my shins.

The hunger hit again—a cold, burning scrape behind my navel that tightened my throat until I tasted bile. My body registered two days of hard march through dark drifts, even though my legs had only covered eighty yards of real stone. I pressed my palm flat against my thigh, feeling the stiff scars across my skin to anchor my focus back on the floor plates.

"How deep are we?" Lloyd asked behind Maria. His voice was steady, uncluttered by the stretch, though his feet dragged heavy in the salt dust.

"Distance doesn't match the count," I said without looking back. "The drift is expanding. We're dropping, but the incline is under two degrees. We should be twenty feet below the chapel floor by now."

The passage widened after forty more paces. The oak bents ended, replaced by natural granite walls that bowed out to six feet. The roof raised to eight feet, the ceiling rough-broken slate draped in thick grey mould.

The hunger ache peaked, held until my vision narrowed to a grey pinhole, then settled into a dull, flat throb at the base of my spine.

"Chamber ahead," I said, pointing with my chin. "Clear of the pinch."

I stepped out of the timbered drift into a square room cut straight out of the granite bed.

The room was twenty feet square, flat-ceilinged, supported by four heavy timber posts set in a square at the center. The floor was rough granite slab covered in three inches of stagnant grey brine that smelled of sulfur and burnt lime. Steam hung thick in the room, trapped in a solid horizontal band between the three-foot and five-foot lines.

I moved three paces left to clear the archway. Lloyd passed me on the right, his boots making heavy, hollow splashes in the standing water. Maria pulled up behind me, her back set three feet from the rock face of the entry wall.

I swept the room, checking the structural corners first. Left corner: clear, stacked slate spoil, stable. Right corner: clear, timber post mortised clean into a rock socket. Center axis: grey steam, motionless.

Then the steam flickered. It was not a draft. A shape was standing against the far granite wall, half-drowned in the grey mist.

I raised my hand to hold Lloyd. "Hold line," I said.

The shape was six feet high, but its proportions were wrong. It was thin as an iron rod, built of long, dark, jointed segments that looked like discarded telegraph wire twisted into a loose skeletal frame. It had no skin, no clothing, and no face. Where its arms joined, there were too many bends—four distinct elbows along a single line that twitched in rapid, mechanical jerks.

The entity shuddered. It skipped three inches left, then three inches right, its movement cutting across the floor without its base touching the water. The wire frame flickered like a reflection on dirty oil, dropping out of sight for a fraction of a second before snapping back two inches higher on the wall.

"You see that, Lloyd?" I said.

"I see it," Lloyd said. He took one step forward, dropping his center of gravity into his knees. His shoulders set broad and square. He was not looking at the fog; his eyes were pinned to the central cluster of jointed wire.

"It's on the back wall frame," I said. "No solid load under it. Watch your footing."

The creature's jointed limbs twitched. A sound like iron pins dragging across dry slate scraped through the chamber.

Lloyd didn't wait. He moved instantly.

He lunged across the twenty-foot room, his boots driving through the standing brine and throwing grey sheets of water against the central timber posts. He covered fifteen feet in two heavy strides, his body kept low, his head tucked down between his broad shoulders.

He reached out with his bare right arm, his fingers splayed wide. He had no steel, no pike, no tool. He drove his bare fist straight at the central joint of the flickering wire frame.

Maria moved in the same breath. She stepped forward on my right, her left boot planted firm in the brine, her coat swinging open.

She pulled her sleeve back from her scarred wrist and extended her right hand, palm flat toward Lloyd's advancing back.

"Lloyd!" she called out, her voice loud in the dead space.

A shaft of bright, cold white light shot from her open palm. It was not fire and it had no smoke. It was a narrow, solid beam of native Arcana, six inches thick, straight as an iron bar. It struck Lloyd between the shoulder blades and ran down his extended right arm, wrapping his wrist and hand in a stark white flare that lit the whole granite room.

The light locked his arm into place. His strike line straightened instantly, his stride settling into a heavy, mechanically sound trajectory that didn't sway or drift.

Lloyd's bare right hand slammed shut around the central joint of the creature.

The wire shape recoiled. It let out a thin, high-pitched screech like steam venting through a cracked pipe. The jointed limbs snapped rearward eighteen inches, driven back by the impact of Lloyd's weight.

Lloyd's thick fingers hit the cold surface of the entity, but the frame was slick with salt oil. His grip slipped. His thumb slid off the central wire, his wrecked knuckles tearing through grey vapor without holding the core.

The creature twitched violently. Its four elbows snapped inward, looping around Lloyd's extended forearm.

Maria didn't break her stance. She shifted her weight two inches forward onto her left heel, her mouth pressed into a thin line, her eyes locked on the contact point.

She thrust her open palm forward another three inches and released a concussive pulse of white light.

The Arcana hit the back wall with a heavy, hollow thud that rattled the brickwork in the corridor behind us. The pulse didn't scorch the stone; it hit like a square-headed timber ram, slamming the wire creature hard against the granite face. The flickering shape was pinned flat against the masonry, five feet off the floor, its jointed limbs spread wide and held motionless by the steady, unyielding stroke of Maria's light.

"Hold him!" Lloyd grunted. He planted his back heel into a seam in the sandstone floor, reset his feet eighteen inches wider, and brought his left fist around in a heavy arc toward the creature's center.

I stood six feet back, watching the room’s load vectors. The back wall was solid granite, but the ceiling timber directly above the entity was taking the vibration. The oak cap sill groaned, dropping a fine dust of dry mortar into the water below.

"Watch the overhead!" I yelled. "The timbering's loose!"

Before Lloyd's left hand could land, my Still Mind hit me.

There was no vision, no voice, no warning image. Just a cold, flat certainty in the back of my skull that a violent strike was landing from the upper left corner of the room half a second before it happened.

I knew the strike was coming. My mind mapped the line instantly: top left ceiling beam, three feet out, coming down hard and fast.

I tried to drive my right heel back to leap clear toward the entry arch. But the physical world was too slow. My legs were heavy from the stretch in the drift, my boots weighed down by water and clay. Before my right knee could lock to push my weight back, the shockwave blew through the chamber.

A sudden surge of barometric pressure erupted from the left wall.

The air pressure jumped instantly. The steam layer suspended at four feet was crushed downward, slamming flat against the standing water with a sound like a gunshot.

The pneumatic shock hit my left shoulder and chest like a falling timber.

My boots were torn from the floor flags. I went down hard, both knees slamming into the rough granite bottom of the brine pool. The impact sent a sharp, dull shock straight up my kneecaps into my hips. My hands slapped down into three inches of filthy water to break the fall, the cold brine soaking through my gloves to the skin.

The entire chamber tilted around me.

The horizon line swung twenty degrees right. The center timber posts looked crooked, the granite walls slanting at an impossible angle as if the whole bed had been tipped onto its side. The standing water rolled toward the right wall in a heavy grey wave, exposing the dark, salt-crusted floor slabs beneath my wet palms.

I stayed on my knees for two seconds, shaking my head to clear the vibration in my right ear. My left ear recorded nothing—just the same heavy, leaden silence.

Ahead of us, the wire creature broke.

Under the combined load of Maria's pinned pulse, the atmospheric shockwave, and Lloyd's secondary brace, the entity's structural frame failed. The four elbow joints on its left side snapped inward with a sharp metallic crack like breaking telegraph wire.

The jointed frame collapsed in on itself. It left behind no iron, no scrap, no bone. As the structure failed, the dark metal lines dissolved instantly into a dense, coarse cloud of dry white salt mist.

The mist expanded five feet outward, then drifted straight up into the damp roof joists, dispersing into the upper air until nothing was left on the granite face but a wet grey stain.

The chamber settled back onto its bed plates. The tilt vanished. The standing water sloshed back across the floor flags, leveling out at three inches deep around my shins. The air pressure dropped back to normal, the grey steam slowly rising off the floor to reform its layer at the four-foot line.

I pushed off my hands and stood up. My knee joints clicked. The cloth over my left knee was torn open, exposing a raw scrape that burned in the salt water. I wiped my face with the back of my wet sleeve.

"Everyone standing?" I asked.

Lloyd was fifteen feet out, his right arm still extended, his fists black with wet coal dust. He lowered his arm slowly, breathing hard through his broken nose. He looked down at his right palm, turned it over twice, then wiped it on his trousers.

"I'm standing," he said.

Maria dropped her hand. The white light died, leaving the room dark, lit only by the faint grey glow off the salt-crusted walls. She pulled her wool coat tight across her chest, her face pale, her lips pressed together.

"I'm alright," she said softly. Her voice was flat with fatigue, her small shoulders trembling slightly from the draw on her Arcana.

"Check the drift behind us," I told her. "Make sure the pinch didn't collapse when the ceiling shifted."

Maria turned and looked back into the timbered passage. The oak bents were still standing straight and true in the clay.

"It's open," she said.

She reached under her coat and pulled a long length of heavy hemp knot-cord from her belt. The cord was half an inch thick, bound at six-inch intervals with tight brass wire ferrules.

She walked five paces over to the north wall, her boots splashing softly through the brine.

Mounted against the masonry was an old, rusted four-inch iron drain pipe running vertically from the floor flags up into the timbering. The iron was heavily scaled with dark orange rust that had gathered salt crust in its joints.

Maria took the lead end of the knot-cord. She passed the line around the iron pipe twice, bringing the working end over the standing part to form a clean double hitch. She pulled the cord tight with both hands, throwing her weight back to set the knot hard into the rust scales.

She left the long tail trailing down into the brine pool, marking our depth and vector for the next run.

I walked three paces over and stood beside her, my boots sinking into the wet clay at the foot of the pipe. The granite behind the iron felt freezing cold, vibrating with a faint, low-frequency hum that pushed through the stone into the soles of my boots.

I watched the knot-cord tighten as the salt-stained walls hummed with unseen tension.
