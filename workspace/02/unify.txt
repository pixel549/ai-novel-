I stepped off the bottom stone stair onto salt-crusted flags. Grey ridges had calcified where water dried over time. Low gas jets burned yellow on the walls, and the stone arches above sent our boots back in flat echoes.

We followed the passage to a vaulted chamber built like a small chapel. Four square pillars of red masonry supported the roof. At the far wall, a brass door green with verdigris blocked the way down.

I pushed. The cold came through the scars on my palms before the grease-slick hinges gave way.

Beyond the threshold, a straight corridor stretched into the dimness. We stepped through. The side walls stayed ten feet apart, but the far end receded. I kept my stride steady—eighty steps a minute—and counted two minutes by my pulse, but the journey felt two days long. A sharp, empty hunger opened in my gut. Time blurred around us, smearing the motion of Lloyd’s boots across the stone.

Ten yards out, something slid along the left wall. It was six feet of slate-grey carapace, moving flat against the ashlar on six jointed limbs, each fitted with two elbows. The masonry didn't creak under it. A quiet hiss came from its forward split as it drew down on us.

Lloyd stepped past my left shoulder—my deaf side—and lunged bare-handed. Two heavy strides, then his right fist struck the central plate. The limbs writhed, double elbows snapping round to pin his sleeve, needle tips scraping wool. Lloyd grunted, set his heel against a flagstone seam, and jammed his fingers into the soft joint under its shell.

Maria moved up on his flank. She lifted her right hand, her sleeve pulled back over pale burn scars, and pulsed white light straight into its core. The limbs buckled under the glare, uncoiling from Lloyd's arm. The force pushed the thing off the wall just enough for him to set his grip inside its frame.

I stepped back to keep our retreat line clear and watched. Lloyd held the frame; Maria held the light.

My mind clicked. A half-second before it hit, I knew a blade was moving across my space.

Knowing gave me the trajectory, but my body was half a step behind the math. A lower limb, tipped like a sapper's wedge, whipped past Lloyd's shoulder. I pulled my arm back, but the spur grazed my forearm. It cut the wool and left a shallow line across the skin, cold first, then warm.

Lloyd braced his boot against the wall and twisted. The shell cracked like dry oak under load. The limbs went slack, and the creature dropped onto the flags in a tangled heap of black whorls. It broke down into grey grease, then boiled off into white steam toward the roof.

Maria let her hand fall and wiped her forehead with her wrist. "I have run this sequence dozens of times," she said. "I know the timing."

Lloyd stood over the wet stone and rubbed his knuckles on his trousers. He nodded once. "Third time I've met that thing in the hydro."

I looked up. Ten feet back, an iron bracket was set into the keystone. A frayed knot-cord hung down, its lower loop hanging six feet off the floor. Steam was rising from the wall vents, thick and hot. As the system pressure built, the tension on the line shifted, and the loop slackened two inches closer to the flags.

I stepped under the bracket, caught the hemp loop, and set my weight back. I pulled. The counterweight inside the ceiling resisted, then released with an iron thud.

Above us, heavy pins shot home inside the channels. I heard the lock click and the chamber seal, trapping us for the next run.
