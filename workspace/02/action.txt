I stepped over the iron threshold of the steam room door. The air changed instantly. The heat from the copper boiler vanished, replaced by a cold, wet draft that smelled of crushed limestone and stagnant sea mud. 

The floor beneath my boots was no longer smooth slate. It was rough, uneven stone, covered in a two-inch layer of dry white salt that crunched under my heels. The doorway behind us did not lead back to the Hydro intake hall. It opened onto the top of a stone staircase that cut down into darkness.

I stopped on the top landing to set my stance. Lloyd was two paces behind me on my right. Maria stood beside him, her head slightly down, her hands tucked under her woollen coat. 

"Watch your footing," I said. "The salt is slick underneath."

"I see it," Lloyd said.

My left ear was completely dead in this space. The silence on that side was absolute, like a lead weight pressed against my jaw. My right ear picked up the sound of water dripping onto stone somewhere below us, clicking at regular two-second intervals. 

I looked down the stairwell. The steps were cut from dark slate, nine inches deep with six-inch risers. The center of each step was worn down by roughly four inches, hollowed out by foot traffic that had cleared the salt from the middle of the treads. The side walls were constructed of double-course brick work, arched overhead into a narrow gothic point six feet six inches above the steps. White salt crust hung from the brick seams in long, brittle icicles that snapped off when my coat sleeve brushed them.

I took the first step down. My boot slipped two inches before the heel caught the edge of the slate tread. I braced my right hand against the wall to steady my weight. The stone was wet and cold enough to numb my fingertips through my leather work gloves. 

I counted twelve steps to the first turning. The stair bent ninety degrees to the left. The archway opened into a wide corridor shaped like a chapel nave. It was twelve feet across at the base, rising to a nine-foot barrel-vaulted ceiling of dark red brickwork. The floor was flags of split sandstone, laying unevenly on a bed of wet crushed shell. Brine pooled in every low joint, three inches deep in the center of the walkway.

I stepped onto the flags and moved three paces clear of the stair foot to let Lloyd and Maria clear the bottom step.

"Clear room," I said. 

Lloyd stepped off the bottom riser and stood six feet to my right. He wiped his palms on his trousers. The fabric of his coat was already damp from the fog hanging in the ceiling vaults.

Maria came down last, her boots making a soft, wet sloshing sound as she hit the brine pool at the base of the stairs. She stopped beside Lloyd, three feet back from the main line of the corridor. 

I surveyed the archways ahead. The hall stretched forward for fifty yards before vanishing into dense grey vapour. Four brick piers supported the left wall, spaced eight feet apart. Each pier was three feet wide, mortared with lime that had turned green from constant damp. The right wall was solid brick, showing three distinct vertical settling cracks that ran from the floor plates up into the arch crowns. The cracks were an inch wide at the bottom and tapered to nothing near the ceiling pin. The mortar had failed inside the fissures, leaving raw brick edges exposed.

"Load is holding," I said, pointing at the cracks with my chin. "The wall settled, but it isn’t moving now. Keep off the right side."

"Which way?" Lloyd asked.

"Straight down the main drift," I said. "We stick to the center line where the stone is solid."

I began walking forward down the chapel hall. My boots churned the brine into grey foam. The air grew colder with every ten paces, the temperature dropping until my breath formed thick white plumes in front of my face. 

We reached the end of the vaulted hall after sixty paces. The chapel arches ended abruptly against a raw rock face. The sandstone flags gave way to mud and wet gravel. A narrow passage was cut into the rock face, four feet wide and six feet high, shored with rough-sawn oak bents.

I stopped at the timber frame. The header beam was an eight-by-eight inch square of green oak, checked for rot at the mortise joints. The side posts were set directly into the mud without footings or base plates. The wood was soaked through, black with moisture, and coated in a slime of fine white salt spores.

"The head clearance drops here," I said. "Mind the posts. Don't grab the timbering."

I turned my body sideways and stepped through the frame into the narrow drift.

The passage ran straight, but as soon as my boots hit the gravel inside, the spatial scale shifted. 

I took ten paces. Then twenty. The timber frames repeated every four feet with mechanical precision, but the far end of the tunnel did not get any closer. I counted my steps out loud. 

"Fifty. Fifty-one. Fifty-two."

The passage seemed to stretch outward ahead of us, extending into an endless straight drift. The air grew dry and hot within twenty paces, then dropped back to freezing cold. 

My stomach gave a sharp, violent twist. A cramp seized my belly, so intense it doubled me over for a second. I pressed my right hand against the oak post to keep my balance. 

It was not a simple muscle cramp. It was the deep, gnawing hollow of extreme starvation. My mouth went bone dry, my tongue coating in a bitter, chalky film of salt. My ribs felt pressed flat against my spine, as if I had walked for two days straight without a scrap of bread or a drop of clean water.

I caught my breath and straightened up. My legs felt light and hollow, the muscles in my thighs trembling from sudden exhaustion.

"You alright, Edie?" Maria asked from three feet behind me. Her voice sounded thin, distorted by the narrow timber walls.

"Stomach," I said. "Time's slipping in the drift. Keep moving."

I checked my pocket watch. The hands were pinned at three minutes past four, completely motionless. The second hand did not tick. 

I stepped forward again. My knees dragged as if I had been on my feet for forty-eight hours without rest. Every pace was a deliberate effort of leverage and balance. My digestive tract ground against itself, screaming for food that wasn't there. I kept my eyes fixed on the timber headers, counting each frame as it passed overhead to anchor my spatial grid.

"One hundred and ten," I muttered. "One hundred and eleven."

The floor beneath the gravel turned to hard-packed river clay, rutted with old wheel tracks that ran parallel to the timber legs. The ruts were four inches deep, filled with dried salt dust that puffed up in small white clouds around my boots as I walked. 

The hunger pangs hit again, sharper this time, a cold burning sensation behind my navel that made my throat tighten. My body registered forty-eight hours of hard march through dark spoil, even though my legs had only covered eighty yards of real stone. I squeezed my right fist tight, feeling the thick scars across my palm press against my fingers to force my focus back onto the path.

"How far down are we?" Lloyd's voice came from behind Maria. His voice was flat, untroubled by the stretch, though his boots dragged heavy in the salt dust.

"Distance doesn't line up with the count," I said without turning. "The drift is expanding. We're going down, but the incline is less than two degrees. We should be twenty feet below the lower arch by now."

The passage began to widen after another forty paces. The oak timber bents ended, replaced by natural granite walls that bowed outward to six feet. The roof raised to eight feet, the ceiling rough-broken slate covered in dense grey mould.

The hunger ache peaked, held for thirty seconds until my sight blurred at the edges, then slowly settled into a low, dull throb at the base of my belly. 

"Next chamber ahead," I said, pointing forward. "Clear of the pinch."

I stepped out of the narrow passage into a square room cut into the granite bed.

The room was twenty feet wide, twenty feet deep, with a flat rock ceiling supported by four massive timber bents set in a square pattern at the center. The floor was rough granite slab, covered in three inches of stagnant grey water that smelled strongly of sulfur and wet coal ash. Steam hung thick in the air, suspended in a dense horizontal layer between the three-foot and five-foot marks from the floor.

I stepped three paces into the room and cleared the doorway to the left. Lloyd moved past me to the right, his boots making heavy splashes in the standing water. Maria took up position directly behind me, her back three feet from the rock face of the entrance wall.

I swept the room from left to right, scanning the structural corners first.

Left corner: clear. Stacked slate spoil up to three feet, stable.
Right corner: clear. Timber leg set straight into a chiseled mortar pocket.
Center axis: grey steam, motionless.

Then I saw the movement near the far wall, twenty feet straight ahead of us.

The steam flickered. It wasn't air movement. A shape was standing against the back granite face, half-submerged in the grey vapour.

I lifted my hand to hold Lloyd in place. "Hold position," I said.

The shape was six feet high, but it was not built like a man. It was extremely thin, constructed of long, dark, jointed segments that looked like discarded iron telegraph wire twisted into a loose skeletal frame. It had no skin, no clothing, and no face. Where its limbs met, there were too many joints—four distinct elbow bends along a single long arm that twitched in rapid, mechanical jerks.

The creature shuddered. It moved three inches to the left, then three inches to the right, its movement skipping across the floor without its feet touching the water. The wire frame flickered like a reflection on disturbed oil, dropping out of sight for a fraction of a second before snapping back into view two inches higher than it had been before.

"You see that, Lloyd?" I asked flatly.

"I see it," Lloyd said. He took one step forward, dropping his weight into his knees. His shoulders hunched. He wasn't looking at the steam; his eyes were locked on the central cluster of jointed wire.

"It's on the back wall frame," I said. "No solid load under it. Watch the water."

The creature's jointed arms twitched again. A metallic clicking sound scraped through the room, like iron pins being dragged across dry slate. 

Lloyd did not wait for an order. He moved instantly.

He lunged forward across the twenty-foot chamber. His boots drove through the three inches of standing water, throwing up grey cascades against the timber posts. He covered fifteen feet in two massive strides, his body kept low, his head tucked between his broad shoulders.

He reached out with his bare right arm, his fingers splayed wide. He had no weapon, no steel, no timber pike. He drove his bare fist straight toward the central joint of the flickering wire frame.

Maria acted in the same second. She stepped forward on my right, her left foot planted firmly in the water, her woollen coat swinging back. 

She raised her right arm, pulling her sleeve back from her scarred wrist. Her hand opened flat, palm facing Lloyd's advancing back.

"Lloyd!" she called out, her voice loud and steady in the dead air.

A beam of bright, clear white light shot from the center of Maria's palm. It wasn't fire, and it wasn't steam. It was a narrow shaft of native Arcana, solid as a brass rod, six inches thick. The beam hit Lloyd square between the shoulder blades and extended along his extended right arm, wrapping his wrist and hand in a stark white flare that lit up the entire granite chamber.

The light locked his arm in place. His forward vector stabilized instantly, his stride dropping into a solid, mechanically perfect strike line that did not sway or tip.

Lloyd's right hand closed around the central joint of the creature.

The wire shape recoiled. It let out a sharp, high-pitched screech like a steam pipe bursting under maximum load. The jointed limbs snapped rearward eighteen inches, driven back by the impact of Lloyd's weight. 

Lloyd's thick fingers hit the cold, flickering surface of the entity, but the material was slick with brine and salt oil. His grip slipped. His thumb slid off the central wire frame, his fingers tearing through the grey vapour without holding the core.

The creature twitched violently, its four elbows snapping inward to latch around Lloyd's extended forearm.

Maria did not drop her stance. She shifted her weight two inches forward onto her left heel. Her face was tight, her eyes fixed on the point of contact.

She thrust her open palm forward another three inches and released a concussive pulse of white light.

The Arcana hit the back wall with a loud, hollow thud that echoed through the brick arches behind us. The pulse did not burn the stone; it hit like a heavy wooden ram, driving the wire creature hard against the granite face. The flickering shape was pinned flat against the masonry, five feet off the ground, its jointed limbs spread wide and held motionless by the steady, pressure of Maria's light.

"Hold him!" Lloyd grunted. He planted his back heel into a seam in the floor flags, reset his stance eighteen inches wider, and brought his left fist around in a heavy arc toward the creature's pinned center.

I stood six feet back, watching the spatial vectors. The back wall was solid granite, but the ceiling timber directly above the entity was taking the brunt of the vibration. The oak bent groaned, a fine spray of dry mortar dust falling from the cap sill into the water below.

"Clear the overhead!" I shouted to Lloyd. "The timbering is loose!"

Before Lloyd's left hand could connect, my Still Mind triggered.

There was no vision, no voice, no warning image. Just a sudden, sharp certainty in the back of my skull that a violent force was arriving from the upper left quadrant of the room half a second before it happened.

I knew the strike was coming. My brain registered the vector instantly: top left ceiling beam, three feet out, coming down hard and fast.

I tried to plant my right heel to dive backward toward the entrance arch. But the physical world was too slow. My legs were heavy from the time-stretch in the passage, my boots weighted down by water and mud. Before my right knee could lock out to drive me back, the shockwave hit the room.

A violent surge of barometric pressure blew out from the left wall. 

The air pressure jumped fifty pounds in a fraction of a second. The steam layer suspended at four feet was crushed downward instantly, slamming flat against the standing water with a loud *crack*. 

The pneumatic blast struck my left shoulder and chest like a falling timber. 

My feet were torn from the floor flags. I went down hard, both knees slamming into the rough granite bottom of the brine pool. The impact sent a sharp, dull shock up through my kneecaps and into my thighs. My hands slapped into the three inches of filthy water to break my fall, the cold brine soaking straight through my leather gloves to the skin.

The entire chamber tilted around me.

The horizon line swung twenty degrees to the right. The timber bents looked crooked, the granite walls slanting at an impossible angle as if the room had been tipped onto its side. The standing water rolled toward the right wall in a heavy, slate-grey wave, exposing the dark, salt-crusted floor flags beneath my bleeding palms.

I stayed on my knees for two seconds, shaking my head to clear the ringing in my right ear. My left ear remained completely dead, recording nothing of the blast.

Ahead of us, the wire creature buckled.

Under the combined strain of Maria's pinned pulse, the atmospheric shockwave, and Lloyd's secondary brace, the entity's structural frame gave out. The four elbow joints on its left side snapped inward with a sharp metallic report like breaking telegraph wire. 

The jointed frame collapsed in on itself. It did not leave behind iron scraps, wire, or bone. As the structure broke, the dark metal lines dissolved instantly into a dense, coarse puff of dry white salt fog.

The fog expanded outward five feet, then drifted straight up into the damp roof joists, dispersing into the upper atmosphere of the room until nothing was left against the granite wall but a grey stain of wet brine.

The room settled back onto its axis. The tilt vanished. The standing water sloshed back across the floor flags, leveling out at three inches deep around my knees. The air pressure dropped back to normal, the heavy grey steam slowly rising back up from the floor to fill the four-foot line.

I pushed off my hands and stood up. My knee joints clicked, the wet cloth of my trousers torn open at the left cap, revealing a raw red scrape that was already stinging from the salt water. I wiped my face with the back of my wet sleeve and cleared the water from my eyes.

"Everyone up?" I asked, my voice flat.

Lloyd was standing fifteen feet out, his right arm still extended, his fists wet and black with coal dust. He lowered his arm slowly, breathing heavy through his broken nose. He looked at his right palm, turned it over twice, then wiped it on his coat.

"I'm up," he said.

Maria dropped her right hand. The white light vanished instantly, leaving the room dark again, lit only by the faint grey glow coming from the salt crust on the walls. She pulled her woollen shawl tight around her chest, her face pale, her lips pressed into a thin line.

"I'm fine," she said softly. Her voice sounded exhausted, her small frame trembling slightly from the effort of the Arcana pull.

"Check the drift behind us," I told her. "Make sure the exit didn't close when the ceiling shifted."

Maria turned and looked back at the timber archway we had stepped through. The narrow passage was still open, the oak bents standing straight and true in the mud floor.

"It's clear," she said.

She reached down under her coat and pulled a long length of heavy hemp knot-cord from her utility belt. The cord was half an inch thick, bound at regular six-inch intervals with tight brass wire ferrules. 

She walked five paces over to the north granite wall, stepping carefully through the standing water. 

Mounted against the brickwork of the north pier was an old, rusted four-inch iron drain pipe that ran vertically up from the floor flags into the ceiling timbering. The iron was heavily oxidized, covered in thick flaking scales of dark orange rust that had collected salt crust in the crevices.

Maria took the end of the knot-cord. She passed the line around the iron pipe twice, bringing the working end over the standing part to form a clean, double-wrapped hitch. She pulled the cord tight with both hands, using her full weight to set the knot hard against the rusted scale of the riser.

She left the long tail of the cord trailing down into the brine pool, marking our depth and position for the return leg.

I walked three paces over and stood beside her, my boots sinking into the wet gravel at the base of the pier. The wall behind the pipe felt freezing cold, vibrating with a faint, low-frequency hum that pulsed through the granite masonry into the soles of my boots.

I watch the knot‑cord tighten as the salt‑stained walls hum with unseen tension.
