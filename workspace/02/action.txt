I step from the doorway into a salt-crusted hall, down a stone stair into a wide, chapel-shaped corridor slick with brine.

The door behind us is four inches of oak framed in wrought iron, set into an ashlar masonry wall three feet thick. Outside, the rain was falling straight down; inside, the air is eight degrees warmer and smells of damp sodium and wet slate. The floor of the hall extends twelve feet from the threshold to the top step of the staircase. It is covered in a layer of dry white salt, three-eighths of an inch deep across the middle, piled to an inch and a half along the skirtings. My boots leave flat, square depressions in the crust as I walk. 

Lloyd stands two feet to my right. His shoulders take up thirty inches of the hall’s width. Maria is eighteen inches behind my left shoulder, her boots clicking once on the flagstone where the salt has cleared before her weight settles. 

The stone stair goes down fourteen steps at a pitch of forty degrees. Each step is cut from the gray limestone bedrock, six inches high at the riser and eleven inches deep on the tread. The stone is worn hollow in the center by eight years of boot traffic, creating shallow basins that hold standing brine. A one-inch iron handrail runs down the right wall, supported by three lead-plugged brackets set into the mortar joints. I keep my right hand four inches off the rail, ready to grab if the slick stone gives way under my heel. My left ear is completely deaf from the breach charge at the fourth redoubt; I keep my head turned ten degrees to the right so my good ear can catch the echo of our boots off the damp vaulting.

At the bottom of the fourteen steps, the corridor opens to eight feet wide and twelve feet high at the center of the parabolic arch. The walls are constructed of square-cut granite blocks, laid in courses eighteen inches high. Water trickles down the vertical mortar joints every four feet, feeding two narrow drainage channels cut directly into the floor along the base of each wall. The channels are three inches wide and two inches deep, carrying a slow current of clear, salty water deeper into the works.

A two-inch wrought iron steam pipe runs along the left wall at a height of five feet six inches. It is fastened to the granite by heavy iron straps every six feet. The pipe joints are sealed with red lead putty, but small wisps of pale vapor escape from a coupling twelve feet down the corridor, rising straight up to flatten against the damp ceiling.

We walk forty paces along the main channel. The floor slopes downward at a steady grade of one inch for every ten feet. The air grows heavier, the humidity climbing until moisture forms in small droplets on the cloth of my sleeves. Lloyd walks with a short, heavy stride, his right arm hanging stiffly at his side while his left hand rests against his belt buckle. Maria keeps pace behind us, her steps lighter, taking two short paces for every one of Lloyd’s long steps.

I follow a narrow passage that seems to stretch; my stomach growls for two days before I reach the next chamber.

The main chapel corridor ends at a low arch cut through a structural cross-wall five feet thick. The passage beyond drops in height to five feet ten inches and narrows to thirty inches across. I have to tilt my head slightly forward to clear the timber collar-sets overhead. Lloyd has to bend at the waist and knees, ducking his head six inches to keep his hair clear of the split-pine lag boards holding up the loose roof shale.

As soon as we cross the threshold into the narrow passage, the geometry of the space alters. The walls remain visible, but the physical length of the tunnel ceases to correspond to the foundation plans of the building above. 

We walk single file. I lead, keeping my palm sliding along the right-hand timber props to gauge the alignment of the framing. Lloyd is four feet behind me, his boots scraping the narrow floor track. Maria is three feet behind Lloyd. 

I count paces. One hundred paces. Two hundred paces. The timber props recur every three feet, held in place by wooden wedges driven between the caps and the fractured shale ceiling. The air temperature settles at seventy-four degrees Fahrenheit. The smell of wet lime replaces the brine, flat and dry against the back of the throat.

By five hundred paces, the physical strain of the Salts space begins to register in the belly and thighs. In standard terrain, five hundred paces is roughly four hundred yards, accomplished in under four minutes. Here, the legs perform the mechanical work of miles while the visual landmarks—the grey pine timbers, the dark shale seams, the white salt rings around the nail heads—repeat in a fixed three-yard loop.

My stomach contracts sharply on the first turn of the interior time. It is a dry, hollow cramp high in the abdomen, the precise physiological signal the body gives after thirty-six hours without food. I feel the saliva dry up on my tongue. My throat constricts, forcing me to swallow twice to clear the dry lining. 

We continue walking. Lloyd’s breathing behind me takes on the rasp of a man carrying eighty pounds of gear up a ten-percent incline. His boots hit the floor every three-quarters of a second. I keep my count running in my head to maintain our speed: one-two-three-four, one hundred paces; one-two-three-four, two hundred paces.

By the time my mental count reaches three thousand paces, the internal duration has stretched into what my body registers as a second full day. The hunger transitions from an acute cramp to a dull, heavy ache behind the belly button. My thigh muscles burn with the buildup of lactic acid, the small stabilization muscles around the kneecaps twitching with every third step down onto the wet shale track. My scarred palms are cold, the skin pulled tight over the old cable burns.

The passage curves fifteen degrees to the left, then nineteen degrees to the right over a distance of twenty feet. The timber shoring gives way to bare, unworked rock—a dark, fine-grained basalt covered in a thin skin of wet salt crystal. The ceiling height fluctuates between six feet and five feet eight inches. 

I pause at pace four thousand two hundred to check the alignment of the ceiling. A longitudinal fracture runs along the spine of the basalt for twelve feet ahead. I raise my right hand, touching the edge of the crack. No fresh grit falls into my palm; the rock is stable for now.

"Keep moving," I say. My voice sounds flat in the tight space, the sound deadening instantly against the wet stone without an echo.

"Moving," Lloyd says behind me. His voice is raspy, reduced to a dry scrape.

Maria does not speak, but the rhythmic scuff of her leather soles against the stone floor continues three feet behind Lloyd.

We walk another four thousand paces. The sensation of empty space pressing against the ribs does not lift. My stomach growls again, a long, low rumbling sound that vibrates against my belt buckle. The body’s clock insists it is forty-eight hours since we stepped off the fourteen stone steps, though the dampness on my wool collar has not yet dried. 

At pace nine thousand one hundred, the basalt walls open outwards at a forty-five-degree angle. The low ceiling rises sharply, disappearing into upper shadow beyond the reach of our sight. We step out of the narrow passage onto a flat stone ledge overlooking a lower chamber.

In the dim steam I see a thin, many-elbowed shape flickering like twisted wire.

The lower chamber is circular, twenty-five feet across from wall to wall. The floor is eight feet below our ledge, reached by a short flight of three wide stone steps. The floor consists of large, irregular flagstones laid around a central iron drainage grate four feet in diameter. Steam rises continuously from four vertical iron pipes set into the floor at the cardinal points, each pipe three inches in diameter and venting gray vapor through a cluster of half-inch holes drilled into the top caps.

The air in the chamber is thick with cloud, reducing visibility to twelve feet. The ambient light has no clear source; it emerges as a sour, yellowish luminescence from the salt crystals embedded in the ashlar walls.

On the far wall of the chamber, fifteen feet from the bottom step and six feet off the floor, something hangs attached to a rusted iron pipe bracket.

I stop on the middle step, extending my left arm sideways to hold Lloyd back. He stops two feet behind me, his right shoulder brushing my shoulder blade. Maria stops on the top step.

The shape on the wall is roughly four feet long and six inches wide at its central mass. It is not solid flesh, nor is it gas. It consists of nine thin, black, wire-like appendages, each no thicker than a telegraph cable, connected by sharp, angular joints that resemble double-hinged elbows. The segments are each seven to ten inches long. They flex and snap back in rapid, jerky motions, bending at ninety-degree angles before springing flat again against the granite block. 

It makes no sound as it moves. The black segments do not reflect the yellow light of the salt; they absorb it, appearing as sharp, dark lines drawn against the wet, pale stone. It shifts three inches to the left, its jointed limbs folding back on themselves like a cluster of iron carpenter's rules being opened and closed at once.

I assess the space. The distance from the bottom step to the wall bracket is fifteen feet six inches. The floor between is wet, slick with a quarter-inch layer of condensate and salt sludge. There are no overhead structural hazards immediately visible above the creature, though an abandoned iron chain hangs from an eye-bolt six feet to its right.

My Still Mind does not trigger. There is no intent focused on us yet; the shape is merely cycling through its mechanical angles against the wall.

"There," Lloyd says quietly. His voice is flat. 

"I see it," I say. 

Lloyd lunges at the creature, bare-handed, while Maria extends a hand of light that steadies his arm.

Lloyd moves past me on the left, taking the last two stone steps in a single heavy leap. His boots strike the flagstones with a wet thud, spraying salt sludge six feet across the floor. 

He covers the fifteen feet to the far wall in three long, driving strides. His stance is low, his center of gravity dropped, his broad shoulders hunched forward. He does not draw a weapon. His left hand is clenched into a fist at his hip; his right hand—the heavy, scarred hand with the two bent fingers—is open, reaching straight for the central joint of the flickering shape.

My Still Mind snaps sharp in my chest a fraction of a second before he makes contact. It is not a vision; it is the sudden, hard certainty of an incoming blow, a physical vector opening in the air between Lloyd's chest and the wall. 

"Lloyd," I say, but he is already in motion.

Maria drops down the steps behind him. She does not run, but her movement is fluid and fast, covering eight feet in two paces. She stops five feet behind Lloyd's left flank, her feet planted shoulder-width apart on the slick flagstones. 

She raises her right arm, palm facing forward toward Lloyd's back. 

A hard, white light flares instantly from her hand. It is not fire; it is a dense, focused beam of radiance three inches thick that cuts through the dim steam like a wooden pole. The beam strikes the upper curve of Lloyd’s right shoulder and glides down his forearm, coating his sleeve, his wrist, and his thick, enlarged knuckles in a bright, steady skin of light.

Simultaneously, Maria steps forward one pace, extending her left hand to touch the fabric of Lloyd's wool coat between his shoulder blades. Her touch is firm, a grounding grip that anchors his forward momentum.

The light running down Lloyd’s arm stiffens the air around his skin. His right hand closes on the central cluster of black joints.

The creature recoils, snapping back as Lloyd's grip slips and Maria's arcane pulse holds it in place.

The moment Lloyd’s fingers touch the black wire-work, the entity reacts. All nine jointed segments snap straight out at once, extending to a total width of six feet. The motion is instantaneous, accompanied by a sharp, metallic crack like a high-tension steel cable snapping under load.

The central mass twists forty-five degrees to the right. The black material is slick, colder than the surrounding air. Lloyd’s thick fingers close around the main joint, but the surface offers no traction. His enlarged knuckles slip off the smooth, wire-thin segments. The skin of his palm skids across the dark angle, his hand sliding four inches downward toward the stone floor.

His left foot slips on the salt sludge. His balance breaks; his upper body tilts six inches to the left, his heavy weight carrying him toward the wet wall.

Maria does not let go of his coat. She drives her heels into the mortar joints between the floor flagstones, bending her knees to take his weight. 

With her right hand still extended, she flexes her fingers inward. The beam of light shining on Lloyd's arm breaks into a broad, concussive pulse of pale white force. The pulse expands outward in a circle four feet across, striking the entity flat against the granite wall.

The impact pins the creature. The nine jointed limbs are slammed backward into the stone, flattened like pressed tin against the ashlar blocks. The pulse holds it rigid, freezing the twitching elbows in mid-flex six inches above the rusted pipe bracket.

Lloyd uses the two seconds of stabilization to reset his feet. He drags his left boot back eighteen inches, plants his heel against a raised flagstone lip, and drives his weight up through his hips. He brings his right palm up, slamming the heel of his hand hard into the center of the pinned mass.

A sudden surge of pressure knocks me to my knees, the world tilting around me.
