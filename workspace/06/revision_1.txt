I step out of Stillwell onto the cobbled street, the sky a harsh white. My boots hit the wet stone. The air was cold, carrying the salt tang of the drains and the burnt coal smoke from the lower districts. The weight of my coat hung from my shoulders, damp from the steam rooms below. My fingers felt stiff inside my gloves where the knuckles didn't want to close all the way, and my right arm swung at my side, the joint stiff and awkward where the elbow had been broken and put back together twice. I checked the balance of it, the way the weight hung from the shoulder, making sure the joint didn't catch before I lifted my hand to pull my collar higher against the damp drift blowing off the river.

My Insight pricks as the daylight shows the city’s polite veneer hiding rot. The buildings along the street looked solid enough with their brick fronts and glass shop windows, but behind the plaster and the fresh paint I could see the damp courses rotting through the foundation timbers. The stone sills were pitted where acid had run off the roofs. The people walking past us on the flags looked busy and clean in their woolen coats and bowler hats, but I could see the gray slackness of their skin underneath, the way they moved like clockwork dolls whose springs were nearly run down, their shoulders hunched under coats that were too thin for the weather. It was the same everywhere you looked if you kept your eyes open long enough to see the seams coming apart, the wood beneath the varnish gone spongy with fungus, the mortar turned to gray sand between the bricks.

I see a rail guard by the tracks, his form throwing three shadows instead of two. He stood beside the iron rail with his hand on his holster, his tunic buttoned to the collar. The overhead lamp and the pale daylight caught the wool of his sleeves, but the ground at his boots told a different story. Three dark shapes stretched out across the cobbles from his heels, long and thin and crooked at the joints, all pointing in slightly different directions while he stood entirely still, not blinking against the glare, his eyes fixed on the empty iron line running north toward the freight yards.

A creature that looks like a dog walks beside him, its eyes glinting metal. It was about the size of a terrier, with a narrow chest and ribs made of riveted iron plate. It did not pant and it did not sniff the ground. Its paws made a sharp clicking sound on the stones, iron against granite, rhythmic and hollow. The brass sockets where its eyes should have been caught the harsh white light and reflected it back as two yellow points, unblinking and dead, tracking the movement of a cart wheel before snapping back to stare straight ahead at the ballast.

Maria puts her hand on the guard’s sleeve, then a second time, saying nothing. She reached out her small hand, her fingers brown from the road and burnt across the knuckles where the heat had caught her years ago. She touched the wool of the guard's arm once, withdrew her fingers, and then touched it again in the exact same spot, as if testing whether the cloth was solid or if her hand would go straight through it into the emptiness beneath. The guard did not look down at her. He stared straight ahead at the rail line. Maria said nothing, her mouth set in a thin line as she pulled her shawl tighter around her shoulders against the wind coming off the river.

"He's not going to answer," I said. My voice sounded flat in the cold air, coming out too loud and then dropping away into the rattle of the carts.

Maria didn't look back at me. She kept her thumb pressed into the seam of her sleeve where the wool was worn down to the white threads. "I wasn't asking him anything, Lloyd."

"He doesn't have anything to give you," I said. "None of them do."

"They have whatever they're standing there with," she said, her voice small and dry, the way it got when she had been sitting in the damp too long. "Somebody has to look at them while they do it."

I note that Maria has no place to go, Edie looks eastward without explaining, and I state I’m heading home to get my mother out. We stood together by the curb with the carts rumbling past on iron wheels. Maria stood with her boots close together, looking down at the puddles between the stones where grease floated in rainbow circles; she had no parish now, no room at the almshouse, and no letters waiting for her in the city, just the small bundle tied up in rag that she carried under her arm like a book she didn't want anyone to open. Edie turned her head away from the shops and the river traffic, her eyes fixed on the gray line of the horizon where the tracks ran out toward the valley and the higher ground, saying nothing about why that direction mattered, her deaf ear turned toward the noise of the dray wagons so she only had to listen to the wind coming off the stone. I looked at the pair of them, then adjusted the collar of my coat and said I was heading home to get my mother out before the winter set in and the passes closed.

"She's still in Carrow Ford?" Maria asked.

"She's where she was," I said.

"And if she won't come?"

"She'll come," I said. "The roof's gone down half an inch since the thaw. You can see the light through the north gable."

Edie didn't look round from the horizon. Her hands stayed deep in her pockets, her knuckles hard and gray from decades of wire and cable. "The bridge at the gorge is out," she said. Her voice was like two flat stones sliding across each other. "The company engineers didn't shore the footings before the frost hit. You'll have to take the low track past the lime kilns."

"I know the track," I said.

"The low track's flooded," Maria said, looking up from her boots. Her face was pale around the edges where the cold had pinched the blood out of her skin. "There was a cart went down into the ditch near the third weir last week. The horses didn't come up."

"They're horses," I said. "They don't care about the water once they're in it."

"The driver cared," Maria said.

"He shouldn't have been hauling wet timber in October," I said.

Edie opens a ledger, tallies the cost of the journey east, and marks a payment for a job waiting at the next station. She pulled a bound book with a stiff brown paper cover from her deep coat pocket. She flipped the pages with her thumb, the skin of her palms worn smooth and white where the wire ropes used to bite. Her pencil moved quickly down a column of figures, adding up the price of rail tickets, coal allowances, and the freight charges for the iron tools in her sack. She checked a line near the bottom, made a short tick mark with the lead, and closed the cover with a dull slap that didn't carry over the sound of the street.

She slides a sealed envelope with a small purse of coins into my palm. The paper was heavy and gray, sealed with a blob of red wax that had no crest pressed into it. The purse was small, made of boiled leather with a draw-string of hempen cord, and it hit my hand with a dull metallic chink when she dropped it there. I closed my fingers around it, feeling the round shape of the copper tokens and the thicker edges of the silver pieces inside, and shoved the whole business down into my deep coat pocket where it would stay against my ribs, right where I could feel the weight of it every time I took a breath.

"That's for the rail?" I asked.

"That's for the work at the junction," Edie said. "The rest comes when the shoring holds."

"And if it doesn't?"

"It holds," she said.

Edie folds a paper plan into her coat, the inked lines outlining the work we will do. It was a large sheet of heavy parchment, yellowed at the folds, covered in neat architectural cross-sections and red ink notations marking load-bearing pillars and drain levels. She creased it down twice with her thumb, squared the edges against her thigh, and slid it into the inside pocket of her tunic next to her spare pencils, her movements efficient and empty of any hesitation, the way she did everything since the tunnels.

Without questioning, we move down the main road, the guard’s mismatched shadows flickering as we pass. We stepped off the curb together and walked in single file past the brick wall of the freight depot. The guard remained by his rail, but as we went by, his three black shapes twisted across the uneven cobbles, lengthening and shortening independently of the white light above us like fingers reaching out to touch our boots, dragging behind us for a second before snapping back to his heels.

The dog‑like creature follows at a distance, its gait oddly mechanical. It trotted ten paces behind us, its iron paws striking the stones with a dry, clicking cadence that did not vary whether it climbed a slope or dropped into a gutter. It did not bark or snarl. It kept its brass eyes fixed on the small of Edie's back, matching our pace step for step without a tongue loll or a heavy breath, a small, dark shape that didn't cast a shadow at all under the flat white glare of the noon sky.

My Insight steadies, confirming that the city’s outward cheer is a false mask over hunger and decay. The pressure behind my eyes eased down from a sharp throb to a dull, flat hum. Through the brick walls of the bakeries and the mercantile houses, I could see the empty larders, the black mold creeping along the floor joists, and the thin, gray shapes of people sitting in unlit rooms waiting for something that was not going to arrive. The paint was bright on the outside, the signs swung smoothly on their iron brackets, and the girls in the milliner's window laughed at something down the lane, but the wood beneath was soft enough to push a thumb through, and the water coming out of the standpipes tasted of old iron and dead things from the upper reservoirs. It was all held together with spit and fresh lime, and if you leaned your weight against any wall in this street, your hand would go straight through to the lath before you felt the studs.

We walked past the coal yards where the wagons were lined up three dead waiting for the gates to open, the drivers sitting on their boxes with their chins tucked into their mufflers, looking like stumps of wood left out in the rain. Nobody looked at us. In a city this size, people were always moving somewhere they didn't want to be, carrying things they'd been paid to take, and nobody had the time to spare for three strangers walking north with their heads down against the cold. We vanish down the road, the extra shadow of the guard stretching ahead of us like a dark omen.
