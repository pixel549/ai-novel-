I stepped out of Stillwell onto the cobbled street, the sky a harsh white. My boots hit the wet stone of the paving. The air was cold and carried the salt smell of the drains and the burnt tang of coal smoke from the lower districts. The weight of my coat hung from my shoulders, damp from the steam rooms below. My right arm swung at my side, the bone set at a five-degree inward angle where the elbow had been broken and put back together. 

My Insight pricked as the daylight shows the city’s polite veneer hiding rot. The buildings along the street looked solid enough with their brick fronts and glass shop windows, but behind the plaster and the fresh paint I could see the damp courses rotting through the foundation timbers. The stone sills were pitted where acid had run off the roofs. The people walking past us on the flags looked busy and clean in their woolen coats and bowler hats, but I could see the gray slackness of their skin underneath, the way they moved like clockwork dolls whose springs were nearly run down. 

I see a rail guard by the tracks, his form throwing three shadows instead of two. He stood beside the iron rail with his hand on his holster, his tunic buttoned to the collar. The overhead lamp and the pale daylight caught the wool of his sleeves, but the ground at his boots told a different story. Three dark shapes stretched out across the cobbles from his heels, long and thin and crooked at the joints, all pointing in slightly different directions while he stood entirely still.

A creature that looks like a dog walks beside him, its eyes glinting metal. It was about the size of a terrier, with a narrow chest and ribs made of riveted iron plate. It did not pant and it did not sniff the ground. Its paws made a sharp clicking sound on the stones, iron against granite, rhythmic and hollow. The brass sockets where its eyes should have been caught the harsh white light and reflected it back as two yellow points.

Maria puts her hand on the guard’s sleeve, then a second time, saying nothing. She reached out her small hand, her fingers brown from the road and scarred across the back where the heat had caught her years ago. She touched the wool of the guard's arm once, withdrew her fingers, and then touched it again in the exact same spot. The guard did not look down at her. He stared straight ahead at the rail line. Maria said nothing, her mouth set in a thin line as she pulled her shawl tighter around her shoulders against the wind coming off the river.

I note that Maria has no place to go, Edie looks eastward without explaining, and I state I’m heading home to get my mother out. We stood together by the curb with the carts rumbling past on iron wheels. Maria stood with her boots close together, looking down at the puddles between the stones where grease floated in rainbow circles; she had no parish now, no room at the almshouse, and no letters waiting for her in the city. Edie turned her head away from the shops and the river traffic, her eyes fixed on the gray line of the horizon where the tracks ran out toward the valley and the higher ground, saying nothing about why that direction mattered. I looked at the pair of them, then adjusted the collar of my coat and said I was heading home to get my mother out before the winter set in and the passes closed.

Edie opens a ledger, tallies the cost of the journey east, and marks a payment for a job waiting at the next station. She pulled a thick, grease-stained oilcloth ledger from her deep coat pocket. She flipped the pages with her thumb, the skin of her palms flat and scarred across the line where the cables used to run. Her pencil moved quickly down a column of figures, adding up the price of rail tickets, coal allowances, and the freight charges for the iron tools in her sack. She checked a line near the bottom, made a short tick mark with the lead, and closed the cover with a dull slap.

She slides a sealed envelope with a small purse of coins into my palm. The paper was heavy and gray, sealed with a blob of red wax that had no crest pressed into it. The purse was small, made of boiled leather with a draw-string of hempen cord, and it hit my hand with a dull metallic chink when she dropped it there. I closed my fingers around it, feeling the round shape of the copper tokens and the thicker edges of the silver pieces inside, and shoved the whole business down into my deep coat pocket where it would stay against my ribs.

Edie folds a paper plan into her coat, the inked lines outlining the work we will do. It was a large sheet of heavy parchment, yellowed at the folds, covered in neat architectural cross-sections and red ink notations marking load-bearing pillars and drain levels. She creased it down twice with her thumb, squared the edges against her thigh, and slid it into the inside pocket of her tunic next to her spare pencils.

Without questioning, we move down the main road, the guard’s mismatched shadows flickering as we pass. We stepped off the curb together and walked in single file past the brick wall of the freight depot. The guard remained by his rail, but as we went by, his three black shapes twisted across the uneven cobbles, lengthening and shortening independently of the white light above us like fingers reaching out to touch our boots.

The dog‑like creature follows at a distance, its gait oddly mechanical. It trotted ten paces behind us, its iron paws striking the stones with a dry, clicking cadence that did not vary whether it climbed a slope or dropped into a gutter. It did not bark or snarl. It kept its brass eyes fixed on the small of Edie's back, matching our pace step for step without a tongue loll or a heavy breath.

My Insight steadies, confirming that the city’s outward cheer is a false mask over hunger and decay. The pressure behind my eyes eased down from a sharp throb to a dull, flat hum. Through the brick walls of the bakeries and the mercantile houses, I could see the empty larders, the black mold creeping along the floor joists, and the thin, gray shapes of people sitting in unlit rooms waiting for something that was not going to arrive. The paint was bright on the outside, but the wood beneath was soft enough to push a thumb through.

We vanish down the road, the extra shadow of the guard stretching ahead of us like a dark omen.
