I step out of Stillwell onto the cobbled street, the sky a harsh white. My right forearm is set at a five-degree inward angle with a misaligned elbow where Maria set the bone, and it hangs heavy against my wool trousers. The air hits my face with the cold smell of burnt lime and wet iron lingering in my nostrils from the steam rooms behind us. Edie walks beside me, her step even and short, her dead left ear turned away from the passing cart traffic. Maria follows close behind her, her hand tucked into the wool of her shawl. 

My Insight pricks as the daylight shows the city’s polite veneer hiding rot. The limestone facades of the terrace houses look clean enough from twenty paces, but where the damp rises from the gutters, the mortar has turned to gray meal and the wood beneath the sills is black with dry rot. I stare at a drainpipe where the rust has eaten through the iron in three places, leaving jagged red teeth that bite into the stone. 

I see a rail guard by the tracks, his form throwing three shadows instead of two under the washed-out glare of the midday sun. The shadows stretch long and thin across the granite setts by the freight spur, one pointing south, one angling toward the switch box, and a third that stays vertical and thick behind his heels like an unhitched post. The guard stands with his thumbs hooked into his belt, staring past the coal tenders. 

A creature that looks like a dog walks beside him, its eyes glinting metal. It has four legs of dull-cast iron and a square chest that ticks with the muffled sound of a clockwork spring winding down. It stops when the guard stops, its metal paws clicking sharp against the flat stones. 

Maria puts her hand on the guard’s sleeve, then a second time, saying nothing. The guard does not turn his head to look at her fingers resting on his wool sleeve. He keeps his gaze fixed on the empty rails. Maria draws her hand back and wipes her palm down her thigh. 

I note that Maria has no place to go, Edie looks eastward without explaining, and I state I’m heading home to get my mother out. The words come out flat in the cold air. Edie does not look at me when I say it. She reaches into her coat and pulls out an oilcloth ledger. 

Edie opens a ledger, tallies the cost of the journey east, and marks a payment for a job waiting at the next station. Her thumb leaves a grease smudge on the grey paper. The scars across her palms are white against the ledger's lines as she turns the page back with a dry rasp. 

She slides a sealed envelope with a small purse of coins into my palm. The paper is heavy and stiff, and the coins in the canvas bag clink together once with a dull leaden sound against my knuckles. I close my fingers over the envelope and shift it into my coat pocket. 

Edie folds a paper plan into her coat, the inked lines outlining the work we will do. The blueprint crinkles as she tucks it deep into her breast pocket and buttons the wool over it. 

Without questioning, we move down the main road, the guard’s mismatched shadows flickering as we pass. Our boots strike the cobbles in an uneven sequence, my right foot dragging slightly where the salt-crusted floor of the hydro left my boots stiff at the sole. 

The dog-like creature follows at a distance, its gait oddly mechanical. It does not trot like a living animal; its shoulders drop together on every fourth step, the iron joints grinding dryly inside the casing of its chest. 

My Insight steadies, confirming that the city’s outward cheer is a false mask over hunger and decay. The bakehouse on the corner smells of fresh crust, but beneath it I smell sour fat and wet floorboards that have not dried in forty years. We vanish down the road, the extra shadow of the guard stretching ahead of us like a dark omen.
