The iron latch on the inner heavy door of Stillwell Hydro clicked when I pushed it down with my thumb. The steel door closed behind us in the steam room rebate, shutting off the smell of burnt lime and wet iron from the lower chambers. We stood in the entry hall before the double outer doors. The plaster was peeling beneath strips of iodine-stained cloth along the doorframe, dry and curling away from the damp lath underneath. I reached out with my left hand, took the heavy brass ring of the street door, and turned it until the latch cleared the keeper. 

I stepped out of Stillwell onto the cobbled street, the sky a harsh white. The light hit my face like a flat board after the dark brick corridors inside. It was not bright with sun, but diffuse and thick, filling the space between the rooflines with an even, grey-white glare that threw no sharp edges on the granite blocks beneath my boots. I stepped down three granite steps. The stone steps were damp near the risers and crusted with white mineral dust on the treads. My boots made a heavy, dry thud against the cobbles. My right arm hung down beside my coat. The right forearm was set at a five-degree inward angle at the misaligned elbow, stiff from the chill in the air, the enlarged knuckles of my hand resting against the wool of my trousers. Behind me, Maria came through the doorway, her small bundle tucked under her elbow, her shawl wrapped around Maria's shoulders and pulled tight across her chest. Edie came last, her canvas oilskin coat buttoned to the throat, her head turned slightly to keep her good right ear toward the road while the lead-weight silence in her dead left ear covered the wall side.

My Insight pricks as the daylight shows the city’s polite veneer hiding rot. The sensation started behind my eyes, a thin needle of pressure behind the bridge of my nose where the bone had been broken and reset. Across the twenty-foot width of the street, the building facades presented a clean face to the road. The butcher’s shop had a newly painted sign board, black letters on yellow wood, and the iron railings outside the apothecary were scrubbed free of horse dung. But my eye did not stay on the paint. The whitewash on the brick frontage opposite was four coats thick, applied with a coarse broom to hide the fact that the third course of masonry above the lintel had settled two inches into dry, crumbly mortar. Below the display window of the grocer, the pine timber sleeper had rotted from the wet earth up into a spongy brown mass that held the weight of the floor joists only because the joists were wedging themselves against the party wall of the house next door. The iron bracket holding the gas lamp to the corner post looked solid and black, but inside the mounting lag-screw the iron had rusted away into thin, flaking leaves of orange oxide, leaving less than a quarter-inch of sound metal inside the pine post to keep sixty pounds of cast iron from dropping onto the pavement below. The whole street was held together by tension, paint, and habit. Sand in the joins. Rust inside the lag-screws. Ten tons of brick floating on rotten pine and dry lime. It looked like a respectable row of shops if you did not look at the joins, but if you looked at the joins, the entire block was resting on air and luck. The pressure behind my nose backed off two steps, dropping from a sharp prickle to a dull, steady ache at the back of my skull, leaving the street standing as it was.

We walked thirty yards down the granite footpath toward the point where the street ran parallel to the rail cut. Beside the iron fence that separated the road from the ballast shoulder stood a rail guard. He was a broad-shouldered man in a heavy dark coat with six polished brass buttons running down the front, wearing a dark cap with a flat leather peak pulled down near his eyebrows. He held an iron-shod signal staff in his left hand and a cold brass lamp in his right, standing perfectly still on the grey limestone ballast three feet inside the fence line. 

The white sky above was flat, casting an even glare across the street, yet I saw a rail guard by the tracks, his form throwing three shadows instead of two. Beneath the flat overhead light, a man standing on open ground should have had a single faint ring of shade directly around his boot soles, or at most two overlapping grey patches from the glare off the white plastered walls across the road. The rail guard had three. From the heels of his heavy leather boots, three distinct dark shapes stretched across the crushed stone ballast and reached onto the granite curb of the footpath. The first shadow pointed north-west, wide and grey. The second shadow pointed north-east, shorter and darker where it crossed an iron sleeper clip. The third shadow pointed due east, narrow as a fence pale, dense and black as coal dust, extending eight feet across the stone to the base of the iron fence. There was no lamp lit on the post behind him. There was no sun behind the clouds to the west. The third shadow lay flat on the ground, independent of the white glare overhead, its edge sharp against the limestone chips.

A creature that looks like a dog walks beside him, its eyes glinting metal. It sat on its haunches on the ballast four inches from the guard’s right heel. It had the general size and frame of a mastiff, but its ribs did not rise or fall with breath, and its skin was hairless, dark grey, and smooth like oiled iron plate. Its front legs were longer than its hind legs, setting its spine at an upward angle toward its neck, and the joints at its elbows were square-edged, without the rounded bulge of muscle or bone. When we approached within six paces, the head turned toward us without any movement in the shoulders or back. Its head was a tapered block of dark grey material, flat on top, with two deep square sockets where the eyes should have been. Inside the sockets, two round brass fittings caught the harsh daylight, glinting with a cold, metallic reflection. It had no nostrils and no lips, only a straight horizontal seam running from the point of its snout back to the jaw hinge. It made no sound. It did not smell of an animal; it smelled of dry iron dust, cold tallow, and coal smoke. Its four feet rested on the limestone ballast, the toes tipped with short brass studs that rested on the stone without leaving marks in the dust.

Maria stopped on the granite curb two paces from the iron fence. She looked at the guard’s face, then down at his wool sleeve, then at the three black shadows stretching across the ballast. She stepped past the end of the fence rail, coming within arm’s reach of the man’s right side. 

Maria puts her hand on the guard’s sleeve, then a second time, saying nothing. Her fingers were short, her skin red from the cold air, her forearm bare where her sleeve had pulled back. She extended her right hand and placed her palm flat against the heavy dark wool of the guard’s upper arm, three inches below his shoulder seam. The guard did not turn his head. His eyes remained fixed straight ahead down the rail cut toward the distant signal gantry. His chest rose once, very slowly, but he gave no sign that he felt the weight of her hand on his coat. Maria held her palm there for four seconds, pressing her fingers slightly into the thick wool. She pulled her hand back five inches, holding her arm suspended in the air between them. She waited three seconds, her face pale, watching the dark cloth where her palm had rested. The wool showed no depression and no change. She reached out again, placing her palm on the exact same spot on his sleeve a second time. She pressed harder, her arm muscles tightening under her skin, pushing her palm into his arm until the wool bunched around her knuckles. She kept her lips pressed together, saying nothing. No words, no prayer, no sound came from her. She held the second touch for five seconds while the guard stood like an iron post under her hand. She drew her hand back, tucked her arm back inside her shawl, and stepped back onto the footpath beside me.

We stood in a line on the granite flags, six feet from the guard and the creature. I looked at Maria. She had no trunk, no copper in her apron pocket, no house in Vantry that would take her in, and no church assignment left to cover her keep. She had no place to go. I looked at Edie. Edie turned her head to the left, her good right ear facing down the road, her eyes fixed on the distant iron bridge where the eastern branch line crossed the river toward the mills. She stared at the black girders against the white sky for six seconds, her mouth set in a thin line, looking eastward without explaining what she was looking for or what lay beyond the river. 

I looked down at my own hands. My left knuckles were enlarged and thick from old fights, two fingers stiff and bent inward toward the palm. My right arm hung heavy at my side, set at its five-degree angle, the elbow misaligned inside my wool sleeve. I looked at both of them and stated my intention clearly.

"I am heading home," I said. "Back to Callow Ford. I am getting my mother out."

Neither of them answered me with a question. Maria pulled her shawl tighter over her shoulders and looked down at the granite flags between her boots. Edie turned her head back from the eastern bridge, her right eye tracking across the ballast to my face.

Edie opens a ledger, tallies the cost of the journey east, and marks a payment for a job waiting at the next station. She unbuttoned the top three buttons of her canvas coat with her right hand. She reached into her inner coat pocket and pulled out a narrow, oblong ledger bound in dark brown leather with brass corner plates. She held the ledger in her left hand, the broad flat scars across her palms from old cable work showing white against her weather-reddened skin. She pulled a short piece of black lead pencil from her waistcoat pocket. She opened the book to a page near the middle, resting the bottom edge of the leather spine against her belt buckle. She licked the point of the lead pencil with the tip of her tongue.

She wrote four lines of small, neat figures down the right-hand column of the paper, speaking the numbers as her pencil moved: "Rail fare on the gravel tender to the junction, four shillings. Timber tax at the valley bridge, sixpence. Bread, lard, and small beer for three, two shillings two pence. Carriage hire from the railhead to the Ford, two shillings."

She drew a single straight line beneath the column using her thumbnail as a ruler. She added the figures without pausing, her pencil hovering an inch above the paper.

"8s 8d," she said.

She flipped the page over to a fresh grid. At the top of the page she wrote the name of a place in thick lead strokes: *Micklethwaite Yard*. Below it, she wrote two lines of trade terms: *Shaft three timber inspection. Wall plate settlement and tie-rod strain.* At the right margin, she marked the agreed fee: *1l 5s 0d.*

"The payment is logged for the junction station," Edie said, her voice flat and even. "The stationmaster has the contract held in the office. We do the inspection on the way through, we collect the twenty-five shillings, and the fare home is clear."

She closed the ledger with a soft snap of the leather cover. She did not ask if I agreed to the stop or if Maria was fit to walk the three miles down the line. She reached into her right jacket pocket and pulled out two objects: a thick cream-coloured paper envelope, three inches by five, and a small coarse linen purse tied at the neck with a piece of tarred string.

She slides a sealed envelope with a small purse of coins into my palm. I held out my left hand, turning the palm up, my stiff fingers resting flat against one another. Edie placed the heavy paper envelope onto my palm first. It was thick, stiff paper, sealed on the back flap with a round smudge of red sealing wax that had been pressed flat with a smooth button. On top of the envelope she set the linen purse. The purse was heavy for its size, about the weight of a lead fishing sinker, and I could feel four silver coins and six copper pennies clinking through the rough cloth when her hand let go.

"That is the residue from the first survey," Edie said. "Your share after the equipment deduction. Put it inside your coat."

I slid the envelope and the purse into the inner breast pocket of my jacket, pushing them down deep beside my ribs where they sat heavy against my wool shirt.

Edie reached into her side pocket again and took out a folded sheet of grid paper. She unfolded it once, revealing ink lines drawn with a fine steel pen. Edie folds a paper plan into her coat, the inked lines outlining the work we will do. The drawing showed the cross-section of a deep vertical timbered shaft, with figures marked along the margins in yards and feet, and small hatched lines indicating where the side-walls had bulged under pressure. It was a clear, mechanical layout of the bracing work required at the Micklethwaite site. She checked one figure near the lower timber frame, ran her scarred thumb along the bottom line, and then folded the paper back along its original creases—once lengthwise, once across, then twice more until it was a thick rectangle four inches wide. She slid the paper plan into her coat lining, buttoned her canvas jacket back up to her chin, and tucked her hands back into her deep side pockets.

Without questioning, we move down the main road, the guard’s mismatched shadows flickering as we pass. Edie stepped off first, turning her right side toward the direction of travel, walking along the edge of the granite curb. I stepped in behind her, my misaligned right arm swinging slightly at my hip, and Maria followed three paces behind me, her boots making a quiet, quick rhythm on the stone. We walked past the rail guard where he stood inside the fence line. As I passed within three feet of his iron-shod staff, I kept my eyes on his feet. The three black shadows stretching across the ballast did not stay still as our light moved. The third shadow—the long, narrow black strip pointing due east—flickered twice, its far tip twitching three inches to the left across the crushed stone before snapping back into line with his heel, though there was no lamp moving in the street and no change in the harsh white sky overhead.

We passed the end of the iron rail fence and entered the long stretch of road leading east toward the industrial yards. Behind us, fifteen yards back, I heard a sound on the road surface. It was a regular, metallic striking sound: *clack-clack, thud. Clack-clack, thud.*

The dog-like creature follows at a distance, its gait oddly mechanical. I turned my head over my right shoulder to look back down the street. The quadruped had stepped off the ballast cut and was walking on the granite footpath behind Maria. It maintained a steady distance of fifteen paces. Its legs moved in paired sets—the front right and rear left stepping together, then the front left and rear right—with no sway in its back or movement in its neck. The square iron-grey head remained perfectly level, suspended two feet above the granite flags, its brass-glinting eye sockets locked onto the back of Maria’s boots. When a butcher’s cart pulled out from a side alley between us and the creature, the quadruped did not stop or alter its course; it passed within six inches of the horse’s rear hoof, its brass-shod toes clicking on the cobbles with the exact same spacing and timing as before. The horse did not turn its head or kick out; it moved past as if the grey thing were not on the road at all.

We walked past the row of three-story brick dwellings that lined the eastern exit of the district. My Insight steadies, confirming that the city’s outward cheer is a false mask over hunger and decay. The prickle behind my nose was gone, replaced by a cold, transparent clarity that let me see the street for what was built into it. The brass fittings on the doors were polished bright with vinegar, but behind the oak panels, the larders were empty, containing only salt jars and dry bread crusts. Through the low ground-floor window of a cooper’s shop, I saw a woman sitting on an upside-down tub, her hands wrapped in grey wool rags, watching a pot of water boil over three cold coals that gave off no smoke. On the corner, a boy in a clean blue smock stood selling paper broadsheets, calling out the news of the senate in a high, clear voice, but his collarbone showed sharp and blue through his collar, and his ankles above his shoes were swollen twice their natural size from lack of food. The freshly painted wooden facades, the scrubbed steps, the neat brass door-knockers, and the tidy iron lamp posts were nothing more than a thin layer of grease rubbed over a dying carcass to make it shine in the white light. The city was starving itself from the inside out while it kept its coat buttoned up to the neck.

We moved down the long straight run of the road, where the granite cobbles gave way to packed cinders and iron slag near the rail yards. The buildings grew lower and wider, turned into timber-clad warehouses with rusted iron roofs and cold brick chimneys. We vanished down the road, the extra shadow of the guard stretching ahead of us like a dark omen.
