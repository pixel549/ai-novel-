I step out of Stillwell onto the cobbled street, the sky a harsh white. My right forearm is set at a five-degree inward angle with a misaligned elbow where Maria set the bone years back, and it hangs heavy against my wool trousers. The air hits my face with the cold smell of burnt lime and wet iron lingering in my nostrils from the steam rooms behind us, thick enough to taste on the back of my tongue. Edie walks beside me, her step even and short, her dead left ear turned away from the passing cart traffic while she keeps her eyes fixed straight ahead on the limestone fronts. Maria follows close behind her, her hand tucked into the coarse wool of her shawl as if she were trying to hold herself together against the wind coming off the canal. 

My Insight pricks as the daylight shows the city’s polite veneer hiding rot. The limestone facades of the terrace houses look clean enough from twenty paces, washed down by the municipal board before the winter freeze set in, but where the damp rises from the gutters, the mortar has turned to gray meal and the wood beneath the sills is black with dry rot. I stare at a drainpipe where the rust has eaten through the iron in three places, leaving jagged red teeth that bite into the stone and leave long orange stains down the white stucco like old dried blood run thin in the rain. 

I see a rail guard by the tracks, his form throwing three shadows instead of two under the washed-out glare of the midday sun. The shadows stretch long and thin across the granite setts by the freight spur, one pointing south toward the river, one angling toward the switch box by the signal pole, and a third that stays vertical and thick behind his heels like an unhitched post driven straight down through his boots. The guard stands with his thumbs hooked into his belt, staring past the coal tenders with a blankness that has nothing to do with boredom. 

A creature that looks like a dog walks beside him, its eyes glinting metal. It has four legs of dull-cast iron and a square chest that ticks with the muffled sound of a clockwork spring winding down against its ribs. It stops when the guard stops, its metal paws clicking sharp against the flat stones with a dry, rhythmic sound that sets my teeth on edge behind my lips. 

Maria puts her hand on the guard’s sleeve, then a second time, saying nothing. Her fingers press against the coarse blue wool of his uniform coat, smoothing the fabric once and then again in a small, circular motion as if she were checking to see if there was any warmth left underneath the cloth. The guard does not turn his head to look at her fingers resting on his sleeve. He keeps his gaze fixed on the empty rails where the coal carts used to run before the strike last autumn. Maria draws her hand back slowly and wipes her palm down her thigh, her knuckles pale against the dark stuff of her skirt. 

I note that Maria has no place to go, Edie looks eastward without explaining, and I state I’m heading home to get my mother out. The words come out flat in the cold air, small and grey like gravel dropped on ice. Edie does not look at me when I say it; she never looks when someone talks about what they are leaving behind or what they expect to find at the end of a line. She reaches into the deep pocket of her grey coat and pulls out an oilcloth ledger with grease-stained corners. 

Edie opens a ledger, tallies the cost of the journey east, and marks a payment for a job waiting at the next station. Her thumb leaves a grease smudge on the grey paper where she holds the page down against the wind. The scars across her palms are white and flat against the ledger's blue lines as she turns the leaf back with a dry rasp of paper against paper, her eyes scanning the columns of figures with the same speed she used to check shoring timbers in the drift. 

She slides a sealed envelope with a small purse of coins into my palm. The paper is heavy and stiff, smelling of glue and damp warehouse floors, and the coins in the canvas bag clink together once with a dull leaden sound against my knuckles as they settle into my grip. I close my fingers over the envelope and shift it into my coat pocket, feeling the corners catch against the lining where the thread has come loose at the seam. 

Edie folds a paper plan into her coat, the inked lines outlining the work we will do. The blueprint crinkles with a sharp dry sound as she tucks it deep into her breast pocket and buttons the heavy wool over it, pressing her palm flat against the chest-piece to make sure the paper has settled square against the wool. 

Without questioning, we move down the main road, the guard’s mismatched shadows flickering as we pass. Our boots strike the cobbles in an uneven sequence, my right foot dragging slightly where the salt-crusted floor of the hydro left my boots stiff at the sole, the leather refusing to bend the way it ought to around the ball of my foot. 

The dog-like creature follows at a distance, its gait oddly mechanical. It does not trot like a living animal; its shoulders drop together on every fourth step, the iron joints grinding dryly inside the casing of its chest like ungreased gears turning in an empty box. 

My Insight steadies, confirming that the city’s outward cheer is a false mask over hunger and decay. The bakehouse on the corner smells of fresh crust and burnt sugar, but beneath it I smell sour fat and wet floorboards that have not dried in forty years, the old mortar sweating salt through the whitewash. We vanish down the road, the extra shadow of the guard stretching ahead of us like a dark omen on the stones.
