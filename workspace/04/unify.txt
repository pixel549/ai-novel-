We walked in single file through the cut below the chapel. The passage was two meters wide and two point four meters high, driven through dark basalt masonry with dressed granite headers placed every four courses. The floor sloped downward at three degrees toward the lower drift. A shallow drainage channel ran along the left footing, two centimeters deep, choked with coal-wash and fine silt. The water moved slow toward the lower workings.

I took the rear position, three meters behind Maria. Maria kept two meters of distance behind Lloyd. Lloyd walked with long, heavy strides, his broad shoulders filling the centre of the vault. His right arm hung slightly clear of his hip, set at a bad inward angle where the broken elbow had knit crooked years back. In his left hand he carried the unlit oil lantern by its wire bail. The bail tapped against his belt buckle with every second stride.

Light came from the ball of soft yellow Arcana Maria held over her right collarbone. It floated forty centimeters above her shoulder, throwing clean, hard shadows across the wet stone floor.

I watched the joints of the wall blocks as we moved. The mortar was river sand and lime, five millimeters thick, intact across every bed seam and cross joint. There was no spall on the lower courses, no sign of roof sag, and no timber sets. The temperature was five degrees lower than the chapel floor above. The air tasted of damp rock, coal-gas, and cold standing water.

In my left ear, the lead-weight silence from the old camouflet kept that side of the passage dead. I held my head turned three degrees to the right so my working ear could pick up their footfalls. Lloyd’s boots made a heavy, iron-shod thud on the flagstones. Maria’s boots clicked light and fast on the dry centre. My own pace was quiet along the wet margin by the drain.

We covered forty meters before Lloyd slowed down. His iron heel caps rasped twice against the basalt flags. He came to a full stop at the forty-five meter mark.

"There is a woman standing at the far end," Lloyd said.

His voice was short and flat. He did not turn to face us. He stood square in the middle of the passage, his head tilted slightly up toward the roof line.

I stopped two meters behind Maria. I shifted one step to my right to clear my sightline past Lloyd’s broad shoulder.

The corridor ran straight ahead for another eighteen meters before ending against a solid bulkhead of raw granite. The face was unexcavated, rough-chipped with hand drills, showing five half-shafts along the top course where the charge holes had been driven and cleared. The rock was grey, wet, and slick with mineral condensation.

No figure stood against the granite face. There was no shadow cast across the drill marks, no dark cloth, no silhouette, and no movement in the air.

"I see cold rock," I said. "Nothing else."

Lloyd did not lower his chin. He shifted his weight to his right heel, bringing his heavy boot down into the wet silt of the drainage channel.

"She has her back to the rock," Lloyd said. "Dark dress. Iron pins in the collar."

I looked at the granite face a second time. I measured the span with my eyes. Eighteen meters from my boots to the stone face. The yellow light from Maria’s floating sphere reached fifteen meters before dropping into grey shadow. The lower footing was plain. Three loose spalls of stone, each under ten centimeters across, rested in the silt near the drain outlet. Nothing stood on them. Nothing broke the line of the rock.

Lloyd turned his head twenty degrees to the left. He raised his left hand, pointing at the wall two meters ahead of us on the left side of the passage.

"There is a second Maria beside the first," Lloyd said.

I turned my head to bring my working right ear forward. I looked straight at Maria. She stood two meters in front of me, her frame turned toward the left wall. She had her dark wool shawl pulled tight across her chest, her fingers hooked into the weave near her throat. In her left hand she held her hemp knot-cord bound with brass ferrules. She was the only person standing in that section of the passage.

I surveyed the space between her left arm and the basalt wall. The clear gap measured sixty centimeters. The wall blocks were smooth grey stone, coated in a thin film of mineral salts. There was no second body occupying those sixty centimeters. There was no shadow on the block work, no mist of exhaled breath in the cold air, and no sound of wool brushing against rough mortar.

"There is one Maria," I said. "She is standing two meters ahead of me."

Lloyd did not answer me. He took two long steps forward, his iron heel caps splashing in the silt channel. He stopped facing the right wall of the corridor.

The wall at that point was built of six courses of ashlar blocks, each block eighty centimeters long by thirty centimeters high. Lloyd raised his right hand—the crooked arm—and extended his index finger until it rested five centimeters off the center face of a middle block.

"The door is here," Lloyd said.

I moved forward three paces, passing Maria on her right side, and pulled up ten centimeters from Lloyd. I looked at the block he pointed to.

It was solid grey basalt, streaked with rust stains from iron run-off. The vertical seam to its left was three millimeters wide, filled with hard grey mortar without hairline cracks. The vertical seam to its right was four millimeters wide, equally tight. The horizontal bed course directly above carried forty centimeters of solid stone, bearing the vault load without an iron lintel, a timber frame, or a keystone break.

I pulled off my right glove and pressed my bare palm flat against the stone face. The flat scars across my palm from old winch cables rasped on the rough grain of the basalt. The rock was cold, damp, and solid. I set my right shoulder against the face and leaned into it, putting fifty kilograms of forward force against the block. It did not budge. I dragged my fingertips down the vertical seams on both sides. The mortar was continuous and dry under the surface moisture. There was no mechanical clearance, no drop-latch, no hidden hinge pin, and no draft of air moving through the seam.

"That is a load-bearing wall block," I said. "There is no frame, no seam, and no door."

Lloyd kept his arm extended. His index finger stayed five centimeters off the wet stone face. He did not turn his head toward me. He stared into the flat grey block as if he were looking down an open drift.

Maria stepped up into the gap between Lloyd and me. Her left shoulder brushed my sleeve as she passed. She stopped twenty centimeters from the ashlar block.

First she looked down the passage toward the eighteen-meter mark where the raw granite stopped the cut. Then she looked to her left side, turning her head to examine the empty space beside her shawl. Finally she looked at the middle basalt block where Lloyd’s finger pointed.

She raised her right hand. She did not lay her skin against the rock. She held her palm open five centimeters above Lloyd’s finger, facing the bare basalt.

"The woman is at the end of the hall," Maria said. Her voice was level, without heat or tremor. "The second Maria is standing beside me. And the door is right here."

She turned her head to look me in the face. Her features were plain and steady in the yellow Arcana light. Her fingers remained hooked in the wool of her shawl.

"If Lloyd sees it, and I say it is real, then it is real for us," Maria said. "That is the rule."

I stood still. I looked at Maria’s face, then at Lloyd’s extended finger, then at the solid basalt block. The mortar in the bed seams was continuous. The vault overhead showed no transfer of structural weight to a hidden header. By every principle of masonry, shoring, and timber work, the tunnel ended eighteen meters ahead in raw rock and held no opening on the right side.

"Understood," I said.

The moment I spoke the word, Lloyd moved. He did not brace his footing or shift his balance. He reached out with his physical right hand and placed his palm against the grey basalt block where my scarred skin had been two seconds prior.

He did not shove with muscle. He swung his right arm forward as if he were pushing a light pine hatch on greased iron hinges.

The solid basalt moved. A rectangular section of the load-bearing wall, two meters high and eighty centimeters wide, swung inward into the space beyond. The movement made no sound. No stone ground against stone, no dried mortar flaked from the joints, and no spall dropped into the silt channel. The mortar seams did not crack or crumble; they separated along clean, straight lines that had not existed when I ran my bare fingers over them.

Behind the swung block lay an open chamber. The air that spilled out was six degrees warmer than the passage air and smelled of dry timber, lime, and undisturbed dust.

Lloyd did not step through immediately. He stood on the threshold, his right palm resting against the cut edge of the open stone slab.

I looked at the flagstones beneath his boots. The basalt floor was continuous, running straight from the main passage into the dark chamber without a break. There was no sill, no step, and no iron catch plate.

I decided to test the rule.

I looked at a spot on the flagstone ten centimeters in front of Lloyd’s left boot. The rock there was bare, grey, and slick with a thin film of water running toward the drain.

"There is a small brass lever on the floor right there," I said. I pointed my gloved index finger straight at the wet flagstone. "Three inches long, set into an iron base plate."

Lloyd stopped. He held his foot above the threshold. His head dropped three degrees as he looked down at the spot I was pointing to. His jaw tightened until the muscles showed hard along his neck. He stayed motionless for four seconds, staring at the empty flagstone.

Then Lloyd turned his head forty-five degrees to the left. He did not look at my face or my pointed finger. He looked past my shoulder at Maria.

"Maria," Lloyd said. His voice was lower than before, stripped down to bare bone. "Is there a lever?"

Maria stepped up beside his shoulder. She looked down at the flagstone where my finger pointed. She examined the bare wet stone, the thin film of water moving toward the drainage channel, and the absolute absence of any brass fitting or iron plate.

"There is no lever on the floor, Lloyd," Maria said. Her tone was clear and direct. "The floor is clear stone."

Lloyd looked down at the flagstone for one second. Then he lifted his left boot, stepped straight over the spot I had pointed out, and walked into the dark chamber beyond. He did not look back at me. He did not ask why I had spoken the lie. He passed over what I had said as if I had made no sound in the passage at all.

I lowered my hand and pulled my right glove back on, drawing the stiff leather down over my knuckles until the fit was snug.

I watched Lloyd take six heavy paces into the dark room. He held the unlit lantern low in his left hand, its wire bail clicking against his belt. He moved with complete physical certainty through space that had been forty centimeters of solid basalt thirty seconds ago.

He had not questioned Maria’s verdict. His own eyes had shown him whatever his Insight brought up—the woman at the bulkhead, the second Maria, the doorway through the load-bearing masonry—but he required her voice before he would treat any of it as physical matter. When I offered him a detail that contradicted the passage, he had not relied on his eyes or on mine. He had waited for Maria to set the line.

Maria was his interface. Lloyd lived in a place where solid rock had doorways, clear corridors held dead bodies, and one person stood in two spots at once. He could not move through that place on his own without walking face-first into solid ashlar or stepping off a real ledge into a shaft. He needed a filter—someone who could take the noise in his head, weigh it against the masonry, and give him a single rule for what he could touch, step on, or open.

Maria gave him the rule. She did it without anger, without hesitation, and without asking him to account for what he saw. She looked where he pointed, made her assessment, and spoke the result aloud for him. Once she declared a thing real, Lloyd acted on it with his full weight. Once she declared it absent, it vanished from his accounting, even if I stood right beside him pointing a finger at the stone.

My own eyes saw none of the visions. I did not see the woman at the eighteen-meter mark. I did not see the duplicate Maria. I did not see the doorway until Lloyd pushed the stone back, and I did not see the brass lever because I had invented it.

My perception was flat, plain, and uncorrupted by Insight or Arcana. I saw thirty-centimeter ashlar blocks, four-millimeter mortar seams, five-degree misalignments in bone, and two-centimeter drainage channels. My eyes registered the physical baseline—the load-bearing reality of stone, mortar, and iron that remained when the visions were stripped away.

I was the baseline for the three of us. Lloyd provided the breaches, Maria provided the rules for crossing them, and I provided the actual dimensions of the tunnel. If the timbering gave way, if the roof sagged under water weight, or if a real iron bolt needed to be turned with a wrench, my sight was the only one in the passage that measured the span without distortion.

There was a quiet gratitude in being the dull one. It was economical. It required no triage, no second-guessing, and no constant effort to separate reality from delusion. A wall was a wall until it swung open. A floor was solid flagstone until it dropped away. My deaf left ear heard nothing from the quiet, and my working eye recorded only the spall, the joint work, and the footings. It kept my hands steady on the cable work and my boots set square on the rock.

Maria walked past me into the opening. She stepped carefully over the cut edge, her boots making a dry crunch on the dust inside the chamber. The light sphere above her shoulder followed her, drifting through the stone frame and casting soft yellow light across the interior walls.

I stayed on the corridor side of the threshold for three seconds. I looked at the cut edge of the open door. The slab was thirty centimeters thick, composed of three solid courses of ashlar basalt that had split cleanly without leaving a rough edge or a grain of rubble. The surface of the cut was as smooth as honed slate.

I reached out and pressed my gloved thumb against the cut edge of the door. The stone was cold and real. It held my weight when I leaned my shoulder against it.

As my rear foot cleared the stone jamb and I stepped into the chamber, I turned my head to look back down the main passage we had left.

The Arcana light floating above Maria’s shoulder threw its glow back through the open doorway, lighting the long, straight corridor behind us. Eighteen meters down, at the end of the passage, the solid granite bulkhead stood bare and wet in the yellow light. The three loose spalls rested untouched in the silt by the drain. The faint glow at the tunnel’s mouth swallows the darkness behind us.
