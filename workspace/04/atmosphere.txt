We walked in single file down the passage beyond the chapel. The corridor was two point two meters wide and two point four meters high, cut through dense basalt masonry with dressed granite headers every four courses. The floor sloped downward at an angle of three degrees. Water ran in a shallow channel along the left footing, two centimeters deep, black with fine silt and coal-wash, moving toward the lower drift.

I stepped at the rear, three meters behind Maria. Maria walked two meters behind Lloyd. Lloyd took long, heavy strides, his broad shoulders filling the middle of the vault. His right forearm hung slightly away from his hip, set at a five-degree inward angle where the misaligned elbow had knit wrong. In his left hand he held the unlit oil lantern by its wire bail. Light came from the ball of soft yellow Arcana Maria held above her shoulder, floating forty centimeters over her right collarbone and throwing clean, sharp shadows across the wet stone floor.

I kept my eyes on the joints of the wall blocks. The mortar was lime and river sand, five millimeters thick, intact across every seam. There was no sign of settling, no spall on the lower courses, and no timber shoring. The air was five degrees colder than the chapel above, smelling of damp rock, coal-gas, and standing water. In my left ear, the lead-weight silence from the old camouflet kept the left side of the passage dead, so I turned my head slightly to use my right ear to track their steps. Lloyd’s boots made a thick, heavy thud on the basalt flags. Maria’s boots clicked higher and lighter. My own pace was quiet on the wet margin.

We covered forty meters before Lloyd slowed down. His iron-heeled boots scraped twice on the flagstone. He stopped at the forty-five meter mark.

"There is a woman standing at the far end," Lloyd said.

His voice was short and flat. He did not turn around. He stood in the middle of the corridor, his head tilted three degrees up.

I stopped two meters behind Maria. I stepped to my right to look past Lloyd’s shoulder. The corridor ran straight ahead for another eighteen meters before terminating in a solid bulkhead of unexcavated granite face. The stone was rough, chipped out with hand drills, showing five drill-hole half-shafts along the top course. The face was wet and grey, slick with condensation. There was no figure standing against it. There was no shadow, no dark cloth, no outline, and no movement.

"I see cold rock," I said. "Nothing else."

Lloyd did not lower his head. He shifted his weight to his right heel, moving his boot five centimeters to the side into the wet silt.

"She has her back to the wall," Lloyd said. His knuckles on the lantern bail were white and thick. "She is wearing a dark dress with iron pins in the collar."

I looked at the granite face again. I measured the span with my eyes. Eighteen meters to the stone face. The yellow light from Maria’s sphere reached fifteen meters before dropping off into shadow. The stone was bare. I looked down at the footing where the wall met the floor. Three loose rocks, each under ten centimeters across, sat in the silt by the water drain. Nothing stood on them.

Lloyd turned his head twenty degrees to the left. He raised his left hand, pointing at the wall two meters ahead of us, on the left side of the passage.

"There is a second Maria beside the first," Lloyd said.

I turned my head to bring my functional right ear toward him. I looked directly at Maria. She stood two meters in front of me, her body turned slightly toward the left wall. She had her shawl wrapped around her shoulders, her fingers hooked into the dark weave near her throat. In her left hand she held the hemp knot-cord bound with brass wire ferrules. She was the only person standing in that section of the passage.

I looked at the space immediately to her left. The gap between Maria’s left sleeve and the basalt wall was sixty centimeters. The wall blocks were smooth, coated in a thin film of damp mineral crust. There was no second body in that sixty-centimeter gap. There was no shadow cast against the wet block work, no breath vapor in the cold air, and no sound of wool rustling against stone.

"There is one Maria," I said. "She is standing two meters ahead of me."

Lloyd did not answer. He took two steps forward, his boots splashing in the shallow drain channel. He stopped facing the right-hand wall of the corridor. The wall here was six courses of ashlar blocks, each block eighty centimeters long and thirty centimeters high. He raised his right hand—the crooked arm—and extended his index finger until it was five centimeters from the centre of a middle block.

"The door is here," Lloyd said.

I moved forward three paces, passing Maria on her right, and stopped ten centimeters from Lloyd. I looked at the block he pointed to. It was solid grey basalt, stained with iron run-off. The vertical seam to its left was three millimeters wide, filled with grey mortar that had no cracks. The vertical seam to its right was four millimeters wide, similarly tight. The horizontal course above it held forty centimeters of solid stone, carrying the load of the vault without an iron lintel, a timber frame, or a keystone break.

I took off my right glove and pressed my bare hand flat against the face of the block. The flat scars across my palms from old cable work rasped against the rough grain of the basalt. The stone was cold, damp, and solid. I set my shoulder against the face and applied fifty kilograms of forward pressure. The block did not shift. I ran my fingertips down the vertical seams on both sides. The mortar was continuous. There was no mechanical clearance, no hidden hinge pin, no drop-latch, and no draft of air passing through the joint.

"That is a load-bearing wall block," I said. "There is no frame, no seam, and no door."

Lloyd kept his arm extended. His finger remained five centimeters from the stone. He did not look at me. He kept his eyes fixed on the blank grey block as if he were staring into an open drift.

Maria stepped forward. She moved into the space between Lloyd and me, her shoulder brushing my left arm. She stopped twenty centimeters from the wall block. She looked down the corridor toward the eighteen-meter mark where the granite bulkhead stopped the passage. She looked to her own left side, turning her head to examine the empty space beside her shawl. Then she looked directly at the basalt block where Lloyd’s finger pointed.

She raised her right hand. She did not touch the stone. She held her hand five centimeters above Lloyd’s pointing finger, her palm open toward the rock.

"The woman is at the end of the hall," Maria said. Her voice was steady and quiet. "The second Maria is standing beside me. And the door is right here."

She turned her head to look at me. Her face was calm in the yellow Arcana light. Her fingers rested lightly against her shawl.

"If Lloyd sees it, and I say it is real, then it is real for us," Maria said. "That is the rule."

I stood still. I looked at Maria’s face, then at Lloyd’s finger, then at the solid basalt wall. The mortar in the seams was unbroken. The granite overhead showed no transfer of weight to an inner lintel. By every metric of structural masonry, shoring, and physical survey, the corridor ended eighteen meters ahead in solid rock and held no opening on the right side.

"Understood," I said.

The moment Maria spoke, Lloyd moved. He did not hesitate, adjust his footing, or brace his legs. He reached out with his physical right hand—the crooked arm—and laid his palm flat against the basalt block where my scarred hand had rested two seconds before.

He did not push with force. He swung his arm forward as if he were opening a light pine panel on greased iron hinges.

The solid basalt stone moved. A rectangular section of the wall, two meters high and eighty centimeters wide, swung inward into the space beyond. The motion made no sound. No stone ground against stone, no mortar flaked from the joints, and no spall fell to the floor. The mortar seams did not crack; they simply stopped being contiguous, separating along clean lines that had not existed when I ran my bare fingers down them.

Behind the swung stone was an open chamber. The air that came out of it was six degrees warmer than the corridor air and smelled of dry timber and old lime.

Lloyd did not step through immediately. He remained standing on the threshold, his right palm resting on the edge of the swung stone slab.

I looked at the floor beneath his boots. The basalt flagstones were unbroken, running continuously from the corridor into the dark space beyond. There was no step, no sill, and no iron catch.

I decided to test the rule.

I looked at a spot on the floor ten centimeters in front of Lloyd’s left boot, where the stone was completely bare, grey, and wet with a thin film of drain water.

"There is a small brass lever on the floor right there," I said. I pointed my gloved index finger directly at the bare stone. "It is three inches long, mounted on an iron base plate."

Lloyd stopped. He did not step into the room. His head dropped three degrees as he looked down at the floor where I was pointing. His jaw set hard. He remained motionless for four seconds, his eyes fixed on the empty flagstone.

Then Lloyd turned his head forty-five degrees to his left. He did not look at my face or my hand. He looked past my shoulder at Maria.

"Maria," Lloyd said. His voice dropped lower, trimmed down to the bone. "Is there a lever?"

Maria stepped up beside him. She looked down at the floor where my finger pointed. She looked at the bare wet stone, the thin skin of water moving toward the drain, and the lack of any metal fitting, iron plate, or raised brass.

"There is no lever on the floor, Lloyd," Maria said. Her tone was direct, without edge. "The floor is clear stone."

Lloyd looked back down at the flagstone for one second. Then he lifted his left boot, stepped straight over the spot I had pointed out, and walked through the opened wall into the dark chamber beyond. He did not look back at me. He did not ask why I had spoken. He ignored my lie completely, as if I had made no sound at all.

I lowered my hand and pulled my right glove back on, smoothing the stiff leather over my knuckles.

I watched Lloyd move six paces into the dark room. He held the unlit lantern low in his left hand, the wire bail clicking faintly against his belt buckle. He moved with total certainty, taking long, heavy steps through a volume of space that had been solid rock thirty seconds earlier.

He had not questioned Maria’s verdict. His own eyes had shown him whatever his Insight produced—the woman at the dead end, the duplicate Maria, the doorway in the load-bearing block—but he had required her word before he would treat any of it as a physical fact. When I offered him a detail that contradicted the room, he had not trusted his own eyes or mine. He had waited for Maria to clear the space.

Maria walked past me into the opening. She stepped carefully over the threshold, her boots making a dry crunch on the floor inside. The light sphere above her shoulder followed her, drifting through the stone opening and casting yellow light across the interior of the dark room.

I stayed on the corridor side of the threshold for three seconds. I looked at the edge of the opened stone wall. The slab was thirty centimeters thick, composed of three solid courses of basalt ashlar that had split cleanly without leaving a rough edge or a trace of rubble. The surface of the cut was as smooth as honed slate.

I reached out and pressed my gloved thumb against the cut edge of the open door. The stone was cold and solid. It held my weight when I leaned into it.

Maria was Lloyd’s interface. He lived in a place where walls were doorways, empty corridors contained people, and single bodies existed in two places at once. He could not move through that place alone without walking into solid stone or stepping off a real ledge. He needed a filter—someone who could take the clutter of his sight, weigh it against the world, and give him a single rule for what he was allowed to touch, step on, or open.

Maria gave him that rule. She did it without anger, without hesitation, and without asking him to explain what he saw. She looked where he pointed, made her decision, and spoke it into existence for him. Once she declared a detail real, Lloyd acted on it with absolute physical weight. Once she declared it false, it ceased to exist for him, even if I stood next to him pointing a finger at it.

My own eyes saw none of it. I did not see the woman at the corridor’s end. I did not see the second Maria. I did not see the door until Lloyd pushed it open, and I did not see the lever because I had lied about it.

My sight was plain, flat, and uncorrupted by Insight or Arcana. I saw thirty-centimeter ashlar blocks, four-millimeter mortar seams, five-degree misalignments in bone, and two-centimeter water channels. My eyes registered the physical baseline—the load-bearing framework of stone, timber, and iron that remained when all the visions were stripped away.

I was the baseline for the three of us. Lloyd provided the breaches, Maria provided the rules for crossing them, and I provided the actual dimensions of the tunnel. If the shoring failed, if the roof sagged, or if a real iron bolt needed to be turned with a wrench, my plain perception was the only thing in the passage that measured the span correctly.

It was quiet work being the dull one. It was economical. It required no interpretation, no second-guessing, and no constant triage of what was real and what was delusion. The wall was a wall until it swung open. The floor was stone until it dropped out. My dead left ear heard nothing from the void, and my eyes recorded only the spall and the joint work. It kept my hands steady on the cable work and my boots set on the footings.

"Edie," Maria called from inside the chamber. Her voice bounced off smooth, plastered walls. "The floor is dry here. The passage continues twenty meters to a stair."

"Coming," I said.

I stepped over the threshold into the new chamber. As my rear foot cleared the stone jamb, I turned my head to look back down the main corridor we had just left.

The sphere of Arcana light floating over Maria’s shoulder threw its yellow glow back through the open door, illuminating the long, straight passage behind us. Eighteen meters down, at the end of the hall, the solid granite bulkhead stood bare and wet in the dim light. The three loose rocks sat untouched in the silt by the water drain. The faint glow at the tunnel’s mouth flickered once against the wet stone wall, then settled back into darkness behind us.
