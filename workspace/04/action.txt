We walked in single file down the passage beyond the chapel. The corridor was two point two meters wide and two point four meters high, cut straight through dense basalt masonry with dressed granite headers every four courses. The floor sloped downward at an angle of three degrees. Water ran in a shallow channel along the left footing, two centimeters deep, moving slowly toward the deeper workings.

I stepped at the rear, three meters behind Maria. Maria walked two meters behind Lloyd. Lloyd took long, heavy strides, his broad shoulders filling the center of the vault. His right arm hung slightly away from his side, the forearm set at a five-degree inward angle where the misaligned elbow had knit. In his left hand he held the unlit oil lantern by its wire bale. Light came from the small sphere of soft yellow Arcana Maria held above her shoulder, floating forty centimeters over her right ear and casting clear shadows across the stone floor.

I kept my eyes on the joints of the wall blocks. The mortar was lime and river sand, five millimeters thick, intact across every seam. There was no sign of settling or timber shoring. The air was five degrees colder than the room above, smelling of damp stone and stagnant water. In my left ear, the lead-weight silence from the old camouflet kept the left side of the corridor dead to sound, so I used my right ear to track their footsteps. Lloyd’s boots made a dull, heavy thud on the basalt flags. Maria’s boots clicked higher and lighter. My own pace was silent on the wet edges.

We covered forty meters before Lloyd slowed his pace. His boots dragged twice on the stone floor. He stopped completely at the forty-five meter mark.

"There is a woman standing at the far end," Lloyd said.

His voice was flat and short. He did not turn around. He stood in the center of the corridor, his head tilted three degrees up.

I stopped two meters behind Maria. I leaned to my right to look past Lloyd’s shoulder. The corridor extended straight ahead for another eighteen meters before terminating in a dead-end bulkhead of unexcavated granite rock. The surface was rough-faced, chipped out with hand drills, showing five drill-hole half-shafts along the top edge. The stone was wet and grey. There was no figure standing against it. There was no shadow, no clothing, no outline, and no movement.

"I see cold rock," I said. "Nothing else."

Lloyd did not lower his head. He shifted his weight to his right heel, adjusting his stance by five centimeters.

"She has her back to the wall," Lloyd said. "She is wearing a dark dress with iron pins in the collar."

I looked at the stone again. I measured the distance with my eyes. Eighteen meters to the rock face. The light from Maria’s sphere reached fifteen meters before dropping off into dim shadow. The stone was bare. I looked at the floor at the base of the wall. There were three loose rocks, each less than ten centimeters across, sitting in the silt near the drain channel. Nothing was standing on them.

Lloyd shifted his head twenty degrees to the left. He raised his left hand and pointed at the wall two meters ahead of us, on the left side of the passage.

"There is a second Maria beside the first," Lloyd said.

I turned my head to bring my functional right ear toward him. I looked directly at Maria. She stood two meters in front of me, her body angled thirty degrees to the left. She had her shawl wrapped tight around her shoulders, her fingers tucked into the weave near her collarbone. In her left hand she held the hemp knot-cord bound with brass wire ferrules. She was the only person standing in that section of the tunnel.

I looked at the space immediately to her left. The clearance between Maria’s left arm and the left wall was sixty centimeters. The wall blocks were smooth basalt, covered in a thin film of damp mineral crust. There was no second body standing in that sixty-centimeter gap. There was no shadow cast against the wet block work, no breath vapor in the cold air, and no sound of footfall or cloth movement.

"There is one Maria," I said. "She is standing two meters ahead of me."

Lloyd did not answer me. He took two steps forward, his boots making two distinct wet splashes in the shallow drain channel. He stopped facing the right-hand wall of the corridor. The wall here was made of six courses of ashlar blocks, each block eighty centimeters long and thirty centimeters high. He raised his right hand—the misaligned arm—and extended his index finger until it was five centimeters from the center of a middle block.

"The door is here," Lloyd said.

I moved forward three paces, passing Maria on her right side, and stopped ten centimeters from Lloyd. I looked at the block he pointed to. It was solid grey basalt. The vertical seam to its left was three millimeters wide, filled with grey mortar that had not cracked. The vertical seam to its right was four millimeters wide, similarly filled. The horizontal course above it held forty centimeters of solid stone, carrying the load of the ceiling arch without any iron lintel, wooden frame, or keystone break.

I took off my right glove and placed my bare hand flat against the face of the block. The flat scars across my palms from old cable work rasped faintly against the rough stone grain. The surface was cold, wet, and solid. I applied fifty kilograms of forward pressure with my shoulder. The block did not move. I ran my fingertips down the vertical seams on both sides. The mortar was continuous. There was no mechanical gap, no hidden hinge pin, no drop-latch, and no draft of air passing through the joint.

"That is a load-bearing wall block," I said. "There is no frame, no seam, and no door."

Lloyd kept his arm extended. His finger remained five centimeters from the stone. He did not look at me. He kept his eyes fixed on the blank grey block as if he were looking into an open room.

Maria stepped forward. She moved into the space between Lloyd and me, her shoulder brushing against my left sleeve. She stopped twenty centimeters from the wall block. She looked down the corridor toward the eighteen-meter mark where the bulkhead blocked the passage. She looked to her own left side, turning her head forty-five degrees to examine the empty space next to her shoulder. Then she looked directly at the basalt block where Lloyd’s finger pointed.

She reached out with her right hand. She did not touch the stone. She held her hand five centimeters above Lloyd’s pointing finger, her palm facing the rock.

"The woman is at the end of the hall," Maria said. Her voice was quiet, steady, and unhurried. "The second Maria is standing beside me. And the door is right here."

She turned her head to look at me. Her expression was neutral. She did not look at the stone again.

"If Lloyd sees it, and I say it is real, then it is real for us," Maria said. "That is the rule."

I stood still for two seconds. I looked at Maria’s face, then at Lloyd’s finger, then at the solid basalt wall. The mortar in the seams was still whole. The granite overhead showed no transfer of weight to an inner frame. By every metric of shoring, structural masonry, and physical survey, the corridor ended eighteen meters ahead in solid rock and held no openings on the right side.

"Understood," I said.

The moment Maria spoke the words, Lloyd stepped forward. He did not hesitate, adjust his stance, or brace his feet. He reached out with his physical right hand—the crooked arm—and placed his palm against the flat surface of the basalt block where my scarred hand had rested two seconds before.

He did not push with force. He simply moved his arm forward as if he were swinging a light wooden panel on lubricated iron hinges.

The solid basalt stone moved. A rectangular section of the wall, two meters high and eighty centimeters wide, swung inward into the space beyond. The motion made no sound. No stone ground against stone, no mortar flaked from the joints, and no dust fell from the top course. The mortar seams did not break; they simply ceased to be contiguous, separating along clean vertical lines that had not existed when I ran my fingers down them.

Behind the opened stone was a dark opening. The air that came out of it was six degrees warmer than the corridor air and smelled of dry timber and old lime.

Lloyd did not step through immediately. He remained standing on the threshold, his right hand resting on the edge of the opened stone slab.

I looked down at the floor beneath his boots. The basalt flagstones were unbroken, running continuously from the corridor into the dark space beyond. There was no step, no threshold bar, and no iron fitting.

I decided to test the rule.

I looked at a point on the floor ten centimeters in front of Lloyd’s left boot, where the stone was completely bare, grey, and wet with a thin film of water from the drain channel.

"There is a small brass lever on the floor right there," I said. I pointed my gloved finger directly at the bare stone. "It is three inches long, mounted on a iron base plate."

Lloyd stopped. He did not step into the opening. His head dropped three degrees as he looked down at the floor where I was pointing. His jaw set hard. He remained motionless for four seconds, his eyes fixed on the empty flagstone.

Then Lloyd turned his head forty-five degrees to his left. He did not look at me. He looked past my shoulder at Maria.

"Maria," Lloyd said. His voice was short. "Is there a lever?"

Maria stepped up beside Lloyd. She looked down at the floor where my finger pointed. She looked at the bare wet stone, the thin sheet of water moving toward the drain, and the lack of any metal fitting, iron plate, or raised brass.

"There is no lever on the floor, Lloyd," Maria said. Her tone was direct and warm. "The floor is clear stone."

Lloyd looked back down at the floor for one second. Then he lifted his left boot, stepped directly over the spot I had pointed out, and walked through the opened wall into the dark room beyond. He did not look back at me. He did not ask why I had spoken. He ignored my statement completely, as if I had made no sound at all.

I lowered my hand and pulled my glove back on, smoothing the wool over my knuckles.

I watched the back of Lloyd’s coat as he moved six paces into the dark room. He swung the unlit oil lantern in his left hand, the metal frame clicking faintly against his belt buckle. He moved with total certainty, taking long, even steps through a space that had been solid rock thirty seconds earlier.

He had not questioned Maria’s verdict for a single second. He had seen whatever his Insight showed him—the woman at the dead end, the duplicate Maria, the doorway in the masonry—but he had required her confirmation before he would treat any of it as a physical fact. When I offered him a physical detail that contradicted the room, he had not trusted his own eyes or mine. He had waited for Maria to clear the space.

Maria walked past me into the opening. She stepped carefully over the threshold, her boots making a dry crunch on the floor inside. The light sphere above her shoulder followed her, drifting through the stone opening and casting light across the interior of the brand-new space.

I stayed on the corridor side of the threshold for three seconds. I looked at the edge of the opened stone wall. The slab was thirty centimeters thick, composed of three solid courses of basalt ashlar that had split cleanly without leaving a rough edge or a trace of rubble. The surface of the cut was as smooth as honed slate.

I reached out and touched the edge of the open door with my gloved thumb. The stone was cold and physical. It held my weight when I pressed against it.

Maria was Lloyd’s interface. He lived in a world where walls were doors, empty corridors contained people, and single individuals existed in two places at once. He could not navigate that world alone without colliding with real stone or falling down real shafts. He needed a filter—someone who could look at the mess of his perception, weigh it against the world, and give him a single, definitive verdict on what he was allowed to touch, step on, or open.

Maria provided that verdict. She did it without anger, without hesitation, and without asking him to explain what he saw. She simply looked where he pointed, made her decision, and spoke it into existence for him. Once she declared a detail real, Lloyd acted upon it with absolute physical commitment. Once she declared it false, it ceased to exist for him, even if I stood right next to him pointing a finger at it.

My own eyes saw none of it. I did not see the woman at the corridor’s end. I did not see the second Maria. I did not see the door until Lloyd pushed it open, and I did not see the lever because I had lied about its existence.

My sight was plain, dull, and entirely uncorrupted by Insight or Arcana. I saw thirty-centimeter ashlar blocks, four-millimeter mortar seams, five-degree misalignments in bone, and three-centimeter water channels. My eyes registered the physical baseline—the real, load-bearing framework of stone, timber, and iron that remained when all the visions were stripped away.

I was the control group for the three of us. Lloyd provided the breaches in reality, Maria provided the rules for crossing them, and I provided the actual dimensions of the tunnel. If the shoring failed, if the roof sagged, or if a real iron bolt needed to be unthreaded with a wrench, my plain perception was the only thing in the passage that could be relied upon to measure the span correctly.

I felt a quiet, flat gratitude for being the dull one. It was economical. It required no interpretation, no second-guessing, and no constant triage of what was real and what was delusion. The wall was a wall until it was opened. The floor was stone until it dropped out. My lead-weight left ear heard nothing from the void, and my eyes recorded only the cold, hard spall of the workings. It kept my hands steady on the cable work and my feet planted on the footings.

"Edie," Maria called from inside the room. Her voice bounced off smooth plaster walls. "The floor is dry here. The passage continues twenty meters to a stair."

"Coming," I said.

I stepped over the threshold into the new room. As my rear foot cleared the stone jamb, I turned to look back down the main corridor we had just left.

The sphere of Arcana light floating over Maria’s shoulder threw its yellow glare back through the open door, illuminating the long, straight passage behind us. Eighteen meters down, at the end of the hall, the solid granite bulkhead stood bare and grey in the dim light. The three loose rocks sat untouched in the silt by the water drain.

The faint glow at the tunnel’s mouth swallows the darkness behind us.
