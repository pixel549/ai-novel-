We arrive at the cracked stone steps that lead down to Hessel Bight’s flooded cellar. The stone is green with damp and worn in the middle where many boots have trodden. Edie takes the first step down, her boots ringing thin against the slate. Maria follows behind her, holding her skirt clear of the slime. I bring up the rear, watching the space behind us where the alley narrows between two brick walls. The air at the bottom smells of stagnant river silt and old lime. The cellar door sighs open, exposing eleven pale bodies bobbing in stagnant water. They float face down and face up among floating crates and loose timber, wedged between the foundation pillars. Their skin is swollen and white like tallow. The water laps against the lowest brick courses with a dull sucking sound. I wade through the chill, pull the rusted latch on a concealed hatch, and lift it ajar. The water rises to my thighs, cold enough to bite through wool and skin. My boots slip on the mud below the surface. I catch the edge of the wood with my hand and heave upward until the rusted iron hinge gives way with a sharp shriek. 

The landlord slides a sack of stale bread and a coarse blanket to us, saying we may spend the night upstairs. He stands at the top of the wooden stairwell that leads out of the cellar pit, holding a tallow candle that gutters in the draft. He does not come down into the damp. He drops the burlap sack on the second step and pushes it with his boot until it teeters. Edie reaches up and takes it before it falls into the water. The blanket is grey wool, stiff with old grease. 

A weary woman recites the going rates for odd jobs—coal carrying, shutter mending—each payment barely enough for a meal. She sits on an upturned wash-tub near the back hearth upstairs, her hands clasped in her apron. Her voice is flat and dry, without any lift at the ends of the sentences. She names the copper weight for shifting a ton of slack, then the smaller coin for hanging pine shutters on a burnt frame. She does not look at our faces while she speaks. She looks at the floorboards between her boots where a knot has rotted out. 

Edie scans the rafters for loose beams, but my right arm, half-ethereal, brushes a hidden iron rod that she does not notice. Edie stands with her chin lifted, her left ear turned slightly toward the ceiling timbers to catch any groan of the wood. Her hands are flat at her sides, scarred across the palms from cable work. My right hand passes directly through a vertical pine support, my fingers closing on empty air before my knuckles strike something cold and hard that has no business being inside a dry-rot beam. Edie does not stop her survey. She moves three paces to the left and tests the next tie-beam with the toe of her boot. 

I grip the cold rod, yank it free, and feel its weight despite Edie’s unchanged assessment of the space. The iron comes out of the solid wood without splintering the grain, sliding through the timber as if the pine were smoke. It is three feet long, square-sectioned, and pitted with red rust at the ends. My physical fingers hold it by the middle while my second arm rests through it, feeling the same cold density without any resistance from the room. Edie walks past me toward the door frame, writing a figure in her grease-stained oilcloth ledger with a short stub of pencil. She does not look at the iron rod in my hand. 

Maria kneels beside a boy whose hand is crushed under a fallen beam, places her palm on it, and the flesh seals together. The boy is ten, perhaps eleven, lying on a pallet of damp straw near the cold hearth. His right hand is pinned beneath a piece of charred joist where the plaster has fallen away. The small bones of his knuckles are flat and grey. Maria puts her bare hand over the mangled skin without hesitation. Her fingers glow with a pale, steady yellow light that does not flicker. The wood does not move. The skin knits together stitch by stitch, the grey edges turning pink and then sealing smooth while the boy gasps and tries to pull his arm back. 

The boy shouts “Witch!” and Maria bows slightly, allowing the label to settle. He scrambles backward across the straw until his shoulders hit the brickwork, holding his healed hand against his chest and staring at her with wide eyes. Maria does not smile. She takes her hand back, wipes her palm on her skirt where a smudge of soot has left a mark, and stands up without assistance. Edie does not look up from her ledger. 

The night settles over the burnt manse, water still lapping against the cellar walls.
