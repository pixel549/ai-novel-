We arrive at the cracked stone steps that lead down to Hessel Bight’s flooded cellar. The stone is wet with river seepage and green moss. Maria steps down first, her boots sliding slightly on the slime before she catches her balance against the damp brick wall. Edie follows behind her, carrying the canvas sack with our gear over her right shoulder. I take up the rear, watching the dark alley behind us clear of the iron-shod guard and his mechanical dog before descending the three slippery grades.

The cellar door sighs open, exposing eleven pale bodies bobbing in stagnant water. They float face-down and face-up in the grey brine that fills the lower room, bumping against the foundation posts with a rhythmic, hollow sound like timber hitting a pier. The water is murky, thick with grease and silt from the street drains above. Maria stops at the threshold, her hand coming up to press against the wool of her shawl. Edie does not pause; she steps past the doorframe onto the high stone ledge that runs the perimeter of the room, her boots crunching on dried salt deposits.

I wade through the chill, pull the rusted latch on a concealed hatch, and lift it ajar. The water rises above my knees, cold enough to numb the skin on my shins and soak through the wool of my trousers. The smell of stagnant bilge and rot hits the back of my throat. I push my right hand down into the murky water, letting the fingers pass through the rusted iron of the hatch frame until I find the ring bolt. The dead bobbing near my thighs brush against my hip, cold and waterlogged, their swollen flesh pressing past my coat without catching. I pull upward with the physical hand while the second arm reaches through the wood of the floorboards, finding the secondary toggle underneath. The latch gives with a wet snap.

The landlord slides a sack of stale bread and a coarse blanket to us, saying we may spend the night upstairs. He stands at the top of the interior cellar stairs, his hands wiped clean on a greasy apron, looking down at us with flat, tired eyes. He does not come down the steps. He shoves the canvas sack across the warped pine boards with his boot heel and kicks the grey wool blanket after it. Edie reaches down from the ledge and picks up the sack. She does not thank him. The landlord closes the heavy oak door above us, dropping the iron drop-bar into its slot with a dry clatter.

A weary woman recites the going rates for odd jobs—coal carrying, shutter mending—each payment barely enough for a meal. She sits on an upturned apple crate by the small iron stove in the corner of the upper room where the landlord has allowed us to sleep after we climbed out of the cellar. Her hands are raw at the knuckles, scrubbed red with lye soap that hasn't cleared the black coal dust from her skin. She names the prices without looking up from her lap. Two pence for a ton of slack shifted from the barge to the bin. Four pence for hanging a pair of oak shutters on the lower wharf. She tells us the price of flour has gone up three farthings since Tuesday.

Edie scans the rafters for loose beams, but my right arm, half-ethereal, brushes a hidden iron rod that she does not notice. She stands near the center of the room, her head tilted up, her good ear turned toward the timber ceiling where the dry rot has started to gray the pine. She looks for deflection in the joints, her eyes measuring the load-bearing span above the stove. While she looks at the wood, my right hand drifts outward through the lath and plaster of the partition wall. My fingers meet nothing for six inches, then close around a cold, cylindrical piece of forged iron buried vertically inside the stud bay. Edie continues her sweep, her gaze dropping to the floor joists without once looking at the wall where my arm is sunk up to the elbow.

I grip the cold rod, yank it free, and feel its weight despite Edie’s unchanged assessment of the space. The iron comes out of the wall without cracking the plaster, sliding through the lath as if the lath were smoke. It is three feet long, scored with old lathe marks, greasy with old machine oil. I hold it in my physical hand, its weight pulling down my shoulder. Edie turns back from her survey, looks at the floorboards near her left boot, and says the joists are good for another winter if the rain stays out of the eaves. She does not see the iron rod in my hand. I drop it behind the woodbox where the shadow is deep.

Maria kneels beside a boy whose hand is crushed under a fallen beam, places her palm on it, and the flesh seals together. We found him near the back of the alley where a carriage had backed into a timber stack. The pine timber is six inches square and stained with old blood. The boy is twelve, his face grey with shock, his teeth clenched hard enough to draw a thin line of red down his chin. His right hand is flat against the dirt, the metacarpals smashed sideways under the weight of the spruce. Maria drops to her knees in the mud, her shawl slipping back from her shoulders. She places her bare palm directly over the broken, leaking skin of the boy's knuckles. Her eyes stay open, fixed on the dirt between her knees. A thin wisp of grey steam rises from the gap between her palm and the crushed bone. The grey edges of the torn flesh knit together, closing over the white shards of the joint with a wet, quiet sound.

The boy shouts “Witch!” and Maria bows slightly, allowing the label to settle. He jerks his hand back from the dirt, staring at the skin where the blood has dried into dark scales, and scrambles backward on his heels until his spine hits the brick wall of the bakery. He points a shaking finger at her face. Maria does not look up. She shifts her weight back onto her heels, wipes her palm clean against the side of her skirt, and gives a short, dipping nod of her head that accepts the word without argument. Edie stands a few feet away, her hands flat against the thighs of her trousers, watching the alley entrance for the iron dog or the guard with the three shadows.

The night settles over the burnt manse, water still lapping against the cellar walls.
